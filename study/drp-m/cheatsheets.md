## 0.I Applicable laws and the IAA framework

- **Which laws govern an arbitration?** Rules, lex arbitri (seat law), law of the contract, law of the arbitration agreement, law for non-contract claims, capacity law → [0-1](#drp-m-0-1)
- **IAA or AA?**
  - IAA if "international" (s 5(2): a party's place of business outside Singapore; seat, place of performance or closest connection outside the parties' State; or agreed to relate to more than one country), or the parties agree in writing (s 5(1)) → [0-2](#drp-m-0-2)
  - Otherwise, Singapore-seated → the AA (see 0.II) → [0-13](#drp-m-0-13)
- **Model Law in Singapore?** Force of law except Chapter VIII (Arts 35–36, enforcement); the 2006 amendments are *not* enacted → [0-3](#drp-m-0-3)
- **Is the dispute "commercial"?** Read widely, contractual or not (Art 1(1) footnote) → [0-4](#drp-m-0-4)
- **Institutional or ad hoc? UNCITRAL Rules v Model Law?** Model Law = legislation; UNCITRAL Rules = contractual procedural rules for ad hoc cases → [0-5](#drp-m-0-5)
- **Do the SIAC Rules or the IAA prevail?**
  - Choosing rules does not by itself exclude the Model Law (s 15(2)); rules apply unless inconsistent with a non-derogable provision (s 15A(1)); gaps or default provisions are not inconsistency (s 15A(3)–(5)) → [0-6](#drp-m-0-6)
  - Soft law binds only if adopted → [0-18](#drp-m-0-18)
- **Rules amended between contract and arbitration?** Presumption: rules in force at commencement (*Black & Veatch*; SIAC25 r 1.5) → [0-7](#drp-m-0-7)
- **Seat v venue; hearings elsewhere; virtual hearings?** Seat is juridical and changes only by agreement; hearings may be anywhere; SIAC can administer any seat → [0-8](#drp-m-0-8)
- **NYC reciprocity?** Singapore enforces under the NYC only awards made in another Contracting State → [0-9](#drp-m-0-9)
- **Law of the substance?** Parties' choice, no connection needed (Art 28(1)); no choice: conflict rules (Art 28(2)) v SIAC r 35.1 "appropriate" law (voie directe) → [0-10](#drp-m-0-10)
- **Law of the arbitration agreement (silent clause)?** Presumed = law of the main contract, unless that would negate the clear intention to arbitrate (*Anupam Mittal*); no choice → closest connection, usually the seat (*BNA v BNB*) → [0-11](#drp-m-0-11)
- **Capacity?** Law of nationality or incorporation; ground to set aside or refuse enforcement → [0-12](#drp-m-0-12)

### drp-m-0-1 — The laws that apply in an arbitration
*Keywords: applicable laws, arbitration rules, lex arbitri, law of the seat, law of the substance, law of the arbitration agreement, non-contractual claims, capacity*

**End point:** Up to six laws can apply in one arbitration:
- the arbitration rules;
- the law of the seat (lex arbitri);
- the law governing the substance of the dispute;
- the law governing the arbitration agreement;
- the law applicable to non-contractual claims;
- the law governing each party's capacity.
The seat supplies the procedural law. A Singapore seat means the IAA (international) or the AA (domestic).
- **The process in outline (background, ARB-SN para 1):** arbitration agreement → notice of arbitration and response (0.XIV) → constitution of the tribunal (0.X) → jurisdiction objections (0.VIII–0.IX) → written submissions, document production, witness and expert evidence, hearing (0.XIII) → award (0.XVII) → setting aside at the seat (0.XVIII) or resisting enforcement (0.XIX).

*Source note: ARB-SN p3 paras 1–2 (para 1 only links to an overview), p6 paras 13–14*

### drp-m-0-2 — When the IAA applies: "international" arbitration or opt-in
*Keywords: International Arbitration Act, IAA s 5, international arbitration, place of business, place of arbitration, substantial part of obligations, most closely connected, more than one country, opt in, s 5(1), s 5(2), s 5(4)*

**End point:** Part 2 of the IAA and the Model Law apply to an international arbitration, or to a non-international one if the parties agree in writing (s 5(1)). Where Part 2 applies, the AA does not (s 5(4)).

> **IAA s 5(2): an arbitration is international if**
> - (a) at least one party, when the agreement was concluded, has its place of business in any State other than Singapore; or
> - (b) one of these is outside the State where the parties have their places of business:
>   - (i) the place of arbitration, if determined in or pursuant to the agreement;
>   - (ii) any place where a substantial part of the obligations of the commercial relationship is to be performed, or the place with which the subject matter is most closely connected; or
> - (c) the parties have expressly agreed that the subject matter relates to more than one country.
> - s 5(3): more than one place of business → the one with the closest relationship to the agreement; none → habitual residence.

*Source note: IAA s 5(1)–(4); ARB-SN p3–4 para 4; SampleQs p56 Q1 (key p122: IAA). Conflict: NIC-ARB gives the "most closely connected" limb as s 5(2)(b)(iii); the IAA puts it in s 5(2)(b)(ii) with the place of performance, as ARB-SN does.*

### drp-m-0-3 — Model Law has the force of law, except Chapter VIII; 2006 amendments not enacted
*Keywords: Model Law, UNCITRAL Model Law, force of law, IAA s 3(1), Chapter VIII excluded, Article 35, Article 36, 2006 amendments not enacted, First Media, travaux, s 4*

**End point:** The 1985 Model Law, except Chapter VIII (Arts 35–36 on recognition and enforcement), has the force of law in Singapore (IAA s 3(1)). Chapter VIII was left out so that foreign awards are enforced under one regime, the NYC, which rests on reciprocity (*PT First Media v Astro* [2013] SGCA 57 at [86]). Singapore has not enacted the 2006 amendments (Art 7 options, Arts 17–17J interim measures). Model Law travaux may be used to interpret it (s 4).
- **Background (non-core):** the IAA was first drafted by a sub-committee of the Singapore Academy of Law's Law Reform Committee, which recommended excluding Arts 35–36 to avoid conflict with the reciprocity-based NYC (IAA Hansard col 627; *First Media* at [86]). The exclusion left a gap for Singapore-seated international awards, now filled by IAA s 19 (0-154). UNCITRAL is the UN body that harmonises international trade law; States may enact the Model Law wholesale or in part, and the IAA enacts it with additions and modifications. Other Model Law jurisdictions include Germany, Hong Kong, New Zealand, Australia and Canada; their case law (collected in the UNCITRAL 2012 Digest of Case Law on the Model Law) and the travaux may be used to interpret it.

*Source note: IAA ss 3(1), 4; ARB-SN p3–5 paras 5–7 (fns 1–3); [2013] SGCA 57 at [86]; ARB-Plan reading list (UNCITRAL 2012 Digest); PYP23 p54 Q1*

### drp-m-0-4 — "Commercial" is read widely
*Keywords: commercial, Model Law Art 1(1), footnote, contractual or not, supply of goods, construction, joint venture*

**End point:** The Model Law applies to international *commercial* arbitration (Art 1(1)). "Commercial" is read widely to cover all relationships of a commercial nature, contractual or not. Examples are supply of goods or services, construction, banking, financing, carriage, joint ventures and investment.

*Source note: MAL Art 1(1) and footnote; ARB-SN p5 para 8, p17 para 54*

### drp-m-0-5 — Institutional v ad hoc arbitration; UNCITRAL Rules v Model Law
*Keywords: institutional arbitration, ad hoc arbitration, SIAC, ICC, HKIAC, LCIA, model clause, UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, Model Law distinguished*

**End point:** Institutional arbitration is administered by an institution (SIAC, ICC, HKIAC, LCIA), usually under its rules. Ad hoc arbitration has no administering institution and often adopts the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules. Do not confuse the two UNCITRAL texts:
- the Model Law is a legislative text enacted by States;
- the UNCITRAL Rules are procedural rules parties adopt by contract.
Many jurisdictional problems come from altering an institution's model clause.
- **Ad hoc not recognised everywhere:** e.g. the PRC Arbitration Law (Arts 16, 18) does not recognise ad hoc arbitration (ARB-SN p5 fn 4), a reason to name an institution in a China-related clause.
- **UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules 2021 (ad hoc), key defaults:** proceedings start when the respondent receives the notice of arbitration (Art 3(2)); the notice must contain a demand for arbitration, the parties' details, the arbitration agreement and contract invoked, a brief description of the claim and amount, the relief sought, and proposals on number of arbitrators, language and place (Art 3(3)). No agreed appointing authority within 30 days of a proposal → any party may ask the PCA Secretary-General to designate one (Art 6(2)). No agreement on number within 30 days of the notice → three arbitrators (Art 7(1)).
- **Commencement documents compared:** SIAC Notice of Arbitration (SIAC25 r 6, 0-107); ICC Request for Arbitration (ICC Rules 2021 Art 4(3): parties and representatives, nature and circumstances of the dispute, relief and amounts, relevant agreements and the arbitration agreement, particulars on the number and choice of arbitrators, and observations on seat, applicable law and language, with the filing fee). A **Procedural Order No 1**, issued after the first case management conference, typically fixes the timetable, written submissions, document production (often adopting the IBA Rules as guidance), witness and expert evidence, hearing logistics and communications (ARB-CS1 p3 points candidates to an actual PO1).
- **No rules chosen:** parties may also write their own procedural rules. If they agree no rules at all, the law of the seat governs the procedure (for a Singapore seat, Model Law Arts 21–27 and the IAA), subject to the tribunal's Art 19(2) discretion (0-95).

*Source note: ARB-SN p5–6 paras 9–12 and fn 4, p8 para 21; UNCITRAL-AR Arts 3, 6–7; MAL Arts 19, 21–27; ARB-CS1 p3 Q3. The ICC Rules 2021 are not in `authorities/` and are not Course Material: Art 4(3) is stated from the published ICC text, and the PO1 contents are typical practice, not a rule.*

### drp-m-0-6 — Institutional rules v the lex arbitri: which prevails (IAA ss 15, 15A)
*Keywords: rules v lex arbitri, IAA s 15(2), s 15A, inconsistent, non-derogable, mandatory provision, unless otherwise agreed, Insigma, John Holland overruled, Dermajaya overruled, SIAC r 1.6*

**End point:** A clause adopting institutional rules does not by itself exclude the Model Law or Part 2 of the IAA (s 15(2)). The rules apply unless inconsistent with a Model Law or IAA provision from which the parties cannot derogate (s 15A(1)). So the chosen rules prevail over the Model Law except against a mandatory provision. Non-mandatory provisions are usually those beginning "unless otherwise agreed by the parties".
- **Cases overruled by statute (ARB-SN para 16):** *John Holland v Toyo Engineering* [2001] SGHC 48 had treated adopting institutional (ICC) rules as an agreement excluding the Model Law; s 15(2) reverses that. *Dermajaya Properties v Premium Properties* [2002] SGHC 53 had let the Model Law prevail over the parties' inconsistent chosen rules; s 15A(1) reverses that, so the rules prevail unless they clash with a non-derogable provision.
- **Comparison (background):** England's Arbitration Act lists its mandatory provisions expressly (ARB-SN para 20). The Model Law does not, hence the "unless otherwise agreed" test.

> **IAA s 15A: when rules are not "inconsistent"
> - (1) Rules apply to the extent not inconsistent with a non-derogable provision of the Model Law or Part 2.
> - (3) A rule is not inconsistent merely because it covers a matter on which the Model Law and IAA are silent.
> - (4) Rules are not inconsistent merely because they are silent on a matter the Model Law or IAA covers.
> - (5) A rule is not inconsistent merely because it covers a matter on which the Model Law or IAA lets the parties agree but applies a default absent agreement.
> - (6) Parties may make such arrangements by adopting rules or providing other means.
> - SIAC25 r 1.6 says the same from the rules' side: a non-derogable provision of the applicable law prevails.

*Source note: IAA ss 15(2), 15A; ARB-SN p7 paras 16–20 (para 16 names John Holland and Dermajaya only as overruled; the holdings stated are the propositions ss 15(2) and 15A(1) reverse); Insigma Technology v Alstom Technology [2009] SGCA 24 at [41]–[43]; SIAC25 r 1.6; ARB-CS1 p2 Q(1); PYP23 p3 Q10. Conflict: ARB-SN para 17 cites "s 15(4)" for the silence rule and para 18 attributes the "fullest effect possible" words to Insigma at [41]. The rule is s 15A(4), and the words are the Minister's speech quoted at [42], so the block cites s 15A(3)–(5) and [41]–[43].*

### drp-m-0-7 — Which version of the arbitration rules applies
*Keywords: version of rules, rules in force at commencement, Black & Veatch, SIAC Rules 2025, r 1.5, for the time being in force*

**End point:** Where rules change between the contract and the arbitration, there is a presumption that the rules in force when the arbitration is commenced apply, not those at the date of contract (*Black & Veatch Singapore v Jurong Engineering* [2004] SGCA 30). SIAC25 r 1.5 says the same: it applies to arbitrations commenced on or after 1 Jan 2025 unless otherwise agreed.

*Source note: ARB-SN p8 para 22; [2004] SGCA 30 at [19]–[20]; SIAC25 r 1.5*

### drp-m-0-8 — Seat v venue; seat is not fixed by the institution; choosing a seat
*Keywords: seat, venue, place of arbitration, juridical seat, hearing location, virtual hearing, PT Garuda, change of seat, SIAC administering Jakarta seat, Art 20, choice of seat factors*

**End point:**
- Parties may agree the seat (Art 20(1)). It can then change only by agreement of all parties.
- The seat is a juridical concept; the venue of hearings is logistical. Holding hearings elsewhere, or online, does not move the seat (*PT Garuda Indonesia v Birgen Air* [2002] SGCA 12). The tribunal may meet anywhere it thinks convenient.
- The institution does not fix the seat: SIAC can administer an arbitration seated in Jakarta or Kuala Lumpur.
- Failing agreement, rules may set the seat. Under SIAC25 r 36.1 the tribunal determines it.

> **Choosing a seat (ARB-SN para 25)**
> - The seat should be a New York Convention State (reciprocity reservations).
> - The level of court supervision and support.
> - The quality of the judiciary.
> - Neutrality and political stability.

*Source note: MAL Art 20; ARB-SN p6 para 13, p8 paras 23–25; [2002] SGCA 12 (eLitigation text unnumbered; ARB-SN cites [24]); SIAC25 r 36.1; PYP23 p5 Q12, p58 Q5*

### drp-m-0-9 — New York Convention and Singapore's reciprocity reservation
*Keywords: New York Convention, NYC, reciprocity reservation, Contracting State, Art I(3), foreign award, Singapore reservation, 170 States*

**End point:** The NYC (over 160 Contracting States) governs recognition and enforcement of foreign awards. It is part of Singapore law through IAA Part 3. Singapore made the reciprocity reservation: it applies the NYC only to awards made in another Contracting State (Art I(3)). This is why the seat should be in a NYC State.

*Source note: NYC Art I(3); IAA s 27(1) ("foreign award", "Convention country"); ARB-SN p9 para 26, p79–80 para 250*

### drp-m-0-10 — Law governing the substance of the dispute (Art 28; SIAC r 35.1)
*Keywords: governing law, substantive law, Art 28(1), Art 28(2), conflict of laws rules, voie directe, voie indirecte, SIAC r 35.1, Quarella, no connection required, amiable compositeur, ex aequo et bono*

**End point:** The parties may choose any law for the substance, with no need for a connection to them or the dispute (Art 28(1); *Quarella SpA v Scelta Marble* [2012] SGHC 166 at [34]). Absent choice, the Model Law requires the tribunal to apply the law found by the conflict rules it considers appropriate (Art 28(2), voie indirecte). SIAC25 r 35.1 lets the tribunal apply the law or rules of law it determines appropriate (voie directe). Many tribunals still run a conflicts analysis, which r 35.1 permits. Amiable compositeur or ex aequo et bono needs express authority (r 35.3).
- **Review of the tribunal's reading of a choice-of-law clause:** not normally reviewable by the court, unless the tribunal deliberately disregarded the parties' choice of law (*Quarella*). A mere error in applying the chosen law is an error of law, which is final (0-131).

> **Art 28(2): conflict-of-laws rules a tribunal may treat as "appropriate" (ARB-SN para 28(b); the Model Law prescribes none)**
> - (i) the conflict rules of the seat;
> - (ii) those of the place where the award is likely to be enforced;
> - (iii) those of the jurisdiction that would have been competent but for the arbitration;
> - (iv) those of the place of contractual performance;
> - (v) those of a jurisdiction with some element common to the parties;
> - (vi) general principles of private international law;
> - (vii) the law with the closest connection to the dispute.

*Source note: MAL Art 28; ARB-SN p9–10 paras 27–30; [2012] SGHC 166 at [34] (the "deliberately disregarded" point is from ARB-SN para 27; no pinpoint found); SIAC25 r 35.1–35.3. ARB-SN para 28(b) numbers the last item "(v)" again; it is the seventh.*

### drp-m-0-11 — Law governing the arbitration agreement (BNA v BNB; Anupam Mittal)
*Keywords: proper law of the arbitration agreement, implied choice, law of the main contract, law of the seat, closest and most real connection, BNA v BNB, Anupam Mittal, null and void, validity, non-signatory, England Arbitration Act 2025 s 6A*

**End point:**
- What it governs: the law of the arbitration agreement governs the agreement's formation, validity (including formal validity), effect and discharge (*BNA v BNB* [2019] SGCA 84 at [55]). The arbitrator's authority flows from it (*Dallal v Bank Mellat* [1986] QB 441). It also decides who the parties are (so it, not the main contract's law, decides whether a non-signatory parent is bound), the clause's scope, the agreed qualifications or constitution of the tribunal, and whether the agreement is "null and void" for a stay.
- Parties may choose it: this is implicit in Art 34(2)(a)(i) MAL and Art V(1)(a) NYC, which test validity "under the law to which the parties have subjected it". Traditionally few clauses did; newer model clauses do.
- No express choice: there is a rebuttable presumption that the law of the main contract applies. The presumption is displaced if that law would negate a clear intention to arbitrate.
- *BNA*: the argument that PRC law would invalidate a SIAC clause failed. PRC law governed, Shanghai was the seat, and parties must live with an unworkable clause ([90], [103]–[104]).
- *Anupam Mittal v Westbridge* [2023] SGCA 1: Indian law was displaced in favour of Singapore law (the seat), because Indian law would have made the dispute non-arbitrable ([74]). ARB-SN para 37 puts the reason as the clause expressly covering disputes on "the management of the company", which showed the parties' clear intention to arbitrate them. Use both: the express words showed the intention, and applying Indian law would have negated it.
- No express or implied choice: the law with the closest and most real connection applies, generally the seat.
- England (Arbitration Act 2025 s 6A, from 1 Aug 2025) now applies the law of the seat by default; Malaysia has done the same (Arbitration (Amendment) Act 2024 s 9A). Singapore consulted but has not legislated, so state the law expressly.
- Background: English cases have read an express choice of law for the "Agreement" (a defined term) as covering the arbitration clause too. Before *BNA*, English and Singapore cases conflicted between the main-contract law and the seat law (ARB-SN para 34).

*Source note: ARB-SN p10–12 paras 31–39 (Dallal is an English case taken from ARB-SN para 31, not checked); [2019] SGCA 84 at [55], [90], [103]–[104]; [2023] SGCA 1 at [74]; PYP23 p19 Q26, p20 Q27. Conflict: ARB-SN para 36 cites [103] for the parties' unawareness of PRC law; the judgment says it at [90].*

### drp-m-0-12 — Law governing a party's capacity
*Keywords: capacity, incapacity, lex personae, place of incorporation, Art 34(2)(a)(i), Art V(1)(a), s 31(2)(a)*

**End point:** A party's capacity is generally governed by its personal law: nationality for individuals, place of incorporation or business for companies. Alternatively it may be determined by the law of the contract. Incapacity can be raised before or during the arbitration. It is a ground to set aside (Art 34(2)(a)(i)) or refuse enforcement (IAA s 31(2)(a); NYC Art V(1)(a)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p13 paras 40–42; IAA s 31(2)(a)*

## 0.II Domestic arbitration under the Arbitration Act 2001

- **When does the AA apply?** Seat in Singapore and IAA Part 2 does not apply (s 3 AA); parties can opt in to the IAA in writing (IAA s 5(1)) or expressly out of it (IAA s 15(1)) → [0-13](#drp-m-0-13)
- **Court case filed despite a domestic clause: must the court stay?** No: under the AA the court *may* stay; it can refuse for "sufficient reason" or if the applicant is not ready and willing (s 6(2) AA; *CSY v CSZ* factors). Under the IAA it *must* → [0-14](#drp-m-0-14)
- **Can a party ask the court to decide a question of law mid-arbitration?** Under the AA, yes, with all parties' agreement, or the tribunal's permission plus costs savings and no delay (s 45 AA); not under the IAA → [0-15](#drp-m-0-15)
- **Appeal on a point of law from the award?**
  - AA: appeal to the court on a question of law, by agreement or with permission (s 49 AA); within 28 days (s 50(3)) → [0-16](#drp-m-0-16)
  - Excluded by agreement; dispensing with reasons = exclusion (s 49(2)); SIAC r 51.6 waiver → [0-16](#drp-m-0-16)
  - IAA: no appeal on law or fact; set aside only (see 0.XVIII) → [0-138](#drp-m-0-138)
- **AA defaults: number of arbitrators, appointment, setting aside?** Sole arbitrator (s 12); SIAC President as appointing authority; s 48 grounds incl. fraud and natural justice; 3 months → [0-17](#drp-m-0-17)

### drp-m-0-13 — When the AA applies; opting into or out of the IAA
*Keywords: Arbitration Act 2001, AA s 3, domestic arbitration, IAA s 15(1), opt out, opt in, s 5(1) IAA, s 5(4) IAA*

**End point:** The AA applies to any arbitration seated in Singapore to which IAA Part 2 does not apply (AA s 3), i.e. a domestic arbitration. Parties to a domestic arbitration may agree in writing that the IAA applies (IAA s 5(1)). Parties to an international one may expressly agree that the Model Law or IAA Part 2 does not apply, or that the AA applies; the AA then governs (IAA s 15(1)). Merely adopting rules does not opt out (s 15(2)). Where Part 2 applies, the AA does not (s 5(4)).

*Source note: AA s 3; IAA ss 5(1), 5(4), 15(1)–(2); ARB-SN p3 para 3*

### drp-m-0-14 — Stay under the AA is discretionary (s 6(2) AA; CSY v CSZ)
*Keywords: stay, domestic arbitration, AA s 6, discretion, sufficient reason, ready and willing, CSY v CSZ, Sim Chay Koon, may stay, shall stay, IAA v AA*

**End point:** Under AA s 6(2) the court *may* stay if satisfied that there is no sufficient reason not to refer the matter to arbitration, and the applicant was and remains ready and willing to arbitrate. The IAA stay is mandatory (see 0-47). "Sufficient reason" covers a broad range of factors. They must outweigh the strong consideration that the parties chose to arbitrate and should be held to it (*CSY v CSZ* [2022] SGCA 43 at [23]–[26]; *Sim Chay Koon v NTUC Income* [2015] SGCA 46 at [7], [9]–[10]). The timing rule is the same as IAA s 6(1).

> **CSY v CSZ [2022] SGCA 43 at [26]: factors for "sufficient reason"**
> - (a) related actions and disputes, some governed by an arbitration agreement and some not;
> - (b) overlap of issues and a real prospect of inconsistent findings;
> - (c) the likely shape of the process for resolving the entire dispute;
> - (d) injustice in the same witnesses dealing with the same facts in two fora;
> - (e) disrepute to the administration of justice from inconsistent determinations;
> - (f) relative prejudice to the parties;
> - (g) possible abuse of process.

*Source note: AA s 6(1)–(2); ARB-SN p24–25 paras 80–81; [2022] SGCA 43 at [23]–[26], [39]; [2015] SGCA 46 at [7], [9]–[10]; CYR-EXAM "Contact Session details" (stay "SHALL" v "MAY"); PYP23 p60 Q7. ARB-SN para 81 also cites Fasi Paul Frank v Speciality Laboratories [1999] 1 SLR(R) 1138 at [18]; that judgment was not found on eLitigation, so the block rests on CSY.*

### drp-m-0-15 — Court determination of a preliminary point of law (s 45 AA)
*Keywords: preliminary point of law, question of law, AA s 45, refer question of law to court, substantially affects rights, substantial savings in costs, without delay, permission of the tribunal, domestic arbitration, IAA no equivalent*

**End point:** In a domestic arbitration the court may determine a question of law arising in the proceedings that substantially affects a party's rights, unless the parties agree otherwise (AA s 45(1)). The IAA has no equivalent. The application must be made with all parties' agreement, or with the tribunal's permission and the court satisfied on costs savings and delay (s 45(2)). The tribunal may continue meanwhile (s 45(4)).

> **AA s 45**
> - (1) Unless otherwise agreed, the Court may, on a party's application (with notice to the others), determine any question of law arising in the proceedings which substantially affects the rights of one or more parties.
> - (2) The Court will not consider the application unless:
>   - (a) it is made with the agreement of all parties; or
>   - (b) it is made with the tribunal's permission and the Court is satisfied that:
>     - (i) determining it is likely to produce substantial savings in costs; and
>     - (ii) it is made without delay.
> - (3) The application identifies the question and, unless agreed, the grounds.
> - (4) Unless otherwise agreed, the tribunal may continue and make an award meanwhile.
> - (5)–(7) Appeal only with the appellate court's permission, granted only for a question of general importance or another special reason.

*Source note: AA s 45; CYR-EXAM "Contact Session details" (s 45, preliminary point of law; "3 questions came out")*

### drp-m-0-16 — Appeal on a question of law and exclusion agreements (ss 49–50 AA)
*Keywords: appeal on question of law, AA s 49, permission to appeal, obviously wrong, general public importance, exclusion agreement, dispense with reasons, s 49(2), 28 days, s 50, domestic award, IAA no appeal*

**End point:** A party to a domestic arbitration may appeal to the court on a question of law arising out of an award. The appeal needs the agreement of all other parties or the court's permission (AA s 49(1), (3)). The parties may exclude the right. An agreement to dispense with reasons counts as an exclusion (s 49(2)). The appeal must be brought within 28 days of the award, after exhausting any arbitral review and the s 43 correction route (s 50(2)–(3)). There is no appeal on law or fact from an IAA award.

> **AA s 49(5): permission is given only if the Court is satisfied that**
> - (a) determining the question will substantially affect the rights of one or more parties;
> - (b) the tribunal was asked to determine the question;
> - (c) on the findings of fact in the award:
>   - (i) the tribunal's decision is obviously wrong; or
>   - (ii) the question is of general public importance and the decision is at least open to serious doubt; and
> - (d) despite the agreement to arbitrate, it is just and proper for the Court to decide the question.
> - s 49(8): the Court may confirm, vary, remit or set aside the award.

*Source note: AA ss 49–50; SampleQs p57 Q6 (key p122: D, none of the statements accurate), p59 Q10 (key p122: B, exclusion effective); CYR-EXAM "Contact Session details"*

### drp-m-0-17 — Other AA defaults: sole arbitrator, appointment, setting aside under s 48
*Keywords: AA s 12, single arbitrator, AA s 13, appointing authority, AA s 48, setting aside domestic award, fraud, natural justice, 3 months, s 4 writing*

**End point:**
- Arbitrators: absent agreement, one arbitrator (AA s 12(2)). Failing an agreed procedure, each side appoints one of three and the two agree the third, with the appointing authority acting on 30 days' default (s 13(3)–(4)).
- Setting aside: s 48 lists the Model Law-type grounds plus fraud or corruption and breach of natural justice in one section (s 48(1)(a)(vi)–(vii)). The application must be made within 3 months of receipt of the award or of disposal of a s 43 request (s 48(2)).
- Form: the arbitration agreement must be in writing (s 4(3)).

*Source note: AA ss 4(3), 12, 13(3)–(4), 48; SampleQs p56 Q2 (key p122: A, AA agreement must be in writing)*

### drp-m-0-18 — Soft law binds only if adopted or applied
*Keywords: soft law, not binding, IBA Rules, IBA Guidelines, CIArb Protocol, Prague Rules, adopted by parties, procedural order, guidance*

**End point:** Soft law instruments are not law and do not bind unless the parties agree to adopt them, often in Procedural Order No 1, or the tribunal decides to apply them under its procedural discretion. They are still widely referred to by parties, arbitrators and courts as guidance. Each instrument yields to mandatory law (IBA-EV Art 1.1; CIARB Art 2.2; PRAGUE Art 1.3).

> **How each instrument comes to apply**
> - IBA Rules of Evidence: where the parties agree or the tribunal determines to apply them (Art 1.1); the version current at the date of the agreement (Art 1.2).
> - IBA Party Representation Guidelines: where the parties agree, or the tribunal wishes to rely on them after deciding it has authority (Guideline 1).
> - CIArb Protocol: where the parties agree, or on application where the tribunal directs it to apply (Art 2.1).
> - Prague Rules: where the parties agree, or the tribunal applies them after hearing the parties (Arts 1.1–1.2).
> - IBA Conflicts Guidelines: not binding, but widely referred to.

*Source note: ARB-CS1 p2 Q(2); ARB-SN p39–40 para 136; IBA-EV Art 1; IBA-PR Guidelines 1–3; CIARB Art 2; PRAGUE Art 1*

### drp-m-0-19 — IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence (2020)
*Keywords: IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence, document production, Request to Produce, narrow and specific category, relevant and material, Art 3.3, Art 9.2, privilege, settlement negotiations, adverse inference, Art 9.6, witness interview, Art 4.3*

**End point:**
- Production: each party submits the documents it relies on (Art 3.1) and may serve a Request to Produce (Art 3.2–3.3).
- Exclusion: the tribunal decides admissibility and weight. It excludes material for lack of relevance or materiality, privilege, unreasonable burden, confidentiality and the other Art 9.2 grounds.
- Adverse inference: the tribunal may infer that a document a party fails to produce without satisfactory explanation was adverse to it (Art 9.6).
- Witnesses: it is not improper to interview witnesses (Art 4.3).

> **IBA-EV Art 3.3: a Request to Produce must contain**
> - (a) a description of each document, or of a narrow and specific category reasonably believed to exist (search terms may be required for electronic documents);
> - (b) how the documents are relevant to the case and material to its outcome;
> - (c) a statement that they are not in the requesting party's possession, custody or control (or why producing them would be unreasonably burdensome), and why it assumes the other party has them.
>
> **Art 9.2: exclusion grounds**
> - (a) insufficient relevance or materiality;
> - (b) legal impediment or privilege;
> - (c) unreasonable burden;
> - (d) loss or destruction shown with reasonable likelihood;
> - (e) compelling commercial or technical confidentiality;
> - (f) compelling political or institutional sensitivity;
> - (g) procedural economy, proportionality, fairness or equality.
> - Art 9.3: may exclude illegally obtained evidence.
> - Art 9.4: on privilege, may consider legal advice, settlement negotiations, the parties' expectations, and waiver.

> **The rest of the IBA Rules 2020, article by article (non-core: background reading)**
> - **Art 2 Consultation:** the tribunal "shall" consult the parties at the earliest appropriate time on an efficient, economical and fair evidence process. Topics may include witness statements and expert reports, oral testimony, document production, confidentiality, cybersecurity and data protection (2.2(e), new in 2020), and efficiency. It is encouraged to flag issues it sees as relevant and material, or fit for preliminary determination (2.3).
> - **Art 3.4–3.14 Documents:** produce all unobjected requested documents (3.4). Objections in writing within the time ordered, on Art 9.2/9.3 grounds or failure to meet Art 3.3 (3.5). The tribunal may invite the parties to consult (3.6), then rules: order production if relevant and material, no 9.2/9.3 objection applies, and Art 3.3 is met (3.7). In exceptional circumstances it may appoint an independent expert to review a disputed document instead of reading it itself (3.8). Non-party documents: the tribunal may take legally available steps (3.9). The tribunal may itself request documents at any time (3.10). Additional documents responding to new matters (3.11). Form (3.12): copies conform to originals; electronic documents in the producer's most convenient reasonably usable form; no duplicates; documents produced on request need not be translated, but documents submitted to the tribunal in another language must be. Confidentiality (3.13): non-public documents are kept confidential and used only for the arbitration, except where disclosure is required to fulfil a legal duty, protect a legal right, or enforce or challenge an award in bona fide court proceedings. Separate phases may have separate document timetables (3.14).
> - **Art 4 Witnesses of fact:** identify witnesses and subject matter (4.1). Anyone may be a witness, including a party or its officer or employee (4.2). Interviewing witnesses is not improper (4.3). The tribunal may order witness statements (4.4). Contents (4.5): name, address, relationship with the parties, background; a full and detailed description of the facts and the source of the information; language; affirmation of truth; signature, date and place. Revised statements only in reply to new matters or new developments (4.6). A witness called under Art 8.1 who fails without valid reason to appear: the tribunal "shall disregard" the statement unless, in exceptional circumstances, it decides otherwise (4.7). Not calling a witness is not agreement with the statement (4.8). Unwilling witnesses: the tribunal may take legally available steps (4.9), and may order a party to produce any person (4.10).
> - **Art 5 Party-appointed experts:** report contents (5.2): name and relationships with the parties, counsel and tribunal; instructions; statement of independence; facts relied on; opinions and methods; language; affirmation of genuine belief; signature; attribution if several authors. Reply reports only on new matters (5.3). The tribunal may order experts to meet and confer and record agreed and disagreed issues in writing (5.4). Non-appearance: report disregarded unless exceptional circumstances (5.5).
> - **Art 6 Tribunal-appointed experts:** appointed after consulting the parties, with terms of reference (6.1). Statement of qualifications and independence before accepting; objections after appointment only on newly learnt grounds (6.2). May request information and access, subject to 9.2/9.3 (6.3). Parties may see what the expert examined and respond (6.5), and question the expert at the hearing (6.6). Fees are costs of the arbitration (6.8).
> - **Art 7 Inspection:** the tribunal may inspect, or have an expert inspect, any site, property, goods, samples, systems or documents, at a party's request or its own motion; parties may attend.
> - **Art 8 Evidentiary hearing:** each witness appears if any party or the tribunal requests (8.1). The tribunal may order a Remote Hearing after consulting the parties, with a protocol on technology, testing, time zones, documents and preventing improper influence of witnesses (8.2). It has complete control and may limit irrelevant, duplicative or burdensome questions; direct questions may not be unreasonably leading (8.3). Usual order: claimant's witnesses, respondent's, then experts; witness conferencing allowed; the tribunal may ask questions at any time (8.4). Statements may stand as direct evidence (8.5).
> - **Art 9 Admissibility:** the tribunal decides admissibility, relevance, materiality and weight (9.1). Art 9.2 exclusion is mandatory ("shall"); 9.3 (illegally obtained) is discretionary ("may"). Confidentiality arrangements (9.5). Adverse inference for unproduced documents (9.6) and for other evidence, including testimony (9.7). A party's bad faith in taking evidence may be reflected in costs (9.8).

*Source note: IBA-EV Arts 1–9 (PDF pp8–25; printed page = PDF page − 1); ARB-CS1 p1; ARB-Plan p4 (reading list)*

### drp-m-0-20 — IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest (2024): structure
*Keywords: IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest, 2024, General Standards, Application Lists, Non-Waivable Red List, Waivable Red List, Orange List, Green List, reasonable third person, disclosure*

**End point:** The Guidelines set out General Standards and then Application Lists, each non-exhaustive:
- **Non-Waivable Red List:** no one may be their own judge, so acceptance cannot cure the conflict.
- **Waivable Red List:** serious situations, cured only if the parties, aware of the conflict, expressly agree.
- **Orange List:** situations the arbitrator must disclose. Parties are deemed to accept if they make no timely objection.
- **Green List:** no appearance of conflict and no duty to disclose.
Disclosure does not imply a conflict. Non-disclosure alone does not make an arbitrator partial; only the facts not disclosed can.

*Source note: IBA-COI Part II paras 2–7 (p14–15); ARB-SN p39–43 paras 136–140. Conflict: ARB-SN heads this "IBA Guidelines 2014" and uses 2014 item numbers; ARB-CS1 p1 asks for the latest version, so the pack uses the 2024 edition (see 0-81 to 0-84).*

### drp-m-0-21 — IBA Guidelines on Party Representation (2013)
*Keywords: IBA Guidelines on Party Representation, party representative misconduct, Guideline 26, admonish, adverse inference, costs, Guideline 27*

**End point:** If the tribunal finds, after notice and a hearing, that a party representative committed Misconduct, it may:
- admonish them;
- draw appropriate inferences;
- reflect the misconduct in costs;
- take any other measure to preserve fairness and integrity.
These remedies are in Guideline 26. The Guidelines do not displace mandatory law, professional or disciplinary rules, or agreed rules, nor the representative's duty of loyalty (Guideline 3).

> **Guideline 27: the tribunal should consider**
> - (a) the need to preserve the integrity and fairness of the proceedings and the enforceability of the award;
> - (b) the impact of a ruling on the parties' rights;
> - (c) the nature and gravity of the misconduct, including its effect on the proceedings;
> - (d) the representative's good faith;
> - (e) relevant considerations of privilege and confidentiality;
> - (f) the extent to which the party knew of, condoned, directed or took part in it.

> **Guidelines 4–25 (non-core: background reading)**
> - **Representation (4–6):** representatives identify themselves early, and changes are notified promptly (4). Once the tribunal is constituted, a person should not take on representation where a relationship with an arbitrator creates a conflict, unless no party objects after proper disclosure (5). If that happens, the tribunal may take measures, including excluding the new representative from all or part of the proceedings (6).
> - **Ex parte communications (7–8):** no ex parte communication with an arbitrator about the arbitration unless the parties agree (7). Permitted (8): with a prospective party-nominated arbitrator on expertise, availability, willingness and conflicts; with a prospective or appointed party-nominated arbitrator to select the presiding arbitrator; with a prospective presiding arbitrator only if the parties agree. A general description of the dispute is fine; never seek views on the substance.
> - **Submissions (9–11):** no knowingly false submission of fact (9). Promptly correct a false submission once known, subject to confidentiality and privilege (10). Do not submit witness or expert evidence known to be false; if it emerges, advise the client and take remedial steps: urge the witness to testify truthfully, correct or withdraw the evidence, or withdraw as representative (11).
> - **Documents (12–17):** tell the client to preserve potentially relevant documents (12). No document request or objection for an improper purpose such as harassment or delay (13). Explain the duty to produce and the consequences of not producing (14), help with a reasonable search (15). Never suppress or conceal requested or ordered documents (16). If a document should have been produced, advise producing it (17).
> - **Witnesses and experts (18–25):** identify yourself, your client and why you are asking before seeking information (18). Tell the witness they may consult their own counsel and stop the conversation (19). You may help prepare witness statements and expert reports (20), but they must reflect the witness's own account and the expert's own analysis (21–22). Do not invite false evidence (23). Meeting witnesses to prepare testimony, including practice questioning, is allowed (24). You may pay witnesses' and experts' reasonable expenses, reasonable compensation for a witness's lost time, and reasonable fees for a party-appointed expert's professional services (25).

*Source note: IBA-PR Guidelines 1–27 (PDF pp13–25; printed page = PDF page − 9)*

### drp-m-0-22 — CIArb Protocol on Party-Appointed Experts (2015)
*Keywords: CIArb Protocol, party-appointed expert, expert witness, impartial and objective, Art 4.1, duty to assist the tribunal, Art 4.3, payment does not vitiate impartiality, expert declaration, instructions disclosed*

**End point:** A party-appointed expert's opinion must be impartial and objective (Art 4.1). Being paid reasonable fees by the appointing party does not of itself vitiate impartiality (Art 4.2). The expert's duty is to assist the tribunal (Art 4.3). The written opinion must give the expert's background, any relationship with the parties or tribunal, and all instructions and the basis of remuneration (Art 4.4). It ends with the expert's declaration (Art 8). The tribunal directs the issues, the number of experts and any tests (Art 3).

> **The rest of the Protocol (non-core: background reading)**
> - Applies only to party-appointed experts, not tribunal-appointed or single joint experts. Its main difference from the IBA Rules is that experts meet before writing their reports.
> - **Art 1:** definitions (Arbitral Tribunal, Evidentiary Hearing and others). **Art 2:** applies where the parties agree or the tribunal directs; mandatory law prevails; the tribunal resolves conflicts and gaps.
> - **Art 5 Privilege:** instructions and terms of appointment are not privileged, but the tribunal will not order their disclosure, or allow questioning about them, without good cause (5.1). Drafts, working papers and other documents the expert creates are privileged and not disclosable (5.2).
> - **Art 6 Expert evidence (sequence):** experts on related issues first meet without prejudice to list the issues and tests and try to agree. They send a joint statement of agreed and disagreed issues, with reasons. Each then writes an opinion on the disagreed issues only, exchanged simultaneously, then an optional reply, also simultaneous. Each testifies at the hearing unless the parties agree otherwise and the tribunal confirms. An expert who fails to appear without valid reason: the opinion is disregarded unless there are exceptional circumstances. Agreeing that an expert need not testify is not acceptance of the opinion (6.3).
> - **Art 7 Testimony:** as the tribunal directs, to narrow the issues. The tribunal may direct further conferral or joint reports and hold preliminary meetings with the experts. An opinion not in line with the Art 8 declaration is disregarded in whole or part (7.4).

*Source note: CIARB Arts 1–8 (Protocol at PDF pp26–34, Appendix I to the CIArb Guideline; printed page = PDF page − 26); ARB-CS1 p1*

### drp-m-0-23 — Prague Rules (2018)
*Keywords: Prague Rules, inquisitorial, proactive tribunal, case management conference, avoid document production, Art 4.2, iura novit curia, Art 7, assistance in amicable settlement, Art 9, arbitrator as mediator*

**End point:** The Prague Rules take a civil-law, tribunal-led approach:
- the tribunal is proactive, with an early case management conference (Arts 2–3);
- the tribunal and parties are encouraged to avoid any form of document production, including e-discovery (Art 4.2);
- the tribunal may apply legal provisions not pleaded, after seeking the parties' views (Art 7.2, iura novit curia);
- unless a party objects, the tribunal may help the parties settle (Art 9.1);
- with all parties' prior written consent, an arbitrator may act as mediator and, with written consent at the end, carry on as arbitrator if mediation fails (Arts 9.2–9.3).

> **Prague Rules, article by article (non-core beyond the end point)**
> - **Art 2 Case management conference:** held without unjustified delay after the tribunal receives the file (2.1). The tribunal discusses the timetable and clarifies relief sought, disputed and undisputed facts, and legal grounds (2.2). It may indicate disputed facts, suitable evidence, its view of the legal grounds and preliminary views on burden of proof, relief and weight of evidence; preliminary views are not evidence of partiality and are not grounds for disqualification (2.4). It may decide preliminary issues, limit the number and length of submissions, and fix strict time limits (2.5).
> - **Art 3 Fact-finding:** the tribunal is entitled and encouraged to be proactive, but the parties keep the burden of proof (3.1). It may on its own initiative request documents or witnesses, appoint experts (including on legal issues) and order site inspections (3.2). It shall consider a cut-off date for evidence, with no new evidence afterwards save in exceptional circumstances (3.3).
> - **Art 4 Documents:** submit documents relied on as early as possible (4.1). A party needing documents raises it at the CMC (4.3); later requests only in exceptional circumstances (4.4). A request must be for a specific document that is relevant and material, not public, and in the other party's possession or control (4.5). Non-public documents are confidential (4.8).
> - **Art 5 Fact witnesses:** identify each witness, the facts and relevance (5.1). The tribunal decides which witnesses are called and may refuse irrelevant, duplicative or burdensome ones (5.2–5.3). If a party insists on calling a witness whose statement the other side filed, the tribunal should generally call them (5.7). The tribunal controls examination and may hold witness conferences (5.9).
> - **Art 6 Experts:** the tribunal may appoint experts, seeking the parties' suggestions, approving terms of reference, and asking the parties to advance costs equally (6.1–6.2). Parties may still file their own expert reports (6.5). Experts may be told to prepare a joint list of questions and a joint report of agreed and disagreed issues (6.6–6.7).
> - **Art 8 Hearing:** seek to resolve the case on documents only where appropriate (8.1). If there is a hearing, keep it cost-efficient, e.g. limited length and video or telephone (8.2).
> - **Art 10 Adverse inference:** if a party fails without justifiable grounds to comply with any order or instruction, the tribunal may draw an adverse inference.
> - **Art 11 Costs:** the tribunal may consider the parties' cooperation in running the case efficiently.
> - **Art 12 Deliberations:** best efforts to issue the award as soon as possible; internal discussions before the hearing and deliberations soon after.

*Source note: PRAGUE Arts 1–12 (PDF pp3–12); ARB-Plan p4 (reading list)*

### drp-m-0-24 — Arbitral institutions and the SIAC Court
*Keywords: SIAC, ICC, HKIAC, LCIA, arbitral institution, SIAC Court, President of the SIAC Court, Registrar, appointing authority, IAA s 8(2), model clause*

**End point:** Common institutions in the region are SIAC, ICC, HKIAC and LCIA. Each publishes model clauses and rules and can administer arbitrations seated anywhere. Within SIAC:
- the SIAC Court, its President and the Registrar make the institutional decisions (appointments, challenges, procedure applications);
- the President of the SIAC Court is the statutory appointing authority under Model Law Art 11(3)–(4) (IAA s 8(2)), so can appoint even for a non-SIAC clause.

## 0.IV The arbitration agreement: form and parties

- **What is an arbitration agreement?** Agreement to submit present or future disputes on a defined legal relationship, contractual or not; clause or separate agreement (s 2A(1)–(2)) → [0-25](#drp-m-0-25)
- **Must it be in writing? Oral contract, email?** Yes, but "writing" = content recorded in any form, even if concluded orally or by conduct; accessible e-communication suffices (s 2A(3)–(5); AA s 4) → [0-26](#drp-m-0-26)
- **Claimant pleads an agreement and respondent does not deny it?** Deemed effective agreement (s 2A(6)) → [0-27](#drp-m-0-27)
- **Contract refers to standard terms containing an arbitration clause?** Incorporated if the reference makes the clause part of the contract; question of construction; no need to mention the clause specifically (s 2A(7); *Lufthansa* [34]) → [0-28](#drp-m-0-28)
- **Is a non-signatory bound?**
  - Implied consent, agency (actual, apparent, ratification), assignment → [0-29](#drp-m-0-29)
  - Group of companies doctrine doubted; estoppel very narrow → [0-30](#drp-m-0-30)
- **Adding a party to a SIAC arbitration?** Joinder: all agree, or additional party prima facie bound (SIAC25 r 18.1) → [0-31](#drp-m-0-31)
- **Several contracts or arbitrations?** Consolidation (r 16.1: all agree / same agreement / compatible agreements + same relationship, principal-ancillary, same transaction); coordinated proceedings (r 17) → [0-32](#drp-m-0-32)

### drp-m-0-25 — Definition: clause or separate agreement
*Keywords: arbitration agreement, definition, IAA s 2A(1), s 2A(2), arbitration clause, submission agreement, defined legal relationship, contractual or not*

**End point:** An "arbitration agreement" is an agreement to submit to arbitration all or certain disputes which have arisen or may arise between the parties in respect of a defined legal relationship, contractual or not (IAA s 2A(1); AA s 4(1)). It may be a clause in a contract or a separate agreement (s 2A(2); MAL Art 7(1)). An arbitral tribunal has jurisdiction only if there is a valid arbitration agreement.

*Source note: IAA s 2A(1)–(2); AA s 4(1)–(2); ARB-SN p6 para 9, p14 para 43*

### drp-m-0-26 — Writing requirement (s 2A(3)–(5) IAA; s 4 AA)
*Keywords: in writing, writing requirement, recorded in any form, concluded orally, by conduct, electronic communication, email, s 2A(3), s 2A(4), s 2A(5), s 2A(9), Art 7 does not apply*

**End point:** An arbitration agreement must be in writing (IAA s 2A(3); AA s 4(3)). It is in writing if its content is recorded in any form, even if the agreement or contract was concluded orally, by conduct or other means (s 2A(4)). An electronic communication suffices if its information is accessible for later reference (s 2A(5)). Model Law Art 7 does not apply (s 2A(9)). Singapore's version resembles Option I of the 2006 Art 7.

*Source note: IAA s 2A(3)–(5), (9); AA s 4(3)–(4); ARB-SN p14 paras 44–45; SampleQs p56 Q2. Conflict: CYR-ARB cites s 2A(9) for the deemed-agreement rule and s 2A(3) for "recorded in any form"; the IAA puts these in s 2A(6) and s 2A(4), as ARB-SN does.*

### drp-m-0-27 — Deemed agreement: assertion in a pleading not denied (s 2A(6))
*Keywords: deemed arbitration agreement, s 2A(6), assertion in a pleading, not denied, calls for a reply, non-signatory participating, silence*

**End point:** Where in any arbitral or legal proceedings a party asserts the existence of an arbitration agreement in a pleading, statement of case or other document, the assertion calls for a reply and it is not denied, there is deemed to be an effective arbitration agreement between the parties to those proceedings (IAA s 2A(6)). So a non-signatory who participates without objecting in time may be bound.

*Source note: IAA s 2A(6); ARB-SN p14 para 45, p32 para 112*

### drp-m-0-28 — Incorporation by reference (s 2A(7))
*Keywords: incorporation by reference, s 2A(7), standard terms, reference to document containing arbitration clause, International Research v Lufthansa, construction, Mancon, Concordia, bill of lading, charterparty, s 2A(8)*

**End point:** A reference in a contract to a document containing an arbitration clause makes an arbitration agreement in writing if the reference makes the clause part of the contract (IAA s 2A(7); bills of lading: s 2A(8)). The test is the parties' intention, a question of construction (*International Research Corp v Lufthansa Systems* [2013] SGCA 55 at [34]). Express reference to the arbitration clause itself is not necessary ([33]–[34]). Where the reference clearly adopts all the terms of the other document, its arbitration clause is included (*Mancon v Heng Holdings* [1999] SGHC 324 at [44]–[45]).

> **ARB-SN para 48, as the Notes put it**
> - The test is the parties' intention to incorporate, a question of construction (*Lufthansa* at [34]).
> - Specific words of incorporation may express that intention (*Concordia v Cornelder* [1999] SGHC 269).
> - Explicit reference to the arbitration clause is not necessary; requiring it would be far too restrictive (*Gay Construction v Caledonian Techmore*, per Kaplan J (Hong Kong), approved in *Lufthansa* at [33]).
> - A reference clearly adopting all the terms of the other document includes its arbitration clause (*Mancon*).

*Source note: IAA s 2A(7)–(8); ARB-SN p14–15 paras 46–48; [2013] SGCA 55 at [33]–[34]; [1999] SGHC 324 at [44]–[45]. Concordia Agritrading v Cornelder [1999] SGHC 269 at [33] took a stricter line (specific reference needed) than Lufthansa now allows; ARB-SN cites it only for the point that specific words may show intention, which remains true. Gay Construction is a Hong Kong case taken from ARB-SN (not checked).*

### drp-m-0-29 — Non-signatories: implied consent, agency, assignment
*Keywords: non-signatory, implied consent, conduct, negotiation, performance, agency, actual authority, apparent authority, ratification, assignment, assignee bound*

**End point:** Arbitration rests on consent, but several doctrines can bind a non-signatory, applying the law of the arbitration agreement:
- **Implied consent:** negotiation or performance of the contract may show an objective intention that the non-signatory is a party.
- **Agency:** an agent with actual, apparent or ratified authority binds its principal.
- **Assignment:** an assignee of the contract is ordinarily bound by its arbitration clause.

*Source note: ARB-SN p15–16 para 49*

### drp-m-0-30 — Non-signatories: group of companies and estoppel
*Keywords: group of companies doctrine, Dow Chemical, Manuchar Steel, estoppel, Jiang Haiying, Parmod Kumar Verma, non-signatory parent company*

**End point:** Both doctrines are weak under Singapore law.
- **Group of companies doctrine:** extends a clause to non-signatory group companies (ICC *Dow Chemical*, where the non-signatory itself invoked the clause). The High Court doubted it applies under Singapore law (*Manuchar Steel Hong Kong v Star Pacific Line* [2014] SGHC 181).
- **Estoppel:** very narrow. It was applied only to stop A from insisting that B start a separate arbitration, where C's arbitration against A was on the same facts and B was a party to the agreement on its proper construction (*Jiang Haiying v Tan Lim Hui* [2009] SGHC 42 at [26]–[28]). The wider US equitable estoppel approach has not been adopted.

*Source note: ARB-SN p16 para 49; [2014] SGHC 181; [2009] SGHC 42 at [26]–[28]; Parmod Kumar Verma v Unique Food Partners [2020] SGDC 254 (as described in ARB-SN; not on eLitigation 9 Nov 2026)*

### drp-m-0-31 — Joinder of additional parties (SIAC r 18)
*Keywords: joinder, additional party, SIAC r 18.1, prima facie bound, all parties agree, intervention, consent*

**End point:** Joinder (an existing party adding a third party) and intervention (a third party asking to join) both turn on consent. The Model Law, NYC and most clauses are silent; institutional rules deal with it. Under SIAC25 r 18.1 a party or non-party may apply, before the tribunal is constituted, to join an additional party where:
- (a) all parties, including the additional party, agree; or
- (b) the additional party is prima facie bound by the arbitration agreement.

*Source note: ARB-SN p17 para 50; SIAC25 r 18.1. Conflict: ARB-SN para 50 cites DFD v DFE [2023] SGHCR 23 as an example of joinder; that case concerned a stranger's intervention in court enforcement proceedings, not joinder to an arbitration, so it is not relied on.*

### drp-m-0-32 — Consolidation and coordinated proceedings (SIAC rr 15–17)
*Keywords: consolidation, multiple contracts, SIAC r 15, r 16.1, compatible arbitration agreements, same legal relationship, principal and ancillary contract, same transaction, coordinated proceedings, r 17*

**End point:** Consolidation merges two or more arbitrations into one. The Model Law and NYC are silent, and institutional rules govern. Under SIAC25 r 16.1, before any tribunal is constituted, arbitrations may be consolidated where one of three conditions is met (box). A claimant with several contracts may file separate Notices, separate Notices with a consolidation application, or one Notice deemed to be that application (r 15.1). Where the same tribunal sits in arbitrations sharing a common question, they may be coordinated but stay separate, with separate awards (r 17).

> **SIAC25 r 16.1: consolidation if**
> - (a) all parties agree; or
> - (b) all claims are made under the same arbitration agreement; or
> - (c) the agreements are compatible and the disputes arise out of:
>   - (i) the same legal relationship(s); or
>   - (ii) a principal contract and its ancillary contract(s); or
>   - (iii) the same transaction or series of transactions.

*Source note: ARB-SN p17 para 51; SIAC25 rr 15–17*

## 0.V Arbitrability

- **Can this dispute go to arbitration at all?** Yes unless contrary to public policy (s 11(1)); a statute giving courts jurisdiction does not by itself make it non-arbitrable (s 11(2)) → [0-33](#drp-m-0-33)
- **Does the clause cover tort / statutory claims?** Construed widely: all claims about the contract unless good reason otherwise (*Larsen* [19]; *Premium Nafta*) → [0-34](#drp-m-0-34)
- **Which subjects are not arbitrable?**
  - Public-interest matters: citizenship, marriage, statutory licences, winding up (*Aloe Vera*); insolvency avoidance claims (*Larsen*) → [0-35](#drp-m-0-35)
  - Minority oppression claims: arbitrable (*Tomolugen*); IP disputes: arbitrable (IAA s 26B) → [0-35](#drp-m-0-35)
- **Which law decides arbitrability before the award?** Composite: law of the arbitration agreement *and* Singapore as seat; foreign public policy is within s 11 (*Anupam Mittal*) → [0-36](#drp-m-0-36)
- **Foreign court has exclusive jurisdiction? Illegality alleged?** Still arbitrable (*Medipas*; *DMF v DMG*) → [0-37](#drp-m-0-37)
- **Award on a non-arbitrable matter?** Set aside (Art 34(2)(b)(i)) or refuse enforcement (s 31(4)(a); Art V(2)(a)), court's own motion → [0-38](#drp-m-0-38)

### drp-m-0-33 — Arbitrable unless contrary to public policy (s 11 IAA)
*Keywords: arbitrability, IAA s 11(1), contrary to public policy, s 11(2), written law confers jurisdiction on court, subjective arbitrability, objective arbitrability*

**End point:** Any dispute the parties have agreed to arbitrate may be determined by arbitration unless it is contrary to public policy to do so (IAA s 11(1)). There are two questions:
- **Subjective arbitrability:** did the parties agree to arbitrate this dispute?
- **Objective arbitrability:** does the law allow it?
That a statute gives a court jurisdiction over a matter, without mentioning arbitration, does not of itself make the dispute non-arbitrable (s 11(2)).

*Source note: IAA s 11; ARB-SN p17–18 paras 52–53, 56*

### drp-m-0-34 — Scope of the clause: construed widely (Larsen; Premium Nafta)
*Keywords: scope of arbitration clause, arising out of, in connection with, wide construction, rational businessmen, one-stop adjudication, Larsen Oil v Petroprod, Premium Nafta, Fiona Trust, tort claims, statutory claims, Frontier Holdings, mutatis mutandis*

**End point:** Arbitration clauses are construed generously: all claims relating to the contract, in contract or tort, common law or statute, fall within them unless there is good reason to conclude otherwise. The starting assumption is that rational businessmen want all disputes from their relationship decided by one tribunal (*Larsen Oil and Gas v Petroprod* [2011] SGCA 21 at [13], [19], applying *Premium Nafta v Fili Shipping*). Phrases such as "in connection with" and "mutatis mutandis" are matters of interpretation (*Frontier Holdings v Petroleum Exploration* [2024] SGHC(I) 34 at [102], [108]–[109]). In *Larsen* the pre-insolvency management was unlikely to have meant to include insolvency avoidance claims ([20]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p17–18 paras 54–55; [2011] SGCA 21 at [13], [19], [20]; [2024] SGHC(I) 34 at [102], [108]–[109]. Conflict: ARB-SN para 54 also cites CYY v CYZ [2023] 5 SLR 1109 at [40] and Frontier at [113]–[114] for wide construction; those paragraphs deal with other points ([2023] SGHC 101 at [40]–[41]: interpretation of a substantive clause is for the merits), so only Larsen is relied on.*

### drp-m-0-35 — Non-arbitrable subject matter; insolvency, oppression and IP disputes
*Keywords: non-arbitrable, public interest, citizenship, legitimacy of marriage, statutory licences, winding up, Aloe Vera, insolvency avoidance claims, Larsen, minority oppression, Tomolugen, s 216 Companies Act, intellectual property, IPR, IAA s 26B, IRDA*

**End point:**
- **No statutory list:** Singapore lists no non-arbitrable subjects by statute. Matters with public interest elements may not be arbitrable, for example citizenship, legitimacy of marriage, grants of statutory licences and winding up (*Aloe Vera of America v Asianic Food* [2006] SGHC 78 at [72]).
- **Insolvency avoidance claims:** non-arbitrable because they are in essence insolvency claims (*Larsen* [2011] SGCA 21 at [59]).
- **Minority oppression claims:** arbitrable (*Tomolugen Holdings v Silica Investors* [2015] SGCA 57 at [76], [84]–[91]).
- **IP disputes:** the subject matter of an IPR dispute, including validity and infringement, is arbitrable between the parties (IAA s 26B, Part 2A). An award on an IPR dispute is not open to challenge just because it concerns an IPR (ss 26D–26E).
- **Corporate insolvency under the IRDA:** arbitrable or not depending on the substantive issue (PYP23).

*Source note: ARB-SN p18 paras 55–56, p27 para 90; [2006] SGHC 78 at [72]; [2011] SGCA 21 at [59]; [2015] SGCA 57 at [76], [84]–[91]; IAA ss 26A–26E (Part 2A, Act 32 of 2019 wef 21 Nov 2019); PYP23 p15 Q22; PYP21-ARB Q1. Conflict: ARB-SN para 56, citing Aloe Vera (2006), lists "validity of registration of trademarks or patents, copyrights" as possibly non-arbitrable; IAA s 26B now makes IPR disputes arbitrable, and the statute is followed. ARB-SN para 87 gives Tomolugen as "[2015] 1 SLR 373"; it is reported at [2016] 1 SLR 373.*

### drp-m-0-36 — Foreign public policy: the Anupam Mittal composite approach
*Keywords: Anupam Mittal v Westbridge, composite approach, foreign public policy, s 11 IAA, law of the arbitration agreement, law of the seat, pre-award arbitrability, Indian law, oppression*

**End point:** "Public policy" in IAA s 11 includes foreign public policy (*Anupam Mittal v Westbridge Ventures II* [2023] SGCA 1 at [48]–[52]). Arbitrability before the award is decided by a composite approach ([55]):
- (1) under the law governing the arbitration agreement: if that foreign law makes the subject matter non-arbitrable, the court will not let the arbitration proceed under s 11;
- (2) under Singapore law as the law of the seat: if the dispute is arbitrable under the agreement's law but non-arbitrable in Singapore, the arbitration also cannot proceed.

*Source note: ARB-SN p18–19 para 57; [2023] SGCA 1 at [48]–[52], [55]; PYP23 p6 Q13 (law of the arbitration agreement and law of the seat)*

### drp-m-0-37 — Foreign exclusive jurisdiction and illegality are no bar (Medipas; DMF)
*Keywords: Medipas v Erdenet, Mongolia, immovable property, exclusive jurisdiction of foreign courts, DMF v DMG, illegality arbitrable, public policy at setting aside*

**End point:** That the subject matter falls within a foreign court's exclusive jurisdiction does not necessarily make it non-arbitrable under Singapore law. Disputes over rights in Mongolian immovable property were arbitrable (*Medipas v Erdenet Mining* [2026] SGHC 97 at [75]). Illegality is also arbitrable, and the tribunal may decide it first. After the award, the court considers under Art 34(2)(b) whether the subject matter was arbitrable and whether the award conflicts with public policy (*DMF v DMG* [2025] SGHC(I) 12 at [192]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p19 paras 58–59; [2026] SGHC 97 at [75]; [2025] SGHC(I) 12 at [192]*

### drp-m-0-38 — Consequences of non-arbitrability
*Keywords: non-arbitrable award, set aside, Art 34(2)(b)(i), refuse enforcement, s 31(4)(a), Art V(2)(a), court's own initiative*

**End point:** An award on a non-arbitrable dispute may be set aside (Art 34(2)(b)(i)) or refused enforcement (IAA s 31(4)(a); NYC Art V(2)(a)). The court may raise these grounds on its own initiative. Before the award, a court asked to stay may refuse because the dispute cannot proceed under s 11.

*Source note: ARB-SN p19 para 60; IAA s 31(4)(a); MAL Art 34(2)(b)(i)*

## 0.VI Pathological clauses and drafting

- **Defective clause: is it void?** "Pathological" is descriptive only; effective interpretation saves it unless no coherent intention can be inferred (*Insigma* [38]–[39]) → [0-39](#drp-m-0-39)
- **Non-existent or misnamed institution ("China International Arbitration Center")?** Upheld if the dominant purpose is arbitration and the intended institution can be identified (*Lucky-Goldstar*; *Re Shanghai Xinan*) → [0-40](#drp-m-0-40)
- **No seat, no appointment method ("arbitration as per Singapore Contract Rules")?** Bare clause upheld; SIAC President appoints under Art 11(3) (*KVC Rice*) → [0-41](#drp-m-0-41)
- **SIAC administering under ICC Rules?** Hybrid clause valid in principle if the institution can supply equivalent organs (*Insigma* [35]; *HKL v Rizq*); avoid: enforcement risk → [0-42](#drp-m-0-42)
- **What must a clause contain? Two arbitrators?** Parties, exclusivity, scope, seat, institution and rules, number (avoid two), language, governing law of the clause, expedited option → [0-43](#drp-m-0-43)
- **"May" / "can" refer to arbitration?** Arguably obligatory (*Madiford*; *Guangdong*); draft "shall" → [0-44](#drp-m-0-44)
- **Later contract has a court clause?** Displaces arbitration only if explicit (*CNA v CNB* [48]) → [0-45](#drp-m-0-45)
- **Escalation / med-arb clauses** → see 0.XII → [0-92](#drp-m-0-92)

### drp-m-0-39 — Pathological clauses: descriptive label, effective interpretation (Insigma)
*Keywords: pathological clause, defective arbitration clause, effective interpretation, curable defect, Insigma v Alstom, descriptive not prescriptive, coherent intention*

**End point:** Calling a clause "pathological" describes it; it does not invalidate it. Whether it survives depends on the nature of the defect and whether it can be cured (*Insigma Technology v Alstom Technology* [2009] SGCA 24 at [38]). Courts use effective interpretation to give effect to the intention to arbitrate. A clause fails only if it is impossible to infer an intention coherent and effective enough for the arbitration to function ([39]).

> **Typical defects (ARB-SN para 63)**
> - naming the institution wrongly, or a non-existent institution;
> - one institution to administer another's rules;
> - naming the institution by its location only;
> - not making the award final and binding;
> - a named arbitrator who has died or cannot act;
> - internally contradictory terms.

*Source note: ARB-SN p19–20 paras 61–63; [2009] SGCA 24 at [38]–[39]; PYP23 p59 Q6*

### drp-m-0-40 — Wrong or non-existent institution named
*Keywords: non-existent institution, misnamed institution, Lucky-Goldstar, International Commercial Arbitration Association, Re Shanghai Xinan, China International Arbitration Center, CIETAC, dominant purpose*

**End point:** Choosing the rules of a non-existent organisation does not nullify the agreement. If the dominant purpose is arbitration, the court may identify the intended institution or direct arbitration before an appropriate tribunal (*Lucky-Goldstar v Ng Moo Kee* (HK, 1993), stay granted). A clause naming the non-existent "China International Arbitration Center" was read as CIETAC and the CIETAC award upheld (*Re Shanghai Xinan Screenwall* [2022] SGHC 58). It is enough that the parties objectively meant the same institution.

*Source note: ARB-SN p20–21 paras 64–65; [2022] SGHC 58; Lucky-Goldstar is a Hong Kong case taken from ARB-SN (not checked)*

### drp-m-0-41 — Bare clause: no seat, no appointment method (KVC Rice)
*Keywords: bare arbitration clause, KVC Rice v Asian Mineral Resources, Singapore Contract Rules, Indian Contract Rules, no seat, appointment, President of SIAC Court, Art 11(3), IAA s 8(2)*

**End point:** A "bare" clause that names neither the seat nor how to appoint arbitrators can still be enforced. In *KVC Rice Intertrade v Asian Mineral Resources* [2017] SGHC 32 ("arbitration as per Singapore Contract Rules" / "Indian Contract Rules") the clauses were upheld. The President of the SIAC Court, as statutory appointing authority, may appoint under Art 11(3) even where the seat is unclear or not yet determined (IAA s 8(2)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p21 para 66; [2017] SGHC 32 at [33]–[35]; IAA s 8(2)*

### drp-m-0-42 — Hybrid clause: one institution administers another's rules
*Keywords: hybrid arbitration clause, SIAC administering ICC Rules, Insigma v Alstom, HKL Group v Rizq, Arbitration Committee at Singapore, ICC Rules Art 1(2), avoid hybrid clauses, enforceability, reciprocity*

**End point:** There is no objection in principle to a hybrid clause (one institution administering another's rules), provided the administering institution can supply organs to perform the equivalent functions (*Insigma* [2009] SGCA 24 at [34]–[35]: SIAC under ICC Rules upheld). In *HKL Group v Rizq International* [2013] SGHCR 5 (a non-existent "Arbitration Committee at Singapore" under ICC Rules) a stay was granted on condition that the parties obtain SIAC's or another Singapore institution's agreement to run a hybrid arbitration, with liberty to apply. That was despite the ICC since amending its rules to say only the ICC may administer them. Avoid hybrids: an award upheld in Singapore may not be enforceable elsewhere.

*Source note: ARB-SN p21–22 paras 67–71; [2009] SGCA 24 at [34]–[35]; [2013] SGHCR 5 at [28]–[29], [37]; PYP23 p59 Q6*

### drp-m-0-43 — Essential elements of a clause; model clauses
*Keywords: drafting arbitration clause, essential elements, model clause, SIAC model clause, number of arbitrators, avoid two arbitrators, seat, language, governing law of arbitration agreement, expedited procedure, multi-tier, SICC*

**End point:** Use the institution's model clause where possible. The SIAC model clause refers any dispute "arising out of or in connection with this contract, including any question regarding its existence, validity or termination" to SIAC under its rules "for the time being in force". The box lists what a free-drafted clause needs. The CS1 exercise (master supply contract) asks the candidate to weigh SICC litigation against arbitration, multi-tier clauses, and the institution and seat.

> **Essential elements (ARB-SN para 72)**
> - certainty of the parties' identity;
> - that disputes go exclusively to arbitration ("shall");
> - the scope of disputes;
> - the seat;
> - the institution and its rules, if any;
> - the number of arbitrators (avoid two);
> - the language;
> - the governing law of the arbitration agreement;
> - whether expedited arbitration may apply.

> **Worked answer to ARB-CS1 Q2 (India Bittal / Sino Steel master supply contract; orders of US$1–2m and US$10m)**
> - **SICC or arbitration?** The award must be enforced against a Chinese counterparty, probably in China. India and China are both New York Convention States, so a Singapore-seated award is enforceable there under the NYC (ARB-SN paras 25–26). A SICC judgment would depend on each country's own rules for foreign judgments, a less certain route. Arbitration also gives party-chosen arbitrators, neutrality and confidentiality. Recommend arbitration.
> - **Seat:** Singapore. It is neutral (neither party's home), a NYC State, has a supportive and high-quality judiciary, and is politically stable (the ARB-SN para 25 factors).
> - **Institution:** SIAC, which administers the case and whose President and Court can appoint and decide challenges. Under SIAC25, a US$1–2m order (about S$1.3–2.7m) falls in the Expedited Procedure band (S$1m–10m, r 14), and a US$10m order (above S$10m) runs under the full rules. A claim under S$1m goes to the Streamlined Procedure (r 13).
> - **Multi-tier?** Keep it short and certain: negotiation between named senior executives for a fixed period (e.g. 30 days), then arbitration. A vague step is unenforceable (*CZQ v CZS*, 0-92). Optionally provide for mediation under the SIAC-SIMC AMA Protocol (0-90) without making it a precondition.
> - **Clause:** the SIAC model clause, adding seat (Singapore), number of arbitrators (one, or three for disputes over a stated sum), language (English), the law of the contract and the law of the arbitration agreement (Singapore), and the tiering step.

*Source note: ARB-SN p22–23 paras 72–73; SIAC25 p95 (model clause), rr 13–14; ARB-CS1 p2–3 (DR clause exercise). The course gives no model answer to CS1 Q2; the worked answer applies ARB-SN paras 25–26, 72 and SIAC25 to the email's facts. The exchange rate (about S$1.33 per US$1) is approximate and only places the orders in the SIAC bands.*

### drp-m-0-44 — "May" or "can" refer to arbitration
*Keywords: optional arbitration clause, may refer, can refer, shall refer, China State Construction v Madiford, Guangdong Agriculture v Conagra, clear obligation to arbitrate*

**End point:** Avoid optional wording; provide that disputes "shall" be referred to arbitration. Whether "may" creates an obligation is arguable. Some courts read "may refer to arbitration" as obliging the parties once one elects (*China State Construction v Madiford*, HK 1992). A clause saying disputes "can" be submitted to arbitration after failed negotiations was upheld as an agreement to arbitrate (*Guangdong Agriculture v Conagra*, HK 1993).

*Source note: ARB-SN p23 paras 73–75 (Hong Kong cases taken from ARB-SN; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-45 — A later agreement must be explicit to displace arbitration (CNA v CNB)
*Keywords: supersede arbitration agreement, later agreement, explicit in its terms, CNA v CNB, court jurisdiction clause, displacement*

**End point:** If parties later agree a different dispute resolution provision, it supersedes the agreement to arbitrate only if it is sufficiently "explicit in its terms" (*CNA v CNB* [2024] SGCA(I) 2 at [48]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p24 para 76; [2024] SGCA(I) 2 at [48]*

## 0.VII Stay of court proceedings under the IAA

- **Sued in a Singapore court despite an arbitration clause: what to do?** Apply to stay under IAA s 6 after filing the notice of intention to contest or not contest and before any pleading or other step (Art 8(1); NYC Art II(3)) → [0-46](#drp-m-0-46)
- **Does the court have a discretion?** No: it "is to" stay unless the agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed (s 6(2)); contrast AA → [0-47](#drp-m-0-47)
- **Is the existence or scope of the agreement disputed: how closely does the court look?** Prima facie only; stay if (a) valid agreement, (b) dispute within scope, (c) not null/inoperative, shown prima facie (*Tomolugen*; *Malini Ventura*) → [0-48](#drp-m-0-48)
- **Defendant has no real defence: still a "dispute"?** Yes: a positive assertion disputing the claim suffices; no dispute only on clear and unequivocal admission (*Dalian* [75]; *Tjong Very Sumito*) → [0-49](#drp-m-0-49)
- **Conditions on the stay? Arrested ship?** Terms as the court thinks fit; interim orders on property; retain arrested property as security (ss 6(2)–(3), 7); courts slow to interfere (*The Duden*) → [0-50](#drp-m-0-50)
- **Can the arbitration start while the stay application is pending? What if nothing happens after the stay?** Yes (Art 8(2)); court may discontinue after 2 years' inactivity (s 6(4)) → [0-51](#drp-m-0-51)
- **Foreign court proceedings in breach?** Anti-suit injunction (0.XVI) → [0-122](#drp-m-0-122)

### drp-m-0-46 — Who may apply and when (s 6(1) IAA)
*Keywords: stay of court proceedings, IAA s 6(1), notice of intention to contest or not contest, before delivering any pleading, taking any other step, Art 8(1) Model Law, first statement on the substance, Art II(3) NYC, person claiming through or under*

**End point:** Where a party to an IAA arbitration agreement sues another party on a matter within the agreement, any party may apply to that court to stay the proceedings so far as they relate to that matter (IAA s 6(1)). The application is made at any time after filing and serving a notice of intention to contest or not contest, and before delivering any pleading (other than one contesting jurisdiction) or taking any other step. Under the Model Law the limit is the first statement on the substance (Art 8(1)). "Party" includes a person claiming through or under a party; "court" includes the State Courts (s 6(5)).

*Source note: IAA s 6(1), (5) (Act 25 of 2021 wef 1 Apr 2022); MAL Art 8(1); NYC Art II(3); ARB-SN p24 paras 77–78. Conflict: ARB-SN para 78 states the IAA timing as "after appearance"; since 1 Apr 2022 s 6(1) reads "after filing and serving a notice of intention to contest or not contest", and the current text is followed.*

### drp-m-0-47 — Mandatory stay unless null and void, inoperative or incapable (s 6(2))
*Keywords: mandatory stay, is to make an order, IAA s 6(2), null and void, inoperative, incapable of being performed, no discretion, international arbitration, Art 8(1)*

**End point:** Under the IAA the court "is to make an order", on such terms as it thinks fit, staying the proceedings so far as they relate to the matter. The only exception is where it is satisfied that the arbitration agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed (s 6(2); Art 8(1); NYC Art II(3)). There is no residual discretion to refuse a stay of an arbitrable matter within the clause. Contrast the AA (0-14).

*Source note: IAA s 6(2); ARB-SN p24 para 79; PYP23 p60 Q7. Conflict: CYR-ARB and NIC-ARB state s 6(2) as "the court shall stay"; the current text (2020 Rev Ed) reads "is to make an order", which has the same mandatory effect.*

### drp-m-0-48 — Prima facie standard: the Tomolugen test
*Keywords: Tomolugen v Silica Investors, prima facie, stay test, valid arbitration agreement, within scope, not null and void, Malini Ventura v Knight Capital, Dalian Hualiang, arguable, balance of probabilities rejected, competence-competence*

**End point:** The court examines the tribunal's jurisdiction only prima facie, because that is for the tribunal. It grants a stay if the applicant shows a prima facie case of three things (box) (*Tomolugen Holdings v Silica Investors* [2015] SGCA 57 at [63]). It refuses only where it is clear on the evidence that a requirement is not met ([64]). If it is arguable that the dispute is within the clause or that a litigant is a party to it, a stay follows (*Dalian Hualiang v Louis Dreyfus* [2005] SGHC 161 at [21]). The prima facie approach applies even where the very existence of the agreement is disputed (*Malini Ventura v Knight Capital* [2015] SGHC 225, rejecting the English balance-of-probabilities approach). The tribunal's ruling remains subject to court review under s 10(3), setting aside and enforcement.

> **Tomolugen [2015] SGCA 57 at [63]: stay if, prima facie,**
> - (a) there is a valid arbitration agreement between the parties to the court proceedings;
> - (b) the dispute (or any part of it) falls within its scope; and
> - (c) the arbitration agreement is not null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed.

*Source note: ARB-SN p26–27 paras 85–89; [2015] SGCA 57 at [63]–[64]; [2005] SGHC 161 at [21]; [2015] SGHC 225; PYP23 p8 Q15 (prima facie test)*

### drp-m-0-49 — Is there a "dispute"? Denying liability or quantum is enough
*Keywords: dispute, mere denial, denial of liability, denial of quantum, no defence, positive assertion, clear and unequivocal admission, Dalian Hualiang, Tjong Very Sumito v Antig, DKB v DKC, merits not examined*

**End point:** The court does not look at the merits. A denial of liability or of the quantum claimed is enough, even where no defence exists (ARB-SN para 82: "a mere denial"). Put precisely, a dispute exists once the defendant positively asserts that the claim is disputed, even if the assertion is easily shown to be wrong; silence or a bare failure to pay is not a denial (*Dalian Hualiang* [2005] SGHC 161 at [75]–[76]; *Tjong Very Sumito v Antig Investments* [2009] SGCA 41 at [49], [51]; *DKB v DKC* [2025] SGHC(I) 11 at [67]). There is no dispute where liability and quantum have been clearly and unequivocally admitted (*Tjong* at [36]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p25 para 82; [2005] SGHC 161 at [75]–[76]; [2009] SGCA 41 at [36], [49], [51]; [2025] SGHC(I) 11 at [67]. Conflict: ARB-SN para 82 cites Dalian at [43] and says "a mere denial" suffices; [43] quotes a Hong Kong judge, and the holding at [75] requires a positive assertion that the claim is disputed (mere silence or refusal to pay is not enough), so the block states the holding at [75].*

### drp-m-0-50 — Terms, interim orders and security (ss 6(2)–(3), 7)
*Keywords: conditions on stay, terms, IAA s 6(3), interim or supplementary order, property, s 7, arrested property retained as security, The Duden, slow to interfere*

**End point:** The court may stay on such terms or conditions as it thinks fit (IAA s 6(2)). It may make interim or supplementary orders over property in dispute to preserve the parties' rights (s 6(3)). Where property was arrested or security given, it may order the property kept as security for any award, or make the stay conditional on equivalent security (s 7(1)). The discretion over terms is unfettered, but courts are slow to interfere in the arbitral process (*The "Duden"* [2008] SGHC 149 at [12], [15]).

*Source note: IAA ss 6(2)–(3), 7; ARB-SN p26 para 84; [2008] SGHC 149 at [12], [15]*

### drp-m-0-51 — Arbitration may run meanwhile; discontinuance after 2 years
*Keywords: Art 8(2), arbitration continues while stay pending, award may be made, IAA s 6(4), discontinue after 2 years, no further step, reinstate*

**End point:** While a stay application is pending, arbitral proceedings may be commenced or continued and an award made (Art 8(2)). After a stay, if no party takes a further step in the court proceedings for at least 2 years, the court may on its own motion discontinue them, without prejudice to an application to reinstate (IAA s 6(4)).
- **Post-award agreements on enforcement:** where parties agree after the award that enforcement is subject to contractual restraints, and refer disputes about those restraints to arbitration, s 6 can stay court proceedings and refer that dispute to arbitration (*DKB v DKC* [2025] SGHC(I) 11 at [72]). Section 31 IAA lists grounds for refusing to enforce an award; it does not cover parties' own post-award agreements on enforcement, so such a stay is not a challenge to the award and does not undermine s 31 ([73]).

*Source note: MAL Art 8(2); IAA s 6(4); ARB-SN p25 para 83; [2025] SGHC(I) 11 at [72]–[73] (as stated in ARB-SN)*

## 0.VIII Jurisdiction of the tribunal

- **Can the tribunal decide its own jurisdiction?** Yes: competence-competence (Art 16(1); SIAC25 r 31.1) → [0-52](#drp-m-0-52)
- **Contract void for fraud / illegality: does the clause fall with it?** No: separability; the challenge must target the clause itself (Art 16(1); *Premium Nafta*) → [0-53](#drp-m-0-53)
- **No contract was ever concluded (exchange of standard forms)?** Separability does not apply to formation (*COT v COU* [30]) → [0-54](#drp-m-0-54)
- **Claimant skipped the agreed negotiation / mediation step?** Unsettled: *Lufthansa* (jurisdiction) v *DRO*, *DSQ* (admissibility, generally) → [0-55](#drp-m-0-55)
- **When must the objection be raised?**
  - No jurisdiction: no later than the statement of defence; exceeding jurisdiction: as soon as the matter arises (SIAC: 15 days); late plea if justified; appointing an arbitrator is no bar (Art 16(2); r 31.2) → [0-56](#drp-m-0-56)
  - Failing to object = waiver (Art 4; SIAC r 64.1: 15 days); non-signatory who pleads without denying → deemed agreement (s 2A(6)) → [0-57](#drp-m-0-57)
- **Preliminary question or with the merits? Bifurcate?** Either (Art 16(3); s 10(2); r 31.3); bifurcate if jurisdiction is separable and the objection is not frivolous → [0-58](#drp-m-0-58)
- **Tribunal rules on jurisdiction: can a party go to court?**
  - IAA s 10(3): positive ruling *as a preliminary question* or negative ruling *at any stage* → High Court within 30 days; appeal only with permission (s 10(4)–(5)); arbitration continues (s 10(9)) → [0-59](#drp-m-0-59)
  - Model Law Art 16(3): only a positive preliminary ruling; no recourse against a negative ruling; a negative ruling is not an award (*PT Asuransi* [66]) → [0-60](#drp-m-0-60)
- **Does the court defer to the tribunal?** No: de novo, original not appellate jurisdiction; further evidence at the court's discretion → [0-61](#drp-m-0-61)
- **Respondent tells SIAC there is no agreement?** Registrar may refer to the SIAC Court for a prima facie determination; otherwise the tribunal decides (r 8) → [0-62](#drp-m-0-62)

### drp-m-0-52 — Competence-competence (Art 16(1))
*Keywords: competence-competence, kompetenz-kompetenz, tribunal rules on its own jurisdiction, Art 16(1), SIAC r 31.1, existence, validity, scope*

**End point:** The tribunal may rule on its own jurisdiction, including objections to the existence or validity of the arbitration agreement (Art 16(1)). SIAC25 r 31.1 adds applicability and scope. Its ruling is subject to court review (s 10(3), setting aside, enforcement).
- **Kinds of objection:** jurisdictional objections are either absolute (the whole claim: e.g. no capacity, illegality, agreement incapable of being performed) or partial (a particular issue falls outside the clause's scope) (ARB-SN para 92). A partial objection leaves the tribunal seised of the rest; compare severance on setting aside (0-151).

*Source note: MAL Art 16(1); ARB-SN p28 para 92, p30 para 103; SIAC25 r 31.1; PYP23 p1 Q8; SampleQs p57 Q5 (key p122: B)*

### drp-m-0-53 — Separability: attack the clause, not the contract
*Keywords: separability, severability, arbitration clause independent of contract, Art 16(1), Premium Nafta, Ferris v Plaister, Philippines v PIATCO, contract void, null and void contract, SIAC r 31.1*

**End point:** An arbitration clause is treated as an agreement independent of the other terms (Art 16(1); SIAC25 r 31.1). A finding that the contract is void does not of itself invalidate the clause. So the tribunal can declare the contract invalid yet keep jurisdiction over the consequences. The clause can be attacked only on grounds that relate directly to it (*Premium Nafta v Fili Shipping* [2007] UKHL 40 at [17]). This is applied in Singapore (*Government of the Philippines v Philippine International Air Terminals* [2006] SGHC 206). Practically, alleging the whole contract is invalid is not enough; the challenge must target the clause.

*Source note: MAL Art 16(1); ARB-SN p31 paras 104–107; [2006] SGHC 206 at [27] ff; Premium Nafta and Ferris v Plaister are foreign cases taken from ARB-SN (not checked)*

### drp-m-0-54 — Separability does not apply to contract formation (COT v COU)
*Keywords: separability, formation, validity, contract never concluded, COT v COU, DHL v Gemini, battle of forms, no agreement*

**End point:** Separability applies to questions of contractual *validity*, not *formation*. If a party says no contract was ever concluded, the clause in the alleged contract does not survive by separability (*COT v COU* [2023] SGCA 31 at [30], following *DHL v Gemini Ocean* (Eng CA)). On a setting-aside application based on the absence of a binding contract, the seat court decides whether the contract existed ([39]). This is the ARB-CS2 Q3 scenario: an exchange of standard forms and a client that says no contract was agreed.

*Source note: ARB-SN p31 para 108, p70–71 para 225(iii); [2023] SGCA 31 at [30], [39]; ARB-CS2 p1–2 Q3*

### drp-m-0-55 — Jurisdiction v admissibility; pre-arbitration conditions
*Keywords: jurisdiction v admissibility, pre-arbitral conditions, condition precedent, multi-tier clause, negotiation before arbitration, International Research v Lufthansa, DRO v DRP, DSQ v DSR, unsettled*

**End point:** Jurisdiction is the tribunal's power to hear a case; admissibility is whether it is appropriate to hear it (*DRO v DRP* [2025] SGHC 255 at [63]). Whether failing to meet a pre-arbitration step goes to jurisdiction or admissibility is unsettled:
- **Lufthansa:** the unsatisfied condition precedent meant the tribunal lacked jurisdiction (*International Research v Lufthansa* [2013] SGCA 55 at [63]). The distinction was not argued.
- **DRO:** Lufthansa's view was obiter ([59]); non-compliance is generally a matter of admissibility ([63]).
- **DSQ:** agreed, as a general proposition that can be displaced (*DSQ v DSR* [2026] SGHC 67 at [87]).
- The Court of Appeal is yet to decide (*DRO* at [68]).
It matters because admissibility rulings are not open to court review as jurisdictional ones are.

*Source note: ARB-SN p28–29 paras 93–96; [2013] SGCA 55 at [63]; [2025] SGHC 255 at [59], [63], [68]; [2026] SGHC 67 at [87]. Conflict: ARB-SN para 95 cites DRO at [59] for the admissibility holding; [59] says Lufthansa was obiter, and the holding is at [63].*

### drp-m-0-56 — When to object (Art 16(2); SIAC r 31.2)
*Keywords: plea of no jurisdiction, timing, not later than statement of defence, exceeding scope of authority, as soon as, Art 16(2), SIAC r 31.2, 15 days, late plea justified, appointed arbitrator not precluded*

**End point:** A plea that the tribunal lacks jurisdiction must be raised no later than the statement of defence. A plea that it is exceeding its authority must be raised as soon as the matter arises (Art 16(2)). The tribunal may admit a later plea if it considers the delay justified. Appointing or participating in appointing an arbitrator does not bar the plea. SIAC25 r 31.2 is to the same effect but gives 15 days for an excess-of-jurisdiction objection.

*Source note: MAL Art 16(2); ARB-SN p32 paras 109–110; SIAC25 r 31.2; PYP23 p4 Q11*

### drp-m-0-57 — Waiver by failing to object (Art 4; SIAC r 64)
*Keywords: waiver, Art 4, failure to object, without undue delay, SIAC r 64.1, 15 days, deemed agreement, s 2A(6), non-signatory participates*

**End point:** A party who knows of a non-compliance yet proceeds without objecting without undue delay, or within any time limit, is deemed to have waived its objection (Art 4). Under SIAC25 r 64.1 the objection must be in writing within the rules' time limit or, if there is none, within 15 days of knowledge. A non-signatory who takes part and does not deny an asserted arbitration agreement may be deemed party to one (IAA s 2A(6)).

*Source note: MAL Art 4; IAA s 2A(6); ARB-SN p32 paras 111–112; SIAC25 r 64.1*

### drp-m-0-58 — Preliminary ruling or ruling with the merits; bifurcation
*Keywords: preliminary question, bifurcation, Art 16(3), IAA s 10(2), SIAC r 31.3, award on the merits, frivolous objection, dilatory tactics*

**End point:** The tribunal may rule on jurisdiction as a preliminary question (bifurcating) or in its award on the merits (Art 16(3); IAA s 10(2), "at any stage"; SIAC25 r 31.3). Bifurcation can avoid long merits proceedings where jurisdiction is doubtful and separable from the merits. Frivolous objections, a common delaying tactic, weigh against it.

*Source note: MAL Art 16(3); IAA s 10(2); ARB-SN p32–33 paras 114–115; SIAC25 r 31.3*

### drp-m-0-59 — Court review under s 10(3) IAA: 30 days; positive or negative ruling
*Keywords: IAA s 10(3), appeal on ruling of jurisdiction, 30 days, positive ruling preliminary question, negative ruling any stage, General Division of the High Court, s 10(4) permission of appellate court, s 10(5), s 10(9) no stay, costs s 10(7), O 48 r 2*

**End point:** If the tribunal rules (a) as a preliminary question that it has jurisdiction, or (b) at any stage that it has no jurisdiction, any party may apply to the General Division of the High Court within 30 days of receiving notice of the ruling (IAA s 10(3)). The application is by originating application (ROC O 48 r 2). Any appeal is only with the appellate court's permission, and there is no appeal against a refusal (s 10(4)–(5)). The application does not stay the arbitration unless the court orders (s 10(9)). If the court finds jurisdiction, the tribunal must continue (s 10(6)).

*Source note: IAA s 10 (s 10(4)–(5) say "permission", Act 40 of 2019); ROC O 48 r 2(1)(c), (2); ARB-SN p34 para 117; PYP23 p17 Q24. Conflict: ARB-SN para 117 says "leave"; the current s 10(4)–(5) say "permission".*

### drp-m-0-60 — Model Law Art 16(3) compared; a jurisdiction ruling is not an award
*Keywords: Art 16(3), Model Law, preliminary ruling, 30 days, no appeal, negative jurisdiction ruling, no recourse, not an award, PT Asuransi v Dexia, Art 34*

**End point:** Under Art 16(3) only a *positive* preliminary ruling can be taken to the court, within 30 days, and its decision is final. The tribunal may continue meanwhile. The Model Law gives no recourse against a ruling that the tribunal lacks jurisdiction. A negative ruling is not a decision on the substance, so it is not an "award" and cannot be set aside under Art 34 (*PT Asuransi Jasa Indonesia v Dexia Bank* [2006] SGCA 41 at [66]). Singapore's s 10(3) adds review of negative rulings. A jurisdictional objection can still be raised against an award on the merits (Art 34(2)(a)(i), (iii); NYC Art V(1)(a), (c)).

*Source note: MAL Art 16(3); ARB-SN p33 para 116; [2006] SGCA 41 at [66]; PYP23 p17 Q24. Conflict: ARB-SN paras 116 and 197 state broadly that "a decision on jurisdiction alone is not an award"; PT Asuransi at [66] concerned a negative ruling, so the block states it for negative rulings.*

### drp-m-0-61 — Standard of court review: de novo; further evidence
*Keywords: de novo review, original jurisdiction, not appellate, Insigma (HC), First Media, AQZ v ARA, further evidence, Laos v Sanum, Ladd v Marshall modified, COT v COU, curial review*

**End point:** Whenever the seat court reviews jurisdiction (under s 10(3) or on setting aside), it decides afresh and is not bound by the tribunal's view. Its jurisdiction is original, not appellate (*Insigma v Alstom* [2008] SGHC 134 at [21]; *PT First Media* [2013] SGCA 57). It may consider the material before it unfettered (*AQZ v ARA* [2015] SGHC 49 at [57]). On new evidence:
- the court has a discretion over what evidence it receives, and how; a party has no right to a full rehearing (*COT v COU* [2023] SGHC 69 at [57]);
- *Laos v Sanum* [2015] SGHC 15 at [43]–[44] applied a modified, slightly less strict version of the *Ladd v Marshall* test.

*Source note: ARB-SN p34 paras 118–119; [2008] SGHC 134 at [21]; [2015] SGHC 49 at [57]; [2023] SGHC 69 at [57]; [2015] SGHC 15 at [43]–[44]; PYP23 p14 Q21. Conflict: ARB-SN para 119 says Laos v Sanum applied a "stricter approach mirroring" Ladd v Marshall; at [43]–[44] the court said Ladd v Marshall did not strictly apply and used a modified version.*

### drp-m-0-62 — SIAC Court's prima facie review (SIAC r 8)
*Keywords: SIAC r 8, prima facie jurisdictional objection, SIAC Court, Registrar, respondent fails to submit Response, existence validity applicability, terminate arbitration*

**End point:** If the respondent files no Response, or a party objects to the existence, validity or applicability of the arbitration agreement, the arbitration proceeds and the tribunal decides jurisdiction. The exception is where the Registrar, before the tribunal is constituted, refers the matter to the SIAC Court (SIAC25 r 8.1). The SIAC Court decides prima facie whether and how far the arbitration proceeds (r 8.2). A decision to proceed does not limit the tribunal's power to rule on jurisdiction; a decision not to proceed leads to termination (r 8.3). This is ARB-CS2 Q3(a): how to respond to SIAC.
- **ICC equivalent (ARB-CS2 Q1, ICC clause, Singapore seat):** under the ICC Rules 2021 the tribunal decides jurisdictional pleas directly, unless the Secretary General refers the matter to the ICC Court; the Court then lets the arbitration proceed if, and to the extent, it is prima facie satisfied that an ICC arbitration agreement may exist (Art 6(3)–(4)). As with SIAC, a decision to proceed leaves the tribunal free to decide jurisdiction. In CS2 Q1 the client's main move is a court stay under IAA s 6 (0-46 to 0-48), while answering the ICC Request (Answer within 30 days, Art 5) and taking part in constituting the tribunal.

*Source note: SIAC25 rr 7.1(d), 8.1–8.3; ARB-CS2 p1 Q1, p2 Q3(a)–(b). The ICC Rules 2021 are not in `authorities/` and not Course Material; Arts 5–6 are stated from the published ICC text.*

## 0.IX Resisting jurisdiction: the respondent's options

- **Client says there is no arbitration agreement: what can it do?** (ARB-CS2 Q3)
  - Participate and object; ask for a preliminary ruling; if the tribunal upholds jurisdiction, apply under s 10(3) within 30 days → [0-63](#drp-m-0-63)
  - Does not apply under s 10(3)? Probably cannot later set aside on the same ground, but can still resist enforcement (*First Media* obiter) → [0-64](#drp-m-0-64)
  - Tribunal decides jurisdiction with the merits? No s 10(3); set aside (Art 34) or resist enforcement → [0-65](#drp-m-0-65)
  - Not participating at all? Can still set aside or resist on jurisdiction (*Rakna*), but risky → [0-66](#drp-m-0-66)
  - Boycott after losing the preliminary ruling? Recourse unclear; risky → [0-67](#drp-m-0-67)
  - Sue in court instead? Other side may stay or waive by defending → [0-68](#drp-m-0-68)
- **Non-participant complains the tribunal ignored a point?** Cannot raise infra petita (*DEM v DEL*) → [0-69](#drp-m-0-69)

### drp-m-0-63 — Participate, object and ask for a preliminary ruling
*Keywords: participate and object, preliminary ruling, s 10(3) application, 30 days, competence-competence, two stages of court review, ARB-CS2*

**End point:** The safest course is to take part, object no later than the statement of defence, and ask for jurisdiction to be decided as a preliminary issue. Under the Model Law a positive ruling is reviewable at two stages: after a preliminary ruling (s 10(3) IAA, 30 days), and after the final award (setting aside, or resisting enforcement).

*Source note: ARB-SN p29 paras 97–99; ARB-CS2 p2 Q3(b)–(d)*

### drp-m-0-64 — Not using s 10(3): no later setting aside on the same ground
*Keywords: failure to invoke s 10(3), loses right to set aside, same jurisdictional objection, First Media obiter, still resist enforcement, Reliance Infrastructure*

**End point:** It has been said obiter that a party who does not apply under s 10(3) after a preliminary positive ruling loses the right to set aside the eventual award on the same jurisdictional objection (*PT First Media v Astro* [2013] SGCA 57 at [130], [132], a tentative view). Subject to any waiver, the award debtor keeps its right to resist enforcement wherever enforcement is sought ([132]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p29 para 99(a); [2013] SGCA 57 at [130], [132]; PYP23 p12 Q19 (cand A, "loses all recourse"; unofficial); ARB-CS2 p2 Q3(e). Conflict: ARB-SN also cites Reliance Infrastructure v Shanghai Electric [2024] SGCA(I) 10 at [71]–[72]; those paragraphs concern waiver of a forgery objection before the tribunal, not s 10(3), so only First Media is relied on.*

### drp-m-0-65 — Jurisdiction decided with the merits: set aside or resist enforcement
*Keywords: jurisdiction decided with merits, s 10(3) not triggered, set aside Art 34(2)(a)(i), Art 34(2)(a)(iii), resist enforcement, s 31(2)(b)*

**End point:** If the tribunal does not decide jurisdiction as a stand-alone preliminary issue and upholds it in an award that also decides the merits, s 10(3) is not triggered. The objecting party may apply to set aside the award (Art 34(2)(a)(i), (iii)) and/or resist enforcement wherever it is sought (IAA s 31(2)(b), (d); NYC Art V(1)(a), (c)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p30 para 99(b); ARB-CS2 p2 Q3(c)*

### drp-m-0-66 — Not participating at all (Rakna)
*Keywords: non-participation, refuse to participate, Rakna Arakshaka Lanka v Avant Garde, set aside on jurisdiction, resist enforcement, risk, default award*

**End point:** A respondent who denies jurisdiction may refuse to take part, then either seek to set aside the award at the seat for want of jurisdiction or resist enforcement on that ground. The Court of Appeal held that a non-participant is not precluded from doing so (*Rakna Arakshaka Lanka v Avant Garde Maritime Services* [2019] SGCA 33 at [54]–[68], reversing the High Court). It is risky: if the plea fails, the party is bound by an award made without its case being argued. The tribunal may proceed and make the award on the evidence (Art 25(b)–(c)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p30 para 100; [2019] SGCA 33 at [54]–[68]; ARB-CS2 p2 Q3(f)*

### drp-m-0-67 — Boycotting after an adverse preliminary ruling
*Keywords: boycott arbitration, after preliminary ruling, recourse unclear, risky, s 10(3), natural justice challenge*

**End point:** A party may take part, ask for a preliminary ruling and, if it loses, boycott the rest. This is risky: case law is unclear what recourse a boycotting party has against the eventual award. It should at least use s 10(3) within 30 days. Non-participation also limits later natural justice complaints (see 0-69).

*Source note: ARB-SN p30 para 101; ARB-CS2 p2 Q3(g)*

### drp-m-0-68 — Going to court instead; the other side may stay or waive
*Keywords: commence litigation, stay application, waiver of arbitration agreement, defending on the merits, submit to court jurisdiction*

**End point:** A party may sue in court instead. The other side will usually apply for a stay (0.VII). If it instead defends on the merits without objecting to the court's jurisdiction, it accepts the court's competence and waives the arbitration agreement for that dispute. A step in the proceedings bars a stay under IAA s 6(1).

*Source note: ARB-SN p30 para 102; IAA s 6(1)*

### drp-m-0-69 — Non-participant cannot raise an infra petita challenge (DEM v DEL)
*Keywords: DEM v DEL, non-participating party, infra petita, failure to consider, natural justice, chose not to participate*

**End point:** A party that had notice of the arbitration but chose not to take part cannot complain that the arbitrator failed to consider a point it never raised. An infra petita ground is not open to a non-participating party (*DEM v DEL* [2025] SGCA 1 at [3], [54]–[58]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p75 para 235; [2025] SGCA 1 at [3], [54]–[58] (also reported [2025] 1 SLR 29)*

## 0.X Constitution of the tribunal

- **Clause silent on the number of arbitrators?** Model Law: 3 (Art 10(2)); IAA: 1 (s 9); AA: 1 (s 12); SIAC: sole unless the Registrar decides three (r 19.1) → [0-70](#drp-m-0-70)
- **Clause says three, but the institution appoints a sole arbitrator (expedited / EA)?** Valid: the parties agreed to the rules (*AQZ v ARA*) → [0-71](#drp-m-0-71)
- **No agreed appointment procedure?** Three arbitrators: each party one, then both agree the third within 30 days, else appointing authority (s 9A); 3+ parties (s 9B); sole: appointing authority (Art 11(3)(b)) → [0-72](#drp-m-0-72)
- **Who is the appointing authority? Which court?** President of the SIAC Court for Art 11(3)–(4); General Division of the High Court for other Art 6 functions (s 8) → [0-73](#drp-m-0-73)
- **SIAC timelines?** Sole: joint nomination in 21 days; three: claimant 14 days, respondent 14 days after; President confirms every nomination; neutral nationality (rr 19–22) → [0-74](#drp-m-0-74)
- **Must the arbitrator disclose?** Yes, continuing duty: circumstances likely to give rise to justifiable doubts (Art 12(1); SIAC r 20) → [0-75](#drp-m-0-75)
- **Can the arbitrator / SIAC be sued?** No for negligence or mistakes (s 25); appointing authority immune unless bad faith (s 25A); SIAC r 65 → [0-76](#drp-m-0-76)

### drp-m-0-70 — Number of arbitrators by default
*Keywords: number of arbitrators, Art 10(2), three arbitrators, IAA s 9, single arbitrator, AA s 12, SIAC r 19.1, sole arbitrator default, Registrar*

**End point:** The parties may fix the number (Art 10(1)). Failing agreement the defaults differ:
- Model Law: three (Art 10(2));
- IAA: a single arbitrator (s 9);
- AA: a single arbitrator (s 12(2));
- SIAC25: a sole arbitrator unless the Registrar decides the dispute warrants three (r 19.1).

*Source note: MAL Art 10; IAA s 9; AA s 12; SIAC25 r 19.1; ARB-SN p36 para 120; SampleQs p56 Q3 (key p122: A, parties free to determine the number); PYP23 p2 Q9*

### drp-m-0-71 — Institutional rules can override an agreed three-member tribunal (AQZ v ARA)
*Keywords: AQZ v ARA, expedited procedure, sole arbitrator despite three-arbitrator clause, SIAC President discretion, emergency arbitrator, institutional rules prevail*

**End point:** Where the parties agree to institutional rules that let the institution appoint a sole arbitrator (expedited procedure, emergency arbitrator), that appointment is valid even though the clause provides for three. By adopting the rules the parties agreed to that mechanism. In *AQZ v ARA* [2015] SGHC 49 at [128]–[137] the SIAC President appointed a sole arbitrator under the 2010 Expedited Procedure despite a three-arbitrator clause, and the appointment was upheld. Under SIAC25 the Expedited Procedure has a sole arbitrator unless the President decides otherwise (Sch 3 para 1), and the Streamlined Procedure always has one (Sch 2 para 1).

*Source note: ARB-SN p36 para 120; [2015] SGHC 49 at [128]–[137]; SIAC25 Sch 2 para 1, Sch 3 para 1*

### drp-m-0-72 — Default appointment procedure (ss 9A, 9B IAA; Art 11)
*Keywords: appointment of arbitrators, default procedure, IAA s 9A, two parties three arbitrators, 30 days, third arbitrator, s 9B, three or more parties, 60 days, Art 11(3)(b), sole arbitrator, appointing authority*

**End point:** Parties may agree the procedure (Art 11(2)); institutional rules usually supply it. Failing agreement, the IAA defaults apply (box).

> **IAA defaults**
> - s 9A (2 parties, 3 arbitrators): each party appoints one; the parties agree the third; if they fail within 30 days of the first request, the appointing authority appoints on a party's request.
> - s 9B (3 or more parties, 3 arbitrators): the claimants appoint one with the request; the respondents appoint one within 30 days; those two appoint the presiding arbitrator within 60 days. On a side's default the appointing authority appoints all three (s 9B(2)); if the two fail to agree, it appoints the third (s 9B(4)).
> - Sole arbitrator not agreed: appointed by the appointing authority (Art 11(3)(b), read with s 8(2)).

*Source note: IAA ss 9A, 9B (Act 32 of 2020); MAL Art 11; ARB-SN p36–37 paras 121–122*

### drp-m-0-73 — Appointing authority and competent court (s 8 IAA)
*Keywords: appointing authority, IAA s 8(1), s 8(2), President of the Court of Arbitration of SIAC, Art 11(3), Art 11(4), General Division of the High Court, Art 6 functions, Chief Justice may appoint another person*

**End point:** The President of the SIAC Court is the authority competent to perform the functions under Model Law Art 11(3)–(4), i.e. default appointments (IAA s 8(2)). The Chief Justice may appoint another person (s 8(3)). The General Division of the High Court performs the other Art 6 functions: challenges (Art 13(3)), termination of mandate (Art 14), jurisdiction (Art 16(3)) and setting aside (Art 34) (s 8(1)).

*Source note: IAA s 8; ARB-SN p37 para 122. Conflict: CYR-ARB was unsure whether the High Court or the SIAC President is the default appointer for a sole arbitrator; s 8(2) makes it the SIAC President.*

### drp-m-0-74 — Appointment under the SIAC Rules 2025
*Keywords: SIAC r 19, r 21, r 22, nomination, President appoints, 21 days sole arbitrator, 14 days three arbitrators, presiding arbitrator, different nationality, agreed qualifications, availability*

**End point:** Under SIAC25 every nomination, by a party, the co-arbitrators or a third person, is subject to appointment by the President, who may refuse after considering views (r 19.2–19.4). The President considers agreed qualifications, impartiality and independence, and availability (r 19.5–19.6). A sole or presiding arbitrator is of a different nationality from the parties unless agreed or the President decides otherwise (r 19.7).

> **SIAC25 timelines**
> - r 21 (sole arbitrator): joint nomination within 21 days of commencement; failing that, the President appoints.
> - r 22.1 (three arbitrators): the Claimant nominates within 14 days of commencement; the Respondent within 14 days of receiving the Claimant's nomination.
> - r 22.2–22.3: the President appoints on default and appoints the presiding arbitrator unless another procedure is agreed.

*Source note: SIAC25 rr 19, 21, 22*

### drp-m-0-75 — Arbitrator's duty to disclose (Art 12(1); SIAC r 20)
*Keywords: duty of disclosure, Art 12(1), justifiable doubts, impartiality, independence, continuing duty, SIAC r 20, Orange List disclosure*

**End point:** A prospective arbitrator must disclose any circumstances likely to give rise to justifiable doubts as to their impartiality or independence. The duty continues throughout the arbitration (Art 12(1); SIAC25 r 20.2–20.3, to the Registrar before appointment and to everyone after). The IBA Orange List shows what to disclose; Green List matters need no disclosure.

*Source note: MAL Art 12(1); ARB-SN p37 para 124; SIAC25 r 20.2–20.3; IBA-COI Part II paras 3, 7*

### drp-m-0-76 — Immunity of arbitrators and institutions (ss 25, 25A)
*Keywords: immunity, arbitrator not liable, negligence, mistake in law fact or procedure, IAA s 25, s 25A, appointing authority, institution, bad faith, SIAC r 65 exclusion of liability*

**End point:** An arbitrator is not liable for negligence in anything done or omitted as arbitrator, or for any mistake in law, fact or procedure in the proceedings or the award (IAA s 25). An appointing authority or institution is not liable for its appointment function unless it acted in bad faith, nor merely because it appointed the arbitrator (s 25A). Institutional rules add exclusions of liability (SIAC25 r 65.1).

*Source note: IAA ss 25, 25A; ARB-SN p45 paras 147–148; SIAC25 r 65.1*

## 0.XI Challenging an arbitrator

- **On what grounds?** Justifiable doubts as to impartiality or independence, or lacks agreed qualifications; own appointee only for reasons learnt later (Art 12(2); SIAC r 26) → [0-77](#drp-m-0-77)
- **What test for bias?** Reasonable suspicion: would a fair-minded, informed observer reasonably suspect bias (possibility, not probability) (*BYL v BYN*; *PT Central Investindo*) → [0-78](#drp-m-0-78)
- **Kinds of bias?** Actual; imputed (own cause, pecuniary interest = automatic); apparent (reasonable suspicion) → [0-79](#drp-m-0-79)
- **Arbitrator corresponded privately with one party?** Only in very exceptional circumstances (*Turner*) → [0-80](#drp-m-0-80)
- **IBA lists: which list is it?**
  - Arbitrator is a party, its director, has a significant interest, or regularly advises it for significant income → Non-Waivable Red → [0-81](#drp-m-0-81)
  - Advised on the dispute; holds shares in a private party; same firm as counsel; firm has a significant commercial relationship; close family interest → Waivable Red (express waiver) → [0-82](#drp-m-0-82)
  - Counsel for or against a party in the past 3 years; repeat appointments; same chambers; close friendship or enmity with counsel; publicly advocated a position → Orange (disclose) → [0-83](#drp-m-0-83)
  - Prior general legal opinion; same professional association or social media; previously co-arbitrators; insignificant listed shares; initial contact on availability → Green (no disclosure) → [0-84](#drp-m-0-84)
- **Procedure and time limits?**
  - Model Law: written reasons to the tribunal within 15 days; tribunal decides; court within 30 days, no appeal; tribunal may continue (Art 13) → [0-85](#drp-m-0-85)
  - SIAC: notice within 15 days to the Registrar; SIAC Court decides, final (rr 27–28) → [0-86](#drp-m-0-86)
- **Arbitrator ill / not acting?** Art 14: mandate ends on withdrawal or agreement, else court (no time limit); substitute (Art 15) → [0-87](#drp-m-0-87)
- **Bias found only after the award?** Set aside (Art 34(2)(a)(iv); s 24(b)) within 3 months, or resist enforcement (s 31(2)(e)); waiver if not raised promptly (Art 4) → [0-88](#drp-m-0-88)

### drp-m-0-77 — Grounds for challenge (Art 12(2))
*Keywords: challenge of arbitrator, grounds, Art 12(2), justifiable doubts, impartiality, independence, qualifications agreed, own appointee, reasons become aware after appointment, SIAC r 26.1, de jure or de facto unable*

**End point:** An arbitrator may be challenged only if circumstances give rise to justifiable doubts as to their impartiality or independence, or they lack qualifications the parties agreed (Art 12(2)). A party may challenge an arbitrator it appointed, or helped appoint, only for reasons it learns of after the appointment. SIAC25 r 26.1 adds inability to perform; r 26.2 has the same own-nominee limit. Object as soon as the grounds are known, or risk waiver (Art 4). Approach: (1) identify the ground (reasonable suspicion; IBA lists); (2) follow the challenge procedure in the rules or lex arbitri.

*Source note: MAL Art 12(2); ARB-SN p37 paras 123–125; SIAC25 r 26; PYP21-ARB Q2 (arbitrator lacking an agreed "Fine Arts degree")*

### drp-m-0-78 — Test: reasonable suspicion of bias
*Keywords: reasonable suspicion test, apparent bias, fair-minded reasonable observer, informed, possibility not probability, BYL v BYN, PT Central Investindo, Re Shankar Alan, Art 13(3), s 24(b), objective test*

**End point:** The test is reasonable suspicion: would a fair-minded, reasonable person with knowledge of the relevant facts reasonably suspect that the tribunal may be biased, so that a fair hearing may not be possible? (*BYL v BYN* [2020] SGHC(I) 6 at [50]; *PT Central Investindo v Franciscus Wongso* [2014] SGHC 190 at [14], [18]; *Re Shankar Alan* [2006] SGHC 194 at [76], [78].) The same test applies to removing an arbitrator under Art 13(3) and setting aside for apparent bias under s 24(b) and Art 34(2). Once justifiable doubts are found, actual bias need not be proved.

> **The hypothetical observer (BYL v BYN at [51])**
> - a lay person informed of the relevant facts and able to see them in context;
> - not wholly uninformed about the law and the issues;
> - aware of the traditions of integrity and impartiality of adjudicators;
> - fair-minded: neither complacent nor unduly sensitive or suspicious.
> - Standard: whether bias is *possible* (not probable). The belief must not be fanciful and must be capable of articulation by reference to the evidence.

*Source note: ARB-SN p38–39 paras 126, 129, 132–135; [2020] SGHC(I) 6 at [50]–[51]; [2014] SGHC 190 at [14], [18]; [2006] SGHC 194 at [76], [78]. Conflict: NIC-ARB describes the observer as "without knowledge of all relevant facts"; ARB-SN and BYL say with knowledge of the relevant facts, which is followed.*

### drp-m-0-79 — Actual, imputed and apparent bias
*Keywords: actual bias, imputed bias, nemo judex in sua causa, pecuniary interest, proprietary interest, automatic disqualification, apparent bias, reasonable suspicion*

**End point:** Singapore courts recognise three forms of bias (*PT Central Investindo* [2014] SGHC 190 at [15]):
- **actual bias**, which obviously disqualifies;
- **imputed bias**, where the arbitrator is in effect a judge in their own cause, e.g. a pecuniary or proprietary interest. Disqualification is automatic, with no inquiry into likelihood or suspicion;
- **apparent bias**, tested by reasonable suspicion (0-78).

*Source note: ARB-SN p38–39 paras 130–132; [2014] SGHC 190 at [15]*

### drp-m-0-80 — Private contact with one party
*Keywords: private contact, ex parte communication, correspondence with one party, Turner (East Asia) v Builders Federal, very exceptional circumstances, impartiality*

**End point:** An arbitrator's private contact or personal correspondence with one party can found a challenge. There is no absolute rule against an arbitrator corresponding directly with the parties, but it should happen only in very exceptional circumstances (*Turner (East Asia) v Builders Federal (Hong Kong)* [1988] SGHC 47 at [135]). Initial contact limited to availability and qualifications is on the Green List (IBA-COI 4.4.1).

*Source note: ARB-SN p38 para 127; [1988] SGHC 47 at [135]; IBA-COI 4.4.1. Conflict: ARB-SN cites Turner at [93], which is narrative; the statement is at [135].*

### drp-m-0-81 — IBA Non-Waivable Red List
*Keywords: Non-Waivable Red List, identity with party, legal representative in the arbitration, employee of party, director, controlling influence, significant financial interest, regularly advises party, significant financial income, IBA 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4*

**End point:** Non-Waivable Red List situations come from the principle that no one may be their own judge, so even the parties' acceptance cannot cure them.

> **IBA-COI 2024, Non-Waivable Red List**
> - 1.1 identity between a party and the arbitrator; or the arbitrator is a legal representative in the arbitration, or an employee of a party;
> - 1.2 the arbitrator is a manager, director or supervisory board member of, or has a controlling influence on, a party or an entity with a direct economic interest in the award;
> - 1.3 the arbitrator has a significant financial or personal interest in a party or the outcome;
> - 1.4 the arbitrator currently or regularly advises a party or its affiliate, and the arbitrator or their firm or employer derives significant financial income from it.

*Source note: IBA-COI Part II para 2 and list (1) (p14–15); ARB-SN p40 para 137. Conflict: ARB-SN quotes the 2014 wording ("legal representative or employee of an entity that is a party"); the 2024 text above is followed.*

### drp-m-0-82 — IBA Waivable Red List
*Keywords: Waivable Red List, express waiver, advised on the dispute, prior involvement, shares in privately held party, close family member interest, currently represents party, same law firm as counsel, significant commercial relationship, close family relationship, IBA 2.1, 2.2, 2.3*

**End point:** Waivable Red List situations are serious. They are cured only if the parties, aware of the conflict, expressly agree to the person acting.

> **IBA-COI 2024, Waivable Red List**
> - 2.1 relationship to the dispute: 2.1.1 gave legal advice or an expert opinion on the dispute to a party or affiliate; 2.1.2 prior involvement in the dispute.
> - 2.2 interest in the dispute: 2.2.1 holds shares in a privately held party or affiliate; 2.2.2 a close family member has a significant economic interest in the outcome; 2.2.3 the arbitrator or a close family member has a close relationship with a non-party liable to recourse from the loser.
> - 2.3 relationship with parties or counsel: 2.3.1 currently or regularly represents or advises a party or affiliate, without significant income; 2.3.2 currently represents or advises counsel's firm; 2.3.3 same law firm as a party's counsel; 2.3.4 director etc. of an affiliate directly involved in the dispute; 2.3.5 the arbitrator's firm had a terminated involvement in the case without them; 2.3.6 the arbitrator's firm has a significant commercial relationship with a party or affiliate; 2.3.7 close family relationship with a party, its management or controller, or counsel; 2.3.8 a close family member has a significant financial or personal interest in a party or affiliate.

*Source note: IBA-COI list (2) (p15–16); ARB-SN p40–41 para 138. Conflict: ARB-SN numbers the close-family-interest item 2.3.9 (2014 Guidelines); in 2024 it is 2.3.8.*

### drp-m-0-83 — IBA Orange List
*Keywords: Orange List, duty to disclose, deemed acceptance, no timely objection, past three years counsel, appointed two or more times, same barristers' chambers, close personal friendship with counsel, enmity, publicly advocated position, material shareholding listed company, IBA 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4*

**End point:** Orange List situations may, in the parties' eyes, give rise to doubts. The arbitrator must disclose them, and the parties are deemed to accept the arbitrator if they make no timely objection after disclosure.

> **IBA-COI 2024, Orange List (main items)**
> - 3.1 services or involvement: 3.1.1 counsel for a party (or advised the appointing party) within 3 years, no ongoing relationship; 3.1.2 counsel against a party within 3 years; 3.1.3 appointed arbitrator by a party 2+ times in 3 years; 3.1.4 mock trials for a party 2+ times; 3.1.5 arbitrator or counsel in a related matter involving a party; 3.1.6 expert for a party within 3 years; 3.1.7 the arbitrator's firm renders services to a party without a significant relationship or the arbitrator's involvement; 3.1.8 fee-sharing firm acts for a party.
> - 3.2 with another arbitrator or counsel: 3.2.1 same firm or employer as another arbitrator; 3.2.2 same barristers' chambers as another arbitrator or counsel; 3.2.3 partner or affiliate of an arbitrator or counsel within 3 years; 3.2.5 close family member in counsel's firm, not on the case; 3.2.6 close personal friendship with counsel; 3.2.7 enmity with counsel; 3.2.8 appointed 3+ times by the same counsel or firm in 3 years; 3.2.11 co-counsel within 3 years; 3.2.12–3.2.13 currently sitting together in another arbitration.
> - 3.3 with a party or others: 3.3.1 the arbitrator's firm is acting adversely to a party; 3.3.3 close friendship, or 3.3.4 enmity, with a party's manager or director or a witness or expert; 3.3.5 former judge who heard a significant case involving a party within 3 years.
> - 3.4 other: 3.4.1 material shareholding in a listed party; 3.4.2 publicly advocated a position on the case, including on social media; 3.4.3 decision-making role in the administering institution on this dispute.

*Source note: IBA-COI list (3) (p16–18); ARB-SN p41–42 para 139. Conflict: ARB-SN uses 2014 numbers (e.g. firm currently rendering services "3.2.1", same chambers "3.3.2", friendship "3.3.6", enmity "3.3.7", publicly advocated "3.5.2"); the 2024 numbers are 3.1.7, 3.2.2, 3.2.6, 3.2.7 and 3.4.2.*

### drp-m-0-84 — IBA Green List
*Keywords: Green List, no duty to disclose, previously expressed legal opinion, law review article, associated firm no fee sharing, professional association, social media network, previously served together as arbitrators, same faculty, conference speaker, initial contact availability, insignificant shares listed, IBA 4.1 to 4.5*

**End point:** Green List situations involve no appearance of, and no actual, conflict, so there is no duty to disclose.

> **IBA-COI 2024, Green List**
> - 4.1.1 previously expressed a legal opinion (e.g. an article or lecture) on an issue in the case, not focused on the case;
> - 4.2.1 an allied firm that does not share significant fees acts for a party in an unrelated matter;
> - 4.3.1 relationship with another arbitrator or counsel through a professional association, social or charitable organisation, or social media;
> - 4.3.2 previously served as co-arbitrators with counsel;
> - 4.3.3 teaches at the same faculty, or is an officer of the same association;
> - 4.3.4 speaker, moderator or organiser at conferences or working parties with them;
> - 4.4.1 initial contact with a party limited to availability, qualifications or names of chair candidates, not the merits or procedure;
> - 4.4.2 insignificant shares in a listed party;
> - 4.4.3 worked with a party's manager as joint experts or in another professional capacity;
> - 4.4.4 social media relationship with a party;
> - 4.5.1 heard testimony from the same expert in another matter.

*Source note: IBA-COI list (4) (p18); ARB-SN p42–43 para 140*

### drp-m-0-85 — Challenge procedure under the Model Law (Art 13)
*Keywords: challenge procedure, Art 13, 15 days, written statement of reasons, tribunal decides, 30 days, court, no appeal, tribunal may continue, Art 13(3), originating application, O 48 r 2*

**End point:** Parties may agree a procedure (Art 13(1)), and institutional rules usually supply one. Otherwise:
- the challenger sends written reasons to the tribunal within 15 days of becoming aware of the circumstances;
- unless the arbitrator withdraws or the other party agrees, the tribunal decides (Art 13(2));
- if the challenge fails, the challenger may within 30 days ask the court to decide, with no appeal; the tribunal may continue and make an award meanwhile (Art 13(3)).
In Singapore the Art 13(3) application goes to a Judge by originating application within 30 days (ROC O 48 r 2(1)(a), (2)). Possible outcomes are that the arbitrator resigns (not an admission), the other party agrees, or the challenge is decided.

*Source note: MAL Art 13; ARB-SN p43 paras 141–142; ROC O 48 r 2*

### drp-m-0-86 — Challenge procedure under the SIAC Rules 2025
*Keywords: SIAC challenge, notice of challenge, 15 days, Registrar, SIAC Court decides, reasoned, final, r 27, r 28, suspension, 7 days agree or withdraw, emergency arbitrator 24 hours, streamlined 3 days*

**End point:** Under SIAC25 a notice of challenge goes to the Registrar within 15 days of receiving notice of appointment, or of when the reasons became known or should reasonably have been known (r 27.1). The Registrar may suspend the arbitration; otherwise the arbitrator continues (r 27.4). If within 7 days the other parties agree or the arbitrator withdraws, a substitute is appointed, without implying the grounds were valid (r 27.5). Otherwise the SIAC Court decides with reasons, and its decision is final under the Rules (r 28). The time limit is 24 hours for an emergency arbitrator (Sch 1 para 9) and 3 days under the Streamlined Procedure (Sch 2 para 5). A court challenge under Art 13(3) may still follow.

*Source note: SIAC25 rr 26–28, Sch 1 para 9, Sch 2 para 5*

### drp-m-0-87 — Failure or inability to act (Art 14); substitute (Art 15)
*Keywords: failure to act, inability to perform, de jure, de facto, undue delay, Art 14, mandate terminates, court decides, no time limit, no appeal, Art 15, substitute arbitrator*

**End point:** If an arbitrator becomes de jure or de facto unable to act, or fails to act without undue delay, the mandate ends if they withdraw or the parties agree. Otherwise any party may ask the court to decide, with no appeal (Art 14(1)). Withdrawal or agreement does not imply the ground was valid (Art 14(2)). Unlike Art 13, Art 14 goes directly to court and has no time limit. A substitute is appointed under the rules that applied to the original appointment (Art 15).

*Source note: MAL Arts 14–15; ARB-SN p44 paras 143–144; ROC O 48 r 2(1)(b)*

### drp-m-0-88 — Arbitrator partiality after the award
*Keywords: set aside for bias, Art 34(2)(a)(iv), composition of tribunal, s 24(b) natural justice, 3 months, waiver Art 4, resist enforcement, s 31(2)(e), Art V(1)(d)*

**End point:** An award may be challenged for the arbitrator's lack of independence:
- by setting aside, under Art 34(2)(a)(iv) (composition not in accordance with the agreement) or IAA s 24(b) (natural justice), within 3 months of receipt (Art 34(3));
- by resisting enforcement, on the same footing (IAA s 31(2)(e); NYC Art V(1)(d)).
A party that did not challenge the arbitrator without undue delay after learning the facts may be deemed to have waived the objection (Art 4).

*Source note: ARB-SN p44 paras 145–146; IAA ss 24(b), 31(2)(e); MAL Arts 4, 34(2)(a)(iv), 34(3)*

## 0.XII Arb-Med, Arb-Med-Arb and multi-tier clauses

- **Can the arbitrator also mediate?** Yes, with all parties' written consent; confidentiality; before resuming, disclose material confidential information; no later objection on that ground alone (s 17 IAA); agreed conciliator-then-arbitrator (s 16(3)) → [0-89](#drp-m-0-89)
- **SIAC-SIMC Arb-Med-Arb?** Arbitrate → stay after Notice and Response → SIMC mediation within 8 weeks (separate mediator) → settlement recorded as consent award, enforceable under the NYC; else arbitration resumes → [0-90](#drp-m-0-90)
- **Settlement during the arbitration?** Consent award (Art 30; IAA s 18) → [0-91](#drp-m-0-91)
- **"Unless settled amicably, any dispute shall be finally settled by arbitration": is negotiation a condition precedent?** No: too uncertain (*CZQ v CZS*) → [0-92](#drp-m-0-92)
- **Clause says parties "shall refer the dispute for mediation at SMC", but the other side goes straight to arbitration?** Specific performance of the duty to mediate (*Maxx Engineering*) → [0-93](#drp-m-0-93)
- **Defendant refuses to take part in the mediation tier, then seeks a stay for arbitration?** Repudiatory breach accepted → med-arb clause inoperative, stay refused (*Heartronics*) → [0-94](#drp-m-0-94)

### drp-m-0-89 — Arbitrator acting as conciliator (ss 16–17 IAA)
*Keywords: Arb-Med, arbitrator as mediator, conciliator, IAA s 17, written consent, confidential information, disclosure before resuming, s 17(3), no objection s 17(4), s 16(3), conciliation fails then arbitration, 4 months s 16(4), double-hatting*

**End point:** If all parties consent in writing, and while none withdraws, an arbitrator may act as conciliator. "Conciliator" includes a mediator (IAA s 16(5)), and the rules are in s 17.

> **IAA s 17 (and s 16)**
> - s 17(1): consent in writing by all parties; lasts while no party withdraws consent.
> - s 17(2): may communicate with the parties collectively or separately; must keep a party's information confidential unless that party agrees or s 17(3) applies.
> - s 17(3): if conciliation fails, before resuming the arbitration the arbitrator must disclose to the other parties as much of the confidential information as they consider material.
> - s 17(4): no objection to the arbitrator's conduct of the arbitration solely because they acted as conciliator.
> - s 16(3): where the clause provides for a conciliator who becomes arbitrator if conciliation fails, no objection on that ground alone.
> - s 16(4): conciliation is deemed to end after 4 months (or longer agreed period) without settlement.

Double-hatting is common in China but raises impartiality concerns, which is why the SIAC-SIMC protocol uses a separate mediator (Prague Rules Art 9 is similar).

*Source note: IAA ss 16–17; ARB-SN p45 paras 149–151; CYR-EXAM 4.6.1*

### drp-m-0-90 — SIAC-SIMC Arb-Med-Arb Protocol
*Keywords: Arb-Med-Arb, AMA Protocol, SIAC-SIMC, SIMC mediation, 8 weeks, stay of arbitration, separate mediator, consent award, New York Convention, AMA clause, Mediation Commencement Date, 4 working days*

**End point:** Under the AMA Protocol the dispute is first referred to SIAC arbitration. The steps run as follows:
- after the Notice and Response, the tribunal stays the arbitration and the case goes to SIMC for mediation by a separate mediator;
- the mediation is to finish within 8 weeks of the Mediation Commencement Date unless the SIAC Registrar extends it, and arbitral time periods stop running meanwhile;
- if the parties settle, the tribunal may record the settlement as a consent award, generally enforceable as an arbitral award under the NYC (over 170 countries);
- if they do not, the arbitration resumes.
SIAC informs SIMC within 4 working days. Use the AMA model clause. SIAC25 r 6.4(a) lets the Notice comment on mediation under the AMA Protocol.

*Source note: SIAC25 pp85–87 (AMA Protocol), r 6.4(a), r 7.3; ARB-SN p45–46 paras 151–153; MED-2 p5 (SIMC administers Arb-Med-Arb with SIAC)*

### drp-m-0-91 — Consent award (Art 30; s 18 IAA)
*Keywords: consent award, award on agreed terms, Art 30, IAA s 18, settlement during arbitration, enforce with permission, judgment in terms*

**End point:** If the parties settle during the arbitration, the tribunal terminates the proceedings and, if asked and it does not object, records the settlement as an award on agreed terms with the same status as any award (Art 30). Under IAA s 18 such an award is treated as an award on an arbitration agreement. It may be enforced with the High Court's permission like a judgment, and judgment may be entered in its terms. Reasons are not needed (SIAC25 r 52.3).

*Source note: MAL Art 30; IAA s 18; ARB-SN p64 para 202; SIAC25 r 52.3*

### drp-m-0-92 — "Unless settled amicably": too uncertain to be a condition (CZQ v CZS)
*Keywords: CZQ v CZS, unless settled amicably, condition precedent, uncertain, escalation clause, multi-tier clause, SICC*

**End point:** A clause opening "[u]nless settled amicably, any dispute shall be finally settled by international arbitration" did not create a legally binding condition precedent. "Settled amicably" was undefined and too uncertain, so arbitration could start without a prior negotiation step (*CZQ v CZS* [2023] SGHC(I) 16 at [13], [20], [22]). To make a pre-arbitration step binding, define it: what, with whom, and for how long.

*Source note: ARB-SN p46 para 156; [2023] SGHC(I) 16 at [13], [20], [22]. Conflict: ARB-SN cites "CZQ v CZS [2024] 3 SLR 111" as an Appellate Division decision; it is a Singapore International Commercial Court decision, [2023] SGHC(I) 16 (reported [2024] 3 SLR 111).*

### drp-m-0-93 — Specific performance of an obligation to mediate (Maxx Engineering)
*Keywords: Maxx Engineering v PQ Builders, specific performance, obligation to mediate, shall refer to mediation, SMC, not a condition precedent, damages inadequate, not futile, not impractical*

**End point:** In *Maxx Engineering Works v PQ Builders* [2023] SGHC 71 the clause said the parties "shall endeavour to resolve the dispute through negotiations" and, if negotiations failed, "shall refer the dispute for mediation at the [SMC]". The clause added that mediation was not a condition precedent to arbitration. The High Court held that the clause still imposed a legal obligation to mediate. It ordered specific performance compelling the respondent to refer the dispute to mediation, on four grounds (box). The parties' choice to mediate should be respected (MED-3 p41).

> **Why specific performance was just (ARB-SN para 159)**
> - (a) damages would not be an adequate remedy;
> - (b) no substantial hardship to the respondent;
> - (c) not futile: no evidence mediation would fail;
> - (d) not impractical: compliance (concrete steps to refer to mediation) is easy to assess.

*Source note: ARB-SN p46–47 paras 157–159; [2023] SGHC 71; MED-3 p41*

### drp-m-0-94 — Repudiating the mediation tier: stay refused (Heartronics)
*Keywords: Heartronics v EPI Life, med-arb clause, repudiatory breach, unitary dispute resolution mechanism, refused to mediate, unpaid mediation fees, arbitration agreement inoperative, stay refused*

**End point:** In *Heartronics Corp v EPI Life* [2017] SGHCR 17 the mediation and arbitration tiers formed one unitary dispute resolution mechanism ([78]–[80]). The first defendant delayed, failed to pay the mediation fees and pleaded unsubstantiated cash-flow problems. It thereby showed it had no intention of performing its obligation to mediate, which was a repudiatory breach ([168]–[169]). The plaintiff accepted the repudiation, so the med-arb clause, including the arbitration tier, was inoperative and the stay was refused ([214]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p46 para 155; MED-3 p10; [2017] SGHCR 17 at [78]–[80], [168]–[169], [214]. Conflict: MED-3 p10 cites [71], which records the defendant's argument; the holdings are at [78]–[80] and [168]–[169].*

## 0.XIII Conduct of the proceedings and evidence

- **Who decides procedure? Does the Evidence Act apply?** Parties' agreement (e.g. rules); otherwise the tribunal, incl. admissibility and weight (Art 19); domestic evidence law does not bind unless agreed → [0-95](#drp-m-0-95)
- **Core rights?** Equality and a full opportunity to present one's case (Art 18); oral hearing on request, notice, all materials shared (Art 24) → [0-96](#drp-m-0-96)
- **A party does not file or appear?** Claimant no claim → terminate; respondent no defence → continue, no admission; no appearance → award on the evidence (Art 25) → [0-97](#drp-m-0-97)
- **Documents from the other side?** Tribunal orders discovery (s 12(1)(b)); IBA Rules (0-19) → [0-98](#drp-m-0-98)
- **Witness in Singapore refuses to come?** High Court order to attend / produce (s 13 IAA); first seek the tribunal's directions (*ALC v ALF*) → [0-99](#drp-m-0-99)
- **Is the arbitration confidential?** Yes by default, with exceptions (*AAY v AAZ*; SIAC r 59); court proceedings heard in private (ss 22–23 IAA) unless confidentiality lost (*India v Deutsche Telekom*) → [0-100](#drp-m-0-100)
- **Can a foreign lawyer act in a Singapore-seated arbitration?** Yes (LPA s 35) → [0-101](#drp-m-0-101)
- **When do proceedings end?** Final award; or termination order on withdrawal, agreement, or continuation unnecessary or impossible (Art 32; *DRL v DRK*) → [0-102](#drp-m-0-102)
- **Claim manifestly without merit?** SIAC early dismissal, 45 days (r 47; *DBO v DBP*) → [0-103](#drp-m-0-103)
- **Decide one issue first?** SIAC preliminary determination, 90 days (r 46) → [0-104](#drp-m-0-104)
- **Security for costs from a foreign claimant?** Tribunal may order (s 12(1)(a); SIAC r 48), but not *only* because foreign (s 12(4)) → [0-105](#drp-m-0-105)
- **Third-party funding?** Disclose existence and funder in the Notice or Response (SIAC r 38) → [0-106](#drp-m-0-106)

### drp-m-0-95 — Party autonomy over procedure; Evidence Act does not bind (Art 19)
*Keywords: procedure, party autonomy, Art 19(1), Art 19(2), tribunal discretion, admissibility relevance materiality weight, Evidence Act not binding, inquisitorial processes, s 12(3)*

**End point:** The parties may agree the procedure, e.g. by adopting institutional rules (Art 19(1)). Failing agreement, the tribunal conducts the arbitration as it considers appropriate. That includes deciding the admissibility, relevance, materiality and weight of evidence (Art 19(2)). Domestic evidence statutes, such as Singapore's Evidence Act, do not bind the tribunal unless the parties agree. Unless the parties agree otherwise in writing, the tribunal may administer oaths and adopt inquisitorial processes (IAA s 12(2)–(3)).

*Source note: MAL Art 19; IAA s 12(2)–(3); ARB-SN p48 paras 160–161; PYP23 p7 Q14 (Evidence Act does not apply unless agreed)*

### drp-m-0-96 — Equal treatment; hearings and notice (Arts 18, 24)
*Keywords: equality of parties, full opportunity to present case, Art 18, oral hearing, Art 24(1), sufficient advance notice, Art 24(2), communicate documents to other party, Art 24(3), natural justice*

**End point:** The parties must be treated with equality and each given a full opportunity of presenting its case (Art 18). A party may request an oral hearing (Art 24(1)). Parties get sufficient advance notice of hearings and meetings (Art 24(2)). Everything one party supplies to the tribunal goes to the other (Art 24(3)). Breaching these rights founds natural justice challenges (0-139, 0-142).

*Source note: MAL Arts 18, 24; ARB-SN p48 para 162*

### drp-m-0-97 — Default of a party (Art 25)
*Keywords: default, non-participation, claimant fails to file statement of claim, terminate, respondent fails to file defence, continue without admission, fails to appear, award on evidence, Art 25(a), (b), (c), sufficient cause, SIAC r 44*

**End point:** Unless otherwise agreed, if a party fails without sufficient cause to act, Art 25 applies (box). A default award can be set aside or resisted only on the usual grounds.

> **Model Law Art 25**
> - (a) the claimant fails to communicate its statement of claim → the tribunal terminates the proceedings;
> - (b) the respondent fails to communicate its statement of defence → the tribunal continues, without treating the failure as an admission of the claim;
> - (c) any party fails to appear or produce documents → the tribunal may continue and make the award on the evidence before it.

*Source note: MAL Art 25; ARB-SN p48–49 paras 163–165, p64 para 202*

### drp-m-0-98 — Document production (s 12(1)(b) IAA)
*Keywords: discovery of documents, document production, IAA s 12(1)(b), discovery of facts, tribunal power, Redfern schedule, IBA Rules Art 3*

**End point:** A Singapore-seated tribunal may order a party to give discovery of documents and discovery of facts (IAA s 12(1)(b)). How production works is usually set by the procedural order and, often, the IBA Rules on Evidence: Request to Produce, objections, adverse inferences (0-19). The Prague Rules discourage it (0-23).

*Source note: IAA s 12(1)(b); ARB-SN p49 para 166; IBA-EV Art 3. Conflict: ARB-SN para 179 lists s 12(1)(b) as "discovery of documents and interrogatories"; the current text (Act 32 of 2020) reads "discovery of documents and discovery of facts".*

### drp-m-0-99 — Court orders to attend and produce documents (s 13 IAA; ALC v ALF)
*Keywords: court assistance taking evidence, Art 27, IAA s 13, order to attend, order to produce documents, subpoena, witness in Singapore, ALC v ALF, seek tribunal's directions first, AA s 30*

**End point:** A party may seek court assistance in taking evidence (Art 27). Any party may request an order to attend or an order to produce documents. If the witness is in Singapore, the High Court may order it, but no one can be made to produce a document they could not be compelled to produce at trial (IAA s 13(1)–(2), (4)). In *ALC v ALF* [2010] SGHC 231 at [40], a domestic case under AA s 30, the subpoena was set aside. The applicant should first have sought the tribunal's directions on calling the witness, as Procedural Order No 1 required.

*Source note: MAL Art 27; IAA s 13; ARB-SN p49 paras 167–168; [2010] SGHC 231 at [40]. Conflict: ARB-SN calls the IAA process a "subpoena"; s 13 now provides for an "order to attend" and an "order to produce documents". NIC-ARB says ALC v ALF was under "s 30 IAA"; it was AA s 30, as ARB-SN and CYR-ARB say.*

### drp-m-0-100 — Confidentiality of arbitration and related court proceedings
*Keywords: confidentiality, privacy, AAY v AAZ, implied duty of confidentiality, exceptions, reasonable suspicion of criminal conduct, protection of legitimate legal rights, IAA s 22, s 23, heard in private, India v Deutsche Telekom, open justice, SIAC r 59*

**End point:** In Singapore an obligation of confidentiality applies by default, even where the parties did not provide for it (*AAY v AAZ* [2009] SGHC 142 at [55]). Exceptions include disclosure to the authorities on reasonable suspicion of criminal conduct ([72]) and disclosure reasonably necessary to protect a party's legitimate legal rights. Court proceedings under the IAA are heard in private unless the court orders otherwise, with restrictions on reporting (IAA ss 22–23). Their purpose is to protect the arbitration's confidentiality. Once that is lost, open justice weighs strongly for lifting privacy (*Republic of India v Deutsche Telekom* [2023] SGCA(I) 4 at [23], [27]).

> **SIAC25 r 59.3: disclosure permitted without consent**
> - (a) to challenge or enforce the award;
> - (b) under a court order or subpoena;
> - (c) to pursue or enforce a legal right or claim;
> - (d) to comply with binding law or a regulator;
> - (e) under a tribunal order;
> - (f) for an application under the Rules.

*Source note: ARB-SN p49–50 paras 169–171; [2009] SGHC 142 (reported [2011] 1 SLR 1093) at [55], [72]; IAA ss 22–23; [2023] SGCA(I) 4 at [23], [27]; SIAC25 r 59; PYP23 p13 Q20*

### drp-m-0-101 — Foreign lawyers may act in Singapore-seated arbitrations
*Keywords: foreign lawyers, party representation, LPA s 35, s 32, s 33, unauthorised person, arbitration proceedings, right of audience in court excluded*

**End point:** Sections 32–33 of the Legal Profession Act (unauthorised practice) do not extend to:
- arbitrators;
- persons representing any party in arbitration proceedings;
- giving advice, preparing documents and other assistance in relation to arbitration proceedings.
Rights of audience in court proceedings are excluded from this (LPA s 35(1)). "Arbitration proceedings" means those under the AA or IAA, or that would be if seated in Singapore (s 35(2)). So foreign lawyers may act in Singapore-seated arbitrations.

*Source note: LPA s 35; ARB-SN p50 para 172*

### drp-m-0-102 — Termination of proceedings (Art 32; DRL v DRK)
*Keywords: termination of proceedings, Art 32, final award, termination order, withdrawal of claim, agreement, unnecessary or impossible, Art 32(2)(c), DRL v DRK, security for costs not furnished, sanctions, mandate revives Art 33, Art 34(4)*

**End point:** Proceedings end with the final award (Art 32(1)). The tribunal must order termination in three cases (box). In *DRL v DRK* [2026] SGHC 32 a claimant's failure to provide security for costs over 26 months, despite sanctions and no effort to find a funder, made continuation impossible ([61]). The provision gives no discretion to proceed once impossibility is found ([57]), and its cause is immaterial ([65]). Claimants must guard against limitation themselves ([58]). The mandate revives only for Art 33 requests or Art 34(4) remission (Art 32(3)). The court reviews only the fairness of the process leading to a termination order, not its merits ([38], [53]).

> **Model Law Art 32(2): the tribunal shall order termination when**
> - (a) the claimant withdraws its claim, unless the respondent objects and the tribunal recognises its legitimate interest in a final settlement;
> - (b) the parties agree to terminate;
> - (c) continuing has for any other reason become unnecessary or impossible.

*Source note: MAL Art 32; ARB-SN p51 paras 173–176; [2026] SGHC 32 at [38], [53], [57], [58], [61], [65] (ARB-SN gives "[2026] 4 SLR 1"; the neutral citation is used)*

### drp-m-0-103 — Early dismissal (SIAC r 47; DBO v DBP)
*Keywords: early dismissal, summary dismissal, manifestly without legal merit, manifestly outside jurisdiction, SIAC r 47, 45 days, DBO v DBP, natural justice, case management deference, collateral contract*

**End point:** Under SIAC25 r 47 a party may apply for early dismissal of a claim or defence that is (a) manifestly without legal merit, or (b) manifestly outside the tribunal's jurisdiction. The tribunal first decides whether to let the application proceed. If it does, after hearing the parties it decides within 45 days of filing (extendable), and reasons may be summary. In *DBO v DBP* [2024] SGCA(I) 4 a natural justice challenge to an early-dismissal award failed. The amendment relying on an alleged collateral contract could not defeat the application ([29], [48]), which shows the court's deference to tribunals' case management. Not available under the Streamlined Procedure (Sch 2).

*Source note: SIAC25 r 47, Sch 2; ARB-SN p73 para 232; [2024] SGCA(I) 4 at [29], [48] (the award was made under r 29.1 of the 2016 Rules)*

### drp-m-0-104 — Preliminary determination (SIAC r 46)
*Keywords: preliminary determination, SIAC r 46, final and binding, savings of time and costs, 90 days, preliminary issue*

**End point:** A party may apply for a final and binding preliminary determination of any issue where:
- (a) the parties agree;
- (b) it is likely to save time and costs and make resolution more efficient; or
- (c) the circumstances otherwise warrant it (SIAC25 r 46.1).
The tribunal decides whether to proceed, sets the procedure, and decides within 90 days of filing unless the Registrar extends (r 46.3–46.4). The tribunal's inherent power to order preliminary issues remains (r 46.5).

*Source note: SIAC25 r 46*

### drp-m-0-105 — Security for costs
*Keywords: security for costs, IAA s 12(1)(a), s 12(4), foreign claimant, resident outside Singapore, foreign corporation, SIAC r 48, security for claims r 49*

**End point:** A Singapore-seated tribunal may order security for costs (IAA s 12(1)(a); SIAC25 r 48). It must not do so *only* because the claimant is an individual ordinarily resident outside Singapore, or a company incorporated or managed outside Singapore (s 12(4)). SIAC25 r 49 also allows security for the claim. Failing to provide security can lead to termination (0-102).

*Source note: IAA s 12(1)(a), (4); SIAC25 rr 48–49; ARB-SN p52 para 179(a)*

### drp-m-0-106 — Third-party funding disclosure (SIAC r 38)
*Keywords: third-party funding, disclosure, funder identity, SIAC r 38, Notice of Arbitration, Response, Direct Economic Interest*

**End point:** Under SIAC25 a party must disclose the existence of any third-party funding agreement and the funder's identity and contact details. It does so in its Notice or Response, or as soon as practicable after concluding the agreement, and must notify later changes (r 38; r 6.3(h); r 7.1(g)). Funders are bound by confidentiality (r 59). Disclosure helps arbitrators check conflicts.

*Source note: SIAC25 rr 2.1 ("Direct Economic Interest"), 6.3(h), 7.1(g), 38, 59.1*

## 0.XIV Commencing the arbitration and SIAC procedures

- **What goes in a SIAC Notice of Arbitration? When does the arbitration start?** Starts when the Registrar receives the Notice; contents (a)–(i): demand, parties, delivery, agreement, contract, dispute and relief with estimate, rules of law / seat / language / number / constitution, third-party funding, filing fee (r 6) → [0-107](#drp-m-0-107)
- **Respondent's Response: deadline and contents?** 14 days from commencement or receipt (later); jurisdiction objections; counterclaims (r 7) → [0-108](#drp-m-0-108)

- **Mid-size claim (S$1m–10m) or agreed?** Expedited Procedure: by agreement, or on application decided by the President; sole arbitrator unless the President decides otherwise; documents-only possible; award in 6 months (r 14; Sch 3) → [0-111](#drp-m-0-111)

### drp-m-0-107 — Notice of Arbitration: commencement and contents (SIAC r 6)
*Keywords: Notice of Arbitration, SIAC r 6, commencement date, received by Registrar, contents, demand, arbitration agreement, relief sought, estimate of claim, seat, language, number of arbitrators, third-party funding, Claim Filing Fee, Statement of Claim, AMA Protocol*

**End point:** The claimant delivers the Notice to the Registrar and the respondent. The date the Registrar receives it is the date of commencement (SIAC25 r 6.1–6.2). The Notice may also comment on mediation under the AMA Protocol and include the Statement of Claim (r 6.4). The Registrar checks for substantial compliance and may set a time to remedy, failing which it may terminate without prejudice (r 6.5–6.6).

> **SIAC25 r 6.3: the Notice shall include**
> - (a) a demand that the dispute be referred to arbitration;
> - (b) the parties' and representatives' identity and contact details;
> - (c) the date and mode of delivery of the Notice to the respondent;
> - (d) a copy or description of the arbitration agreement;
> - (e) a copy or description of the contract or instrument;
> - (f) the nature and circumstances of the dispute, the relief sought and an initial estimate of the claim amount;
> - (g) any comment on the applicable rules of law, seat, language, number of arbitrators and procedure for constituting the tribunal;
> - (h) a statement on any third-party funding agreement and the funder's identity and contact details;
> - (i) payment of the Claim Filing Fee.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 6; ARB-Plan p2 (drafting Notice and Response); ARB-CS1 p3 (key elements of a Notice under the SIAC and ICC Rules)*

### drp-m-0-108 — Response to the Notice (SIAC r 7)
*Keywords: Response to Notice of Arbitration, SIAC r 7, 14 days, later of commencement or receipt, jurisdiction objection, counterclaim, set-off, failure to respond, Statement of Defence optional*

**End point:** The respondent delivers its Response within 14 days of commencement or of receiving the Notice, whichever is later (SIAC25 r 7.1). If a Statement of Claim came with the Notice, the Response may, but need not, include the Defence and Counterclaim (r 7.2). Failing to respond does not stop SIAC proceeding (r 7.4).

> **SIAC25 r 7.1: the Response shall include**
> - (a) the respondent's and representatives' identity and contact details;
> - (b) the date and mode of delivery to the claimant;
> - (c) a brief statement of the dispute and a confirmation or denial of all or part of the claims;
> - (d) any jurisdictional objection under r 8.1 and/or r 31.2;
> - (e) any counterclaim, cross-claim or set-off, with relief claimed and an initial estimate;
> - (f) any comment on the applicable rules of law, seat, language, number of arbitrators and constitution;
> - (g) a statement on third-party funding.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 7*

### drp-m-0-109 — Commencement under the Model Law; limitation (Art 21; s 8A IAA)
*Keywords: commencement of arbitral proceedings, Art 21, receipt of request by respondent, Limitation Act applies, IAA s 8A, time excluded after award set aside, s 8A(2)*

**End point:** Unless otherwise agreed, arbitral proceedings start on the date the respondent receives a request to refer the dispute to arbitration (Art 21). The Limitation Act 1959 and Foreign Limitation Periods Act 2012 apply to arbitrations as to court proceedings (IAA s 8A(1)). If an award is set aside, the court may exclude the time between commencement and the setting-aside order (s 8A(2)). Under SIAC rules the start date is the Registrar's receipt of the Notice instead (0-107).

*Source note: MAL Art 21; IAA s 8A; SIAC25 r 6.2*

### drp-m-0-110 — Streamlined Procedure (SIAC r 13, Schedule 2)
*Keywords: Streamlined Procedure, SIAC r 13, S$1,000,000, S$1 million, Schedule 2, sole arbitrator, 3 days, 3 months, no early dismissal, case management conference 5 days, opt out in writing*

**End point:** The Streamlined Procedure applies where the parties agree before the tribunal is constituted, or the amount in dispute does not exceed S$1,000,000. In the second case the President may disapply it on a party's application (SIAC25 r 13.1). It can be excluded by written agreement (r 13.3). Its features are set out below.

> **SIAC25 Schedule 2**
> - sole arbitrator always; joint nomination within 3 days, else the President appoints;
> - challenge within 3 days;
> - case management conference within 5 days of constitution;
> - final award within 3 months of constitution unless the Registrar extends;
> - no preliminary determination (r 46) or early dismissal (r 47) applications.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 13, Sch 2. Conflict: NIC-ARB gives an S$5m expedited threshold under the 2016 Rules; SIAC25 has a Streamlined Procedure up to S$1m and an Expedited Procedure up to S$10m (0-111).*

### drp-m-0-111 — Expedited Procedure (SIAC r 14, Schedule 3)
*Keywords: Expedited Procedure, SIAC r 14, S$10,000,000, S$10 million, S$1 million, President decides, Schedule 3, sole arbitrator, documents only, remote hearing, 6 months, summary reasons, exceptional urgency*

**End point:** The Expedited Procedure applies if the parties agree before constitution (SIAC25 r 14.1). Otherwise a party may apply, up to constitution, where one of three conditions is met (box). The President decides after hearing the parties (r 14.3). It can be excluded in writing (r 14.4).

> **SIAC25 r 14.2: application grounds**
> - (a) the amount in dispute exceeds S$1,000,000 but not S$10,000,000;
> - (b) it is ≤ S$1,000,000 and the President has disapplied the Streamlined Procedure; or
> - (c) the circumstances warrant it.
>
> **Schedule 3**
> - sole arbitrator unless the President decides otherwise;
> - decided on documents unless a party requests a hearing or the tribunal thinks one appropriate; hearings remote by default;
> - the tribunal may refuse or limit document production and submissions;
> - summary reasons unless the parties agree none;
> - final award within 6 months of constitution unless extended.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 14, Sch 3; ARB-SN p23 para 72 (agree to expedited arbitration); ARB-SN p36 para 120. Conflict: NIC-ARB's S$5m threshold is the 2016 Rules figure; SIAC25 r 14.2(a) is S$10m.*

## 0.XV Interim measures

- **What interim orders can a Singapore-seated tribunal make?** s 12(1)(a)–(j): security for costs, discovery, affidavit evidence, preserve or sell property, samples, preserve evidence, secure the amount in dispute, prevent dissipation, interim injunction, enforce confidentiality → [0-112](#drp-m-0-112)
  - Can a s 12 order be set aside? No: not an "award" (*PT Pukuafu*) → [0-112](#drp-m-0-112)
- **Party ignores the tribunal's order?** Enforce with the High Court's permission like a court order (s 12(6)); forum non conveniens irrelevant (*CXG v CXI*) → [0-113](#drp-m-0-113)
- **Model Law 1985 v 2006?** 1985 Art 17 applies; 2006 Arts 17–17J (test, ex parte preliminary orders, 20 days) not enacted → [0-114](#drp-m-0-114)
- **Need relief before the tribunal exists?** SIAC emergency arbitrator: appointed within 24 hours; order or award within 14 days; binding; lapses if no tribunal in 90 days (Sch 1) → [0-115](#drp-m-0-115)
- **Court or tribunal?** Court if no tribunal yet, relief needed abroad, third parties or bank accounts, or the tribunal lacks power; but confidentiality may be lost → [0-116](#drp-m-0-116)
- **What can the Singapore court order under s 12A?**
  - Matters in s 12(1)(c)–(j); urgent → preserve evidence or assets, even for a *proposed* party (s 12A(4)); not urgent → only with the tribunal's permission or the other parties' written agreement (s 12A(5)) → [0-117](#drp-m-0-117)
  - Always only if or to the extent the tribunal or institution cannot act effectively (s 12A(6)) — so try the emergency arbitrator; lapses when the tribunal rules (s 12A(7)) → [0-117](#drp-m-0-117)
  - Foreign seat? Power exists (s 12A(1)(b)) but may refuse as inappropriate (s 12A(3)); against non-signatories and non-parties → [0-118](#drp-m-0-118)
- **How does the court weigh urgency and grant an injunction?** Urgency judged by what the situation justified, not delay (*KBP*); balance of convenience (*Maldives Airports*) → [0-119](#drp-m-0-119)
- **Does going to court for interim relief waive the clause?** No (Art 9; SIAC r 45.2) → [0-120](#drp-m-0-120)
- **Enforcing a foreign tribunal's or emergency arbitrator's interim order in Singapore?** Treated as a foreign award (s 27(1)); refusal on s 31 grounds (*CVG v CVH*) → [0-121](#drp-m-0-121)

### drp-m-0-112 — Tribunal's powers (s 12(1) IAA); orders are not awards
*Keywords: interim measures, tribunal powers, IAA s 12(1), security for costs, discovery, preservation of property, preservation of evidence, securing amount in dispute, dissipation of assets, interim injunction, confidentiality, PT Pukuafu v Newmont, not an award, cannot be set aside, SIAC r 45*

**End point:** A Singapore-seated tribunal may make orders or give directions to any party on the s 12(1) matters (box). A s 12 order or direction is not an "award": the s 2(1) definition excludes it. So it cannot be set aside under Art 34 or s 24 (*PT Pukuafu Indah v Newmont Indonesia* [2012] SGHC 187 at [19]). In that case the court had no jurisdiction to set aside an interim order restraining Indonesian proceedings. The leave requirement for enforcement under s 12(6) gives residual protection ([27]). SIAC25 r 45.1 lets the tribunal grant interim relief by order or award and require security.

> **IAA s 12(1): orders or directions for**
> - (a) security for costs;
> - (b) discovery of documents and discovery of facts;
> - (c) evidence by affidavit;
> - (d) preservation, interim custody or sale of property in dispute;
> - (e) samples, observation or experiments on property in dispute;
> - (f) preservation and interim custody of evidence;
> - (g) securing the amount in dispute;
> - (h) ensuring an award is not rendered ineffectual by dissipation of assets;
> - (i) an interim injunction or any other interim measure;
> - (j) enforcing confidentiality agreed in writing, under written law or rule of law, or under the agreed rules.

*Source note: IAA ss 2(1), 12(1); ARB-SN p52–53 paras 178–180; [2012] SGHC 187 at [19], [27]; SIAC25 r 45.1*

### drp-m-0-113 — Enforcing a Singapore tribunal's orders (s 12(6))
*Keywords: enforcement of interim order, IAA s 12(6), permission of High Court, judgment in terms, CXG v CXI, forum non conveniens ill-suited, domestic interim measures, O 48*

**End point:** All orders or directions of a tribunal are, with the High Court's permission, enforceable as if they were court orders, and judgment may be entered in their terms (IAA s 12(6)). Forum non conveniens principles are ill-suited to enforcing interim measures from a Singapore-seated arbitration. The court does not look at the merits, and choosing Singapore as seat gives its courts jurisdiction under s 12(6) (*CXG v CXI* [2023] SGHC 244 at [31], [33], [37], [61]–[63]).

*Source note: IAA s 12(6) ("permission"); ROC O 48 r 3–5; ARB-SN p56–57 para 193; [2023] SGHC 244 at [31], [33], [37], [61]–[63]. Conflict: ARB-SN cites CXG v CXI as "[2023] SGHC 224"; the correct citation is [2023] SGHC 244.*

### drp-m-0-114 — Model Law 1985 Art 17; the 2006 regime is not in force
*Keywords: Model Law Art 17, 1985, interim measure of protection, security, 2006 amendments not enacted, Art 17A test, irreparable harm, reasonable possibility of success, Art 17B preliminary order, ex parte, 20 days, Art 17D, 17E, 17F, 17G, 17H, 17I*

**End point:** Under the 1985 Model Law, in force in Singapore, the tribunal may, unless otherwise agreed, order a party to take an interim measure it considers necessary on the subject matter of the dispute, and require security (Art 17). The parties may agree it has no such power. The 2006 amendments, not enacted in Singapore, add the features set out below. Singapore instead enacted s 12 and s 12A.

> **Model Law 2006 (not law in Singapore)**
> - Art 17(2): measures to maintain the status quo, stop prejudice to the process, preserve assets or evidence.
> - Art 17A(1): (a) harm not adequately reparable by damages is likely and substantially outweighs the harm to the other side; (b) a reasonable possibility of success on the merits.
> - Arts 17B–17C: ex parte preliminary orders where notice risks frustration; notice immediately after; the other side heard; 20-day life, which may be varied after hearing the party subject to the order (Art 17C(4)); binding but not an award and not court-enforceable.
> - Arts 17D–17G: modify, suspend or terminate; security; disclosure of changes; requesting party liable for costs and damages if the measure should not have been granted.
> - Arts 17H–17I: recognition and enforcement of interim measures wherever issued, with refusal grounds.

*Source note: MAL Art 17 (1985); MAL-2006 Arts 17–17J; ARB-SN p53–54 paras 181–186, p57 para 194*

### drp-m-0-115 — Emergency arbitrator (SIAC Schedule 1)
*Keywords: emergency arbitrator, EA, SIAC Schedule 1, r 12.1, before constitution, 24 hours appointment, 14 days order or award, binding, 90 days, ceases to be binding, protective preliminary order, seat Singapore default, IAA s 2(1) arbitral tribunal includes emergency arbitrator*

**End point:** Before the tribunal is constituted, a party may apply for an Emergency Arbitrator (SIAC25 r 12.1, Sch 1). Under the IAA an "arbitral tribunal" includes an emergency arbitrator appointed under the agreed rules (s 2(1)). So the EA's orders are enforceable under s 12(6), and a foreign EA award as a foreign award (0-121).

> **SIAC25 Schedule 1**
> - Application before, with or after the Notice, but before constitution (para 2); the Notice must follow within 7 days if the application came first (para 6).
> - The President seeks to appoint the EA within 24 hours (para 7); challenge within 24 hours (para 9).
> - Seat of the EA proceedings: the agreed seat, otherwise Singapore (para 12).
> - The EA has all the tribunal's powers; sets a schedule within 24 hours; makes the order or award within 14 days of appointment unless extended.
> - Binding from when made (deemed agreement to comply).
> - Ceases to bind if the parties agree, the EA or tribunal decides, the claims are withdrawn, on the final award, or if the tribunal is not constituted within 90 days. The tribunal is not bound by the EA's reasons.
> - Protective preliminary order without notice: decided within 24 hours; expires after 14 days.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 12.1, Sch 1; IAA s 2(1); ARB-SN p52 para 177, p55–56 para 190*

### drp-m-0-116 — When to go to court for interim relief
*Keywords: court-ordered interim measures, tribunal not yet constituted, enforceability abroad, attachment of bank account, third parties, tribunal lacks power, confidentiality lost*

**End point:** A party may go to court rather than the tribunal for four reasons:
- the tribunal is not yet constituted and urgent relief is needed;
- a tribunal's interim measure may not be enforceable abroad under the NYC, so relief may be needed from the court where it must be executed;
- the relief will need court help anyway, e.g. attaching a bank account;
- the parties have taken away the tribunal's power (Art 17).
The downside is that confidentiality may be lost. In Singapore, s 12A(6) means a court acts only where the tribunal or institution cannot act effectively, so consider an emergency arbitrator first.

*Source note: ARB-SN p55–56 paras 187, 190*

### drp-m-0-117 — Court's powers under s 12A: urgent and non-urgent cases
*Keywords: IAA s 12A, court-ordered interim measures, s 12A(2), s 12(1)(c) to (j), urgency, preserving evidence or assets, proposed party, Mareva, freezing injunction, s 12A(5) permission of tribunal or written agreement, s 12A(6) unable to act effectively, emergency arbitration, s 12A(7) ceases*

**End point:** The High Court has the same power over the s 12(1)(c)–(j) matters as in a court action (IAA s 12A(2)). It does not cover (a) security for costs or (b) discovery. The power is limited as set out below. Because the major institutional rules provide for emergency arbitrators, a party under SIAC rules can be met with the argument that it should use the EA first.

> **IAA s 12A(3)–(7)**
> - (3) may refuse if a foreign seat makes the order inappropriate (0-118).
> - (4) urgency: on a party's or *proposed* party's application, orders necessary to preserve evidence or assets (e.g. a freezing injunction).
> - (5) not urgent: only on a party's application, on notice to the others and the tribunal, made with the tribunal's permission or the other parties' written agreement.
> - (6) in every case, only if or to the extent that the tribunal, and any institution or person vested with power, has no power or is unable for the time being to act effectively.
> - (7) the court order ceases to have effect, wholly or partly, when the tribunal or institution makes an order expressly relating to it.

*Source note: IAA s 12A; ARB-SN p55–56 paras 188–190; PYP23 p18 Q25, p25 Q32 (evidence about to be destroyed: yes, if the tribunal is unable for the time being to act effectively)*

### drp-m-0-118 — Foreign-seated arbitrations; non-parties (s 12A(1)(b), (3))
*Keywords: foreign seat, s 12A(1)(b), irrespective of place of arbitration, Swift-Fortune overruled, s 12A(3) inappropriate, seat court objectionable, Novo Nordisk v KBP Biosciences, non-signatory, non-party, Alphard Maritime*

**End point:** Section 12A applies whether or not the seat is in Singapore (s 12A(1)(b)), reversing *Swift-Fortune v Magnifica Marine* [2006] SGCA 42. The court may refuse where a foreign seat makes an order inappropriate (s 12A(3)). That turns on whether the seat court would regard the relief as objectionable; that the lex arbitri lacks such relief is not enough (*Novo Nordisk v KBP Biosciences* [2025] SGHC(I) 22 at [107], not disturbed on appeal). The court may grant relief against a non-signatory to the arbitration agreement (*Novo Nordisk* at [95]) and a non-party to the arbitration (*KBP Biosciences v Novo Nordisk* [2026] SGCA(I) 2 at [88]). It may also act against non-parties within its jurisdiction (*Alphard Maritime v Samson Maritime* [2025] SGHC 154 at [35]).

*Source note: IAA s 12A(1), (3); ARB-SN p56 paras 190–191; [2006] SGCA 42; [2025] SGHC(I) 22 at [95], [107]; [2026] SGCA(I) 2 at [88]; [2025] SGHC 154 at [35]; PYP23 p18 Q25. Conflict: ARB-SN says the CA at [89] did not overrule the [107] point; [89] does not address s 12A(3), so the block says only that the point was not disturbed on appeal.*

### drp-m-0-119 — Urgency and balance of convenience (KBP; Maldives Airports)
*Keywords: urgency, delay, risk of dissipation, KBP Biosciences v Novo Nordisk, balance of convenience, American Cyanamid, Maldives Airports v GMR Male, damages adequate remedy, vague injunction, supervision in foreign land*

**End point:**
- **Urgency:** judged not by the length of delay but by whether the situation justified the application. Consider what was known of the dissipation risk and any limits on the emergency arbitrator's powers (*KBP Biosciences v Novo Nordisk* [2026] SGCA(I) 2 at [83]). A 5–7 month gap did not defeat urgency where the applicants did not know the relevant facts earlier ([82]).
- **Injunctions:** the test is the balance of convenience (*Maldives Airports v GMR Male International Airport* [2013] SGCA 16 at [53]). There the injunction was set aside ([54]–[62]): damages were adequate, its broad and vague terms left the Maldives government unsure what compliance required, and it needed unacceptable supervision abroad.

*Source note: ARB-SN p55, 56 paras 189, 192; [2026] SGCA(I) 2 at [82]–[83]; [2013] SGCA 16 at [53]–[62]*

### drp-m-0-120 — Asking a court for interim relief is not a waiver (Art 9)
*Keywords: Art 9 Model Law, court interim measure not incompatible, no waiver of arbitration agreement, SIAC r 45.2, notify tribunal*

**End point:** It is not incompatible with an arbitration agreement for a party to ask a court for an interim measure, before or during the arbitration, nor for the court to grant it (Art 9). So seeking interim or injunctive relief in court does not waive the agreement. SIAC25 r 45.2 is to the same effect, but requires the application and decision to be notified promptly to the tribunal and Registrar.

*Source note: MAL Art 9; ARB-SN p32 para 113; SIAC25 r 45.2*

### drp-m-0-121 — Enforcing foreign interim orders and emergency awards (s 27(1))
*Keywords: foreign interim order, foreign emergency arbitrator award, IAA s 27(1), arbitral award includes order or direction, s 12(1)(c) to (j), CVG v CVH, refusal on s 31 grounds, NYC Art I awards only, Model Law 2006 Art 17H*

**End point:** For Part III (foreign awards), "arbitral award" includes an order or direction of a tribunal on the s 12(1)(c)–(j) matters (IAA s 27(1)). So a foreign tribunal's interim orders, and a foreign emergency arbitrator's award, can be enforced in Singapore as foreign awards, subject to the s 31 refusal grounds (*CVG v CVH* [2022] SGHC 249). There, enforcement was ultimately refused because CVH could not present its case. Elsewhere this can be a problem, since the NYC covers only awards (Art I). States that adopted the 2006 Model Law enforce interim measures under Arts 17H–17I.

*Source note: IAA s 27(1); ARB-SN p57 para 194; [2022] SGHC 249 at [1]–[2]. Conflict: ARB-SN cites CVG v CVH as "[2022] SGHC 49"; the correct citation is [2022] SGHC 249.*

## 0.XVI Anti-suit injunctions

- **Counterparty sues abroad in breach of a Singapore arbitration clause: will the Singapore court restrain it?** Yes, if there is a valid agreement, a breach or threatened breach, and no strong cause not to enforce (*Sun Travels*); no diffidence (*Angelic Grace*; *Maldives Airports*) → [0-122](#drp-m-0-122)
- **Source of the power? Court's attitude?** Civil Law Act s 4(10); duty to uphold the agreement (*WSG Nimbus*) → [0-123](#drp-m-0-123)
- **Will the foreign court obey?** Depends on its law; comity; *UniCredit* (UK ASI for a French-seated arbitration) → [0-124](#drp-m-0-124)
- **Can the tribunal itself restrain the foreign proceedings?** Interim order (s 12(1)(i)), not an award, so not set aside (*PT Pukuafu*); enforceable with permission → [0-125](#drp-m-0-125)

### drp-m-0-122 — When a Singapore court grants an ASI in aid of arbitration
*Keywords: anti-suit injunction, ASI, restrain foreign proceedings, valid arbitration agreement, s 2A, breach, no strong cause, Sun Travels v Hilton, Angelic Grace, Maldives Airports, comity, ARB-CS2 Indonesian court*

**End point:** An anti-suit injunction is an order by a court with personal jurisdiction over a party, requiring that party not to file a claim in a foreign jurisdiction, or not to proceed with one already filed. It acts on the party, not the foreign court. The concern is that the foreign action would frustrate the forum's proceedings on the same issues, or is brought in bad faith to harass (ARB-SN para 195).
A Singapore court will generally grant an anti-suit injunction in aid of arbitration if three things are shown:
- a valid and binding arbitration agreement (IAA s 2A);
- that the defendant has threatened or acted in breach of it by suing elsewhere;
- no strong cause not to enforce the agreement (*Sun Travels & Tours v Hilton International Manage (Maldives)* [2019] SGCA 10 at [65]–[68]).
The CA accepted that there is "no good reason for diffidence" in restraining foreign proceedings the defendant promised not to bring (*Maldives Airports* [2013] SGCA 16, adopting *The Angelic Grace*). This answers ARB-CS2 Q2 (Indonesian proceedings, Singapore-seated ICC clause) and SampleQs Arb Q9 ("Narnia" proceedings).

*Source note: ARB-SN p58–59 paras 195–198 (definition: p58 para 195); [2019] SGCA 10 at [65]–[68]; [2013] SGCA 16; ARB-CS2 p1 Q2; SampleQs p59 Q9 (key p122: D). Conflict: ARB-SN fn 8 cites Sun Travels only as [2019] 1 SLR 732; the neutral citation is [2019] SGCA 10.*

### drp-m-0-123 — Source of the power and the court's attitude
*Keywords: Civil Law Act s 4(10), permanent injunction, R1 International v Lonstroff, WSG Nimbus v Board of Control for Cricket in Sri Lanka, duty to uphold arbitration agreement, Singapore as arbitration centre*

**End point:** The court's power to grant an injunction comes from Civil Law Act s 4(10) (*R1 International v Lonstroff* [2014] SGHC 69, reversed on other grounds). Once satisfied there is an arbitration agreement, the court "has a duty to uphold that agreement and prevent any breach of it" (*WSG Nimbus v Board of Control for Cricket in Sri Lanka* [2002] SGHC 104 at [91]). This reflects the IAA's purpose of facilitating Singapore-seated arbitration ([90]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p59 paras 199–200; [2002] SGHC 104 at [90]–[91] (reported [2002] 1 SLR(R) 1088); [2014] SGHC 69 (reported [2014] 3 SLR 166)*

### drp-m-0-124 — Enforceability abroad; UniCredit
*Keywords: ASI enforceability, foreign court may ignore, comity, coercive power over party, UniCredit v RusChemAlliance, UK Supreme Court, French seat, English governing law*

**End point:** Anti-suit injunctions raise comity concerns. They work only through the coercive power the court can exert over the party, and a foreign court may ignore them. Whether a Singapore ASI is enforceable abroad depends on that country's law. In *UniCredit Bank v RusChemAlliance* [2024] UKSC 15 the UK Supreme Court granted an ASI to protect an arbitration seated in France, its only link being English law governing the arbitration agreement. Singapore's apex courts have not yet considered this.

*Source note: ARB-SN p58–59 paras 196, 201–202 (UniCredit is a foreign case taken from ARB-SN; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-125 — Tribunal-ordered anti-suit relief
*Keywords: tribunal anti-suit order, interim injunction, s 12(1)(i), PT Pukuafu, restrain foreign court proceedings, not an award, enforce with permission s 12(6)*

**End point:** A tribunal can itself order a party not to pursue foreign court proceedings, as an interim injunction or other interim measure (IAA s 12(1)(i)). Such an order is not an award and cannot be set aside. In *PT Pukuafu* [2012] SGHC 187 the court refused to set aside a tribunal's order restraining Indonesian proceedings ([19]). It is enforced with the High Court's permission (s 12(6)).

*Source note: IAA s 12(1)(i), (6); [2012] SGHC 187 at [19], [27]; ARB-SN p53 para 180; SampleQs p59 Q9*

## 0.XVII The award

- **Can the tribunal delegate the decision?** No; test: did the arbitrator exercise their own judgment (*Neale v Richardson*; *Anderson v Wallace*) → [0-126](#drp-m-0-126)
- **What relief can the tribunal award? Interest?** Any remedy the High Court could give; simple or compound interest (s 12(5); s 20) → [0-127](#drp-m-0-127)
- **Costs: who pays? "Disproportionate costs" against public policy?** Loser pays / proportional / own costs; not contrary to public policy (*VV v VW*) → [0-128](#drp-m-0-128)
- **Is this decision an "award" (can it be set aside)?** Decision on the substance, incl. interim, interlocutory, partial; excludes s 12 orders; substance not label (*PT Asuransi* [70]; *DLS v DLT*) → [0-129](#drp-m-0-129)
- **Types of award?** Final, interim, partial, consent, default, additional; several awards on different issues (s 19A) → [0-130](#drp-m-0-130)
- **Can the tribunal revisit its award?** No: final and binding; functus officio except Art 33 and Art 34(4) (s 19B; *Bloomberry*); extended res judicata (*CIX v DGN*) → [0-131](#drp-m-0-131)
- **Form?** Writing, signed (majority with reason), reasons unless agreed or consent award, date and place; deemed made at the seat (Art 31) → [0-132](#drp-m-0-132)
- **Arbitrators disagree?** Majority (Art 29); SIAC: presiding arbitrator alone if no majority (r 51.2) → [0-133](#drp-m-0-133)
- **Award late?** Laws rarely set limits; rules do (SIAC: draft within 90 days, scrutiny); consequences debated (*Hasbro*; *Alphamix*) → [0-134](#drp-m-0-134)
- **Clerical or arithmetic error?** Request within 30 days, correct within 30 days; tribunal's own initiative 30 days; own-initiative correction does not extend setting-aside time (*DBX v DBZ*) → [0-135](#drp-m-0-135)
- **Unclear award?** Interpretation only if the parties agree; 30 + 30 days → [0-136](#drp-m-0-136)
- **Claim not dealt with?** Additional award: request within 30 days, made within 60; tribunal may extend (Art 33(3)–(4)) → [0-137](#drp-m-0-137)

### drp-m-0-126 — Decision-making cannot be delegated
*Keywords: non-delegable, delegation of decision, Neale v Richardson, Anderson v Wallace, own judgment, tribunal secretary, improper delegation, improperly constituted*

**End point:** An arbitrator's duty to decide cannot be delegated (*Neale v Richardson*). The test for excessive delegation is whether the arbitrator exercised their own judgment in accepting or rejecting advice or opinions (*Anderson v Wallace*). Improper delegation may make the award challengeable as not in accordance with the agreed procedure, or as made by an improperly constituted tribunal (Art 34(2)(a)(iv)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p60 para 203 (Neale v Richardson and Anderson v Wallace are foreign cases taken from ARB-SN; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-127 — Remedies and interest (s 12(5), s 20 IAA)
*Keywords: remedies, damages, specific performance, declaration, rectification, indemnity, IAA s 12(5)(a), any remedy High Court could order, interest, simple or compound, s 20, judgment debt rate, SIAC r 51.5*

**End point:** Typical remedies are:
- damages and interest;
- specific performance;
- declarations;
- rectification;
- indemnities;
- costs.
The tribunal may award any remedy the High Court could have ordered in civil proceedings (IAA s 12(5)(a)). It may award simple or compound interest from any date, at any rate and with any rest, on sums awarded, sums paid before the award, and costs (s 20(1)). Unless the award says otherwise, a sum awarded carries interest from the award date at the judgment-debt rate (s 20(3)). SIAC25 r 51.5 is similar.

*Source note: IAA ss 12(5), 20; ARB-SN p60 paras 189–190 (second numbering run); SIAC25 r 51.5*

### drp-m-0-128 — Costs
*Keywords: costs, loser pays, costs follow the event, proportional allocation, each party bears own costs, VV v VW, disproportionate costs not public policy, DKB v DKC, SIAC r 51.4, s 21 assessment by SIAC Registrar*

**End point:** The final award usually deals with costs. The options are reasonable costs borne by the overall loser, allocated proportionally, or each party bearing its own. A costs award said to be so disproportionate as to breach public policy was not set aside. Proportionality in assessing arbitration costs is not part of Singapore public policy (*VV v VW* [2008] SGHC 11 at [31]). Courts have a discretion in related applications. In *DKB v DKC* [2025] SGHC(I) 21 at [30]–[31] the SICC refused costs to a successful stay applicant whose conduct showed no intention of arbitrating. Costs directed by an award are assessable by the SIAC Registrar unless the award says otherwise (IAA s 21).

*Source note: ARB-SN p60–61 paras 191–193 (second run); [2008] SGHC 11 at [31]; [2025] SGHC(I) 21 at [30]–[31]; IAA s 21; SIAC25 r 51.4*

### drp-m-0-129 — What counts as an "award" (s 2(1) IAA)
*Keywords: definition of award, IAA s 2(1), decision on the substance, interim, interlocutory, partial, excludes s 12 orders, substance not form, PT Asuransi v Dexia, DLS v DLT, provisional, monthly payments order, jurisdiction ruling*

**End point:** Only an "award" can be set aside or enforced under the NYC. An "award" is a decision of the tribunal on the substance of the dispute, including interim, interlocutory or partial awards, but excluding s 12 orders or directions (IAA s 2(1)). Substance, not the title, decides (*PT Asuransi v Dexia* [2006] SGCA 41 at [70]; *DLS v DLT* [2025] SGHC 61 at [19], [22]). Orders and directions are provisional: they do not finally dispose of an issue or claim and can be varied ([22]). An order to make "Monthly Payments" pending final assessment was not an award ([27]). A ruling declining jurisdiction is not an award (*PT Asuransi* at [66]). For foreign awards, Part III counts s 12(1)(c)–(j) orders as awards (s 27(1)).

*Source note: IAA ss 2(1), 27(1); ARB-SN p61–63 paras 194–200 (second run); [2006] SGCA 41 at [66], [70]; [2025] SGHC 61 at [19], [22], [27]; PYP23 p54 Q1. Erratum: ARB-SN para 200(b) (p62) reads "inherently incapable of being varied"; the Examiner has confirmed it should read "inherently capable" (SILE notice, Nov 2026), consistent with DLS at [22].*

### drp-m-0-130 — Types of award (s 19A IAA)
*Keywords: types of award, final award, interim award, partial award, consent award, default award, additional award, provisional ruling, IAA s 19A, more than one award, PT Perusahaan Gas Negara v CRW, time runs from receipt*

**End point:** Unless otherwise agreed, the tribunal may make more than one award at different times on different aspects, e.g. on an issue affecting the whole claim or on part of a claim. It must say what the award covers (IAA s 19A; SIAC25 r 51.1). Interim and partial awards dispose of certain preliminary issues or claims before the rest (*PT Perusahaan Gas Negara v CRW Joint Operation* [2015] SGCA 30 at [45]–[47]). The time to set aside each runs from its receipt.

> **Kinds (ARB-SN para 202)**
> - Final: terminates the proceedings (Art 32(1)).
> - Interim: disposes of discrete issues, not a whole claim.
> - Partial: finally disposes of certain claims.
> - Provisional rulings (e.g. interim measures): not final, can be varied; not awards.
> - Consent: settlement recorded as an award (Art 30).
> - Default: made despite a party's non-participation (Art 25(c)).
> - Additional: on claims omitted from the award (Art 33(3)).

*Source note: IAA s 19A; ARB-SN p63–64 paras 201–202 (second run); [2015] SGCA 30 at [45]–[47]. Conflict: ARB-SN attributes the "inherently capable of being varied" description of provisional rulings to Persero at [49]–[50]; that phrase comes from DLS v DLT at [22], and the interim/partial definitions are at [45]–[47].*

### drp-m-0-131 — Finality, functus officio and res judicata (s 19B IAA)
*Keywords: finality of award, IAA s 19B, final and binding, functus officio, Bloomberry, cannot vary amend correct review revoke, Art 33, Art 34(4), res judicata, Henderson v Henderson, extended doctrine, CIX v DGN, non-party, SIAC r 51.6, r 51.7*

**End point:**
- **Final and binding:** an award is final and binding on the parties and those claiming through them, and may be relied on as a defence or set-off (IAA s 19B(1); SIAC25 r 51.6).
- **Functus officio:** except under Arts 33 and 34(4), the tribunal must not vary, amend, correct, review, add to or revoke the award once it has been signed and delivered (s 19B(2)–(3)). A tribunal is functus on the issues an award decides, whether interim, partial or final (*Bloomberry Resorts v Global Gaming Philippines* [2021] SGCA 94 at [157]).
- **Errors of law or fact** in an award are final and binding; they cannot be appealed or set aside (*PT Asuransi v Dexia Bank* [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]). The two routes left are setting aside at the seat and resisting enforcement. Policy: too much court review shifts decision power to national courts; too much arbitral autonomy creates moral hazard (ARB-SN para 216).
- **Did the tribunal decide the issue finally?** A partial award cannot be revised in a later award, so it matters whether an earlier decision was final (test in box).
- **Extended res judicata (Henderson v Henderson):** it bars points that could with reasonable diligence have been raised earlier (*CIX v DGN* [2025] SGCA 10 at [56]–[57]). The CA held it can apply even without identity of parties, where the earlier proceedings were an arbitration and the later ones a court action ([66], [68]–[69]). The inquiry is into substance, not form, to stop repackaged claims against new parties.

> **Was an issue finally and conclusively resolved? (ONGC Petri Additions v DL E&C [2023] SGHC 197 at [35])**
> - (a) substance carries real weight, not mere form;
> - (b) form still has a role: the tribunal's own description of the decision is relevant but not conclusive;
> - (c) ask how a reasonable recipient of the decision would have viewed it;
> - (d) that recipient looks at objective attributes: the tribunal's description, the formality of the language, and the level of detail of the reasoning;
> - (e) the recipient has all the information available to the parties and tribunal when the decision was made, so the background and context of the proceedings matter.

*Source note: IAA s 19B; ARB-SN p62 paras 195, 198–199 and p67–69 paras 214–219 (ONGC Petri test: p67 para 214, as stated in ARB-SN; moral hazard: p68 para 216); [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]; [2021] SGCA 94 at [157]; [2025] SGCA 10 at [56]–[57], [65]–[69]; SIAC25 r 51.6–51.7. Conflict: ARB-SN cites CIX at [58] for the "twice vexed" policy and [67]–[68] for the undisputed premise; they are at [57] and [65]. ARB-SN para 214 cites "Rule 28.9 SIAC" (2016 numbering); the 2025 provision is r 51.6.*

### drp-m-0-132 — Form and content (Art 31; SIAC r 52)
*Keywords: form of award, Art 31, in writing, signed, majority signatures, reason for omitted signature, reasons, unless agreed, consent award, date and place, deemed made at seat, SIAC r 52*

**End point:** The award must be in writing and signed. With more than one arbitrator, the majority's signatures suffice if the reason for any omitted signature is stated (Art 31(1)). It states its reasons unless the parties agree otherwise or it is a consent award (Art 31(2)). It states its date and the place of arbitration, and is deemed made at that place (Art 31(3)). It is delivered to each party (Art 31(4)). Under the NYC a "foreign award" means one made at a seat outside Singapore. SIAC25 r 52 adds electronic or counterpart signing and delivery only after the costs are paid.

*Source note: MAL Art 31; ARB-SN p64 paras 203–204 (second run); SIAC25 r 52*

### drp-m-0-133 — Majority decisions (Art 29; SIAC r 51.2)
*Keywords: majority decision, Art 29, presiding arbitrator procedural questions, SIAC r 51.2, presiding arbitrator alone, dissenting opinion, truncated tribunal r 51.3*

**End point:** With more than one arbitrator, decisions are by majority unless otherwise agreed. Procedural questions may be decided by the presiding arbitrator if authorised (Art 29). Under SIAC25 r 51.2, failing a majority the presiding arbitrator alone makes the award. If an arbitrator refuses to take part after a reasonable opportunity, the others may proceed and must give reasons (r 51.3).

*Source note: MAL Art 29; ARB-SN p65 para 208; SIAC25 r 51.2–51.3*

### drp-m-0-134 — Time limits and SIAC scrutiny
*Keywords: time limit for award, institutional rules, SIAC r 53, draft award 90 days, scrutiny by Registrar, mandate ends, Hasbro v Catalyst, no prejudice, Alphamix v Riviere du Rampart, tacit extension*

**End point:** Arbitration laws rarely set time limits for awards; institutional rules may. Under SIAC25 r 53 the tribunal estimates timing within 30 days of the last submission. It submits the draft award within 90 days of the last submission unless the Registrar decides otherwise. The Registrar may suggest changes of form and point out matters of substance, and no award issues until approved as to form. If a time limit is missed there are two views:
- the mandate ends;
- annulment is unjustified where there is no prejudice (*Hasbro v Catalyst*).
In *Alphamix v District Council of Rivière du Rempart* [2023] UKPC 20 the parties' conduct showed a tacit agreement to extend, so an award 3 days late stood.

*Source note: SIAC25 r 53; ARB-SN p64–65 paras 205–207 (Hasbro and Alphamix are foreign cases taken from ARB-SN; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-135 — Correction of the award (Art 33(1)(a), (2))
*Keywords: correction of award, Art 33(1)(a), computation error, clerical error, typographical error, 30 days, own initiative Art 33(2), DBX v DBZ, setting aside time limit, SIAC r 54.1*

**End point:** Within 30 days of receipt (unless another period is agreed), a party may, with notice to the other, ask the tribunal to correct computation, clerical, typographical or similar errors. If justified, the tribunal corrects within 30 days of the request (Art 33(1)(a); SIAC25 r 54.1). The tribunal may also correct such errors on its own initiative within 30 days of the award (Art 33(2)). Effect on the setting-aside deadline:
- a party's request postpones the start of the 3 months until the request is disposed of (Art 34(3));
- a correction on the tribunal's own initiative does not extend it (*DBX v DBZ* [2023] SGHC(I) 18 at [43], [56]).

*Source note: MAL Arts 33(1)(a), 33(2), 34(3); ARB-SN p65–66 paras 209–210; [2023] SGHC(I) 18 at [43], [56] (ARB-SN cites "[2024] 3 SLR 141"); SIAC25 r 54.1; PYP23 p16 Q23. Conflict: ARB-SN para 209 cites "Art 34(3)" for the correction request; the provision is Art 33(1)(a).*

### drp-m-0-136 — Interpretation of the award (Art 33(1)(b))
*Keywords: interpretation of award, Art 33(1)(b), if so agreed by the parties, specific point, 30 days, forms part of the award*

**End point:** If the parties so agree, a party may, with notice to the other, ask the tribunal within 30 days of receipt to interpret a specific point or part of the award. If justified, the tribunal gives the interpretation within 30 days, and it forms part of the award (Art 33(1)(b)).

*Source note: MAL Art 33(1)(b); ARB-SN p66 para 211*

### drp-m-0-137 — Additional award; extension of time (Art 33(3)–(4))
*Keywords: additional award, Art 33(3), claims presented but omitted, 30 days request, 60 days, Art 33(4) extend, infra petita, natural justice L W Infrastructure*

**End point:** Unless otherwise agreed, a party may, with notice to the other, ask the tribunal within 30 days of receipt to make an additional award on claims presented but omitted from the award. If justified, the tribunal makes it within 60 days (Art 33(3)). The tribunal may extend the time for a correction, interpretation or additional award (Art 33(4)). Failing to hear the parties on issues for an additional award has founded a natural justice challenge (0-166). A key issue left undecided may also support setting aside (infra petita) or remission (PYP23 Q35).

*Source note: MAL Art 33(3)–(4); ARB-SN p66 paras 212–213, p86 para 277; PYP23 p28 Q35*

## 0.XVIII Setting aside the award

- **Can a party appeal an IAA award for errors of law or fact?** No; set aside only, on exhaustive grounds, only at the seat (Art 34(1); *PT Asuransi* [57]) → [0-138](#drp-m-0-138)
- **Grounds the applicant must prove (Art 34(2)(a))?** (i) incapacity / invalid agreement; (ii) no proper notice / unable to present case; (iii) beyond the submission (severable); (iv) composition or procedure not as agreed → [0-139](#drp-m-0-139)
- **Grounds the court raises itself (Art 34(2)(b))?** (i) not arbitrable under Singapore law; (ii) conflicts with Singapore public policy → [0-140](#drp-m-0-140)
- **Extra IAA grounds?** s 24(a) fraud or corruption; s 24(b) breach of natural justice prejudicing rights → [0-141](#drp-m-0-141)
- **Natural justice: what must be shown?** Which rule, how breached, connection with the award, prejudice (*John Holland*); read the award generously (*Soh Beng Tee*); disregarded evidence that could have changed the outcome (*DFI v DFJ*) → [0-142](#drp-m-0-142)
- **Complained during the arbitration only after the award?** Too late: must give fair intimation and seek suspension, not reserve its position (*China Machine*) → [0-143](#drp-m-0-143)
- **Tribunal decided an unpleaded point / ignored a pleaded point?** Ultra petita = Art 34(2)(a)(iii), two-stage, no prejudice needed; infra petita = natural justice, four conditions (*DKT v DKU*) → [0-144](#drp-m-0-144)
- **Must prejudice be shown?** Jurisdictional: no (*ST Group* [103]); procedural: generally yes → [0-145](#drp-m-0-145)
- **Tribunal copied another award?** Not per se fatal; inquire into prejudgment and bias (*DJP v DJO*) → [0-146](#drp-m-0-146)
- **Thin reasons?** Inadequate reasons = error of law, not a setting-aside ground (*CVV v CWB*) → [0-147](#drp-m-0-147)
- **Challenge a procedural decision mid-arbitration?** No (Art 5; *DMZ v DNA*) → [0-148](#drp-m-0-148)
- **Deadline? Extension for fraud discovered later?** 3 months from receipt (or disposal of an Art 33 request); no extension, even for fraud (*Bloomberry* [2021] SGCA 9) → [0-149](#drp-m-0-149)
- **Ground proved: must the court set aside?** No: "may"; residual discretion → [0-150](#drp-m-0-150)
- **What can the court do?** Set aside wholly or partly; suspend and remit (Art 34(4); *Vietnam Oil* factors) → [0-151](#drp-m-0-151)
- **London-seated award: set aside in Singapore?** No: only at the seat; resist enforcement instead → [0-152](#drp-m-0-152)

### drp-m-0-138 — No appeal; exhaustive grounds; only at the seat
*Keywords: no appeal, errors of law or fact final, PT Asuransi v Dexia, exhaustive grounds, Art 34(1), Art 1(2), seat court, General Division of the High Court, DBO v DBP, originating application, Registrar's Circular*

**End point:** An award's errors of law or fact are final and binding. They cannot be appealed or set aside (*PT Asuransi v Dexia* [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]; *DBO v DBP* [2023] SGHC(I) 21 at [60], [65]). The only exceptions to finality are setting aside at the seat and resisting enforcement wherever it is sought. The setting-aside grounds are exhaustive: Art 34(2), plus IAA s 24 for Singapore seats. Setting aside is available only at the seat (Arts 1(2), 34(1)), before the General Division of the High Court (IAA s 8(1)), by originating application (ROC O 48 r 2(1)(d)). Compare AA s 49 for domestic awards (0-16).

*Source note: MAL Arts 1(2), 34; ARB-SN p67–68 paras 214–216, p70 paras 222–224; [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]; [2023] SGHC(I) 21 at [60], [65]; ROC O 48 r 2; PYP23 p11 Q18, p21 Q28*

### drp-m-0-139 — Art 34(2)(a) grounds the applicant must prove
*Keywords: Art 34(2)(a), incapacity, invalid arbitration agreement, law of Singapore default, proper notice, unable to present case, beyond scope of submission, severability, composition of tribunal, arbitral procedure not in accordance with agreement, COT v COU*

**End point:** The applicant must prove one of the Art 34(2)(a) grounds (box). Where it says no binding contract existed, the seat court decides whether one existed (*COT v COU* [2023] SGCA 31 at [39]).

> **Model Law Art 34(2)(a): the applicant furnishes proof that**
> - (i) a party was under some incapacity, or the agreement is not valid under the law the parties chose or, failing that, the law of Singapore;
> - (ii) it was not given proper notice of the appointment of an arbitrator or of the proceedings, or was otherwise unable to present its case;
> - (iii) the award deals with a dispute not contemplated by or not within the submission, or contains decisions beyond its scope. If decisions on matters submitted can be separated, only the part not submitted is set aside;
> - (iv) the composition of the tribunal or the procedure was not in accordance with the parties' agreement (unless it conflicts with a non-derogable provision) or, failing agreement, with the Model Law.

*Source note: MAL Art 34(2)(a); ARB-SN p70–71 para 225; [2023] SGCA 31 at [39]; PYP21-ARB Q2 (composition: arbitrator lacking agreed qualifications; refusal to adjourn for chosen counsel)*

### drp-m-0-140 — Art 34(2)(b) grounds the court may raise itself
*Keywords: Art 34(2)(b), not capable of settlement by arbitration, non-arbitrable, public policy of Singapore, court's own motion, DMF v DMG*

**End point:** The court may also set aside if it finds, whether or not the applicant proves it, that:
- (i) the subject matter is not capable of settlement by arbitration under Singapore law; or
- (ii) the award conflicts with Singapore public policy (Art 34(2)(b)).
On illegality, the court considers both arbitrability and conflict with public policy (*DMF v DMG* [2025] SGHC(I) 12 at [192]). Public policy is narrow (0-165).

*Source note: MAL Art 34(2)(b); ARB-SN p71 para 225(b), p19 para 59; PYP21-ARB Q1 (winding-up award: arbitrability and public policy)*

### drp-m-0-141 — IAA s 24: fraud or corruption; natural justice
*Keywords: IAA s 24, additional grounds, fraud, corruption, s 24(a), breach of natural justice, s 24(b), rights prejudiced, invoke both s 24(b) and Art 34(2)(a)(ii), CZT v CZU*

**End point:** Despite Art 34(1), the High Court may also set aside an award if:
- (a) its making was induced or affected by fraud or corruption; or
- (b) a breach of the rules of natural justice occurred in connection with its making, by which a party's rights were prejudiced (IAA s 24).
In practice natural justice challenges plead both s 24(b) and Art 34(2)(a)(ii) (e.g. *CWP v CWQ* [2023] SGHC 61; *ONGC Petri v DL E&C* [2023] SGHC 197; *CUW v CUZ* [2023] SGHC(I) 2; *DGE v DGF* [2024] SGHC 107). In *CZT v CZU* [2024] SGCA(I) 6 at [72]–[79] the natural justice complaint was rejected.

*Source note: IAA s 24; ARB-SN p71 para 226, p73 para 231; [2024] SGCA(I) 6 at [72]–[79]; SampleQs p58 Q7 (key p122: C)*

### drp-m-0-142 — Natural justice: elements and threshold
*Keywords: natural justice, John Holland v Toyo, four elements, which rule, how breached, connection with making of award, prejudice, Soh Beng Tee v Fairmount, read generously, not microscopically, DFI v DFJ, material evidence disregarded, different outcome, nemo judex, audi alteram partem, BZW v BZV*

**End point:** Natural justice means no one is judge in their own cause and each party must be heard. The applicant must establish four elements (box) (*John Holland v Toyo Engineering* [2001] SGHC 48; applied in *BZW v BZV* [2022] SGCA 1 at [59]–[60]). The threshold is high. Courts do not scrutinise awards microscopically; awards are read generously, and only meaningful breaches that caused actual prejudice are remedied (*Soh Beng Tee v Fairmount Development* [2007] SGCA 28 at [65(f)]). Two steps apply for disregarded evidence (*DFI v DFJ* [2024] SGHC(I) 4 at [73]):
- there was relevant and material evidence the tribunal disregarded;
- in context, it would arguably have led to a different outcome.

> **John Holland: the applicant must show**
> - (a) which rule of natural justice was breached;
> - (b) how it was breached;
> - (c) how the breach was connected to the making of the award;
> - (d) how it prejudiced the applicant's rights.

*Source note: ARB-SN p72–73 para 230, p85 paras 272–275; [2001] SGHC 48; [2022] SGCA 1 at [59]–[60]; [2007] SGCA 28 at [65(f)]; [2024] SGHC(I) 4 at [73]; SampleQs p58 Q7 (key: C); PYP23 p23 Q30. Conflict: ARB-SN cites Soh Beng Tee at [64]; the "not microscopically / read generously" statement is at [65(f)].*

### drp-m-0-143 — Raise the breach at the time, do not reserve it (China Machine)
*Keywords: China Machine v Jaguar Energy, fair intimation, seek suspension, reserve position, cannot wait to see outcome, DNO v DNP, waiver, natural justice, DFM v DFL, Reliance Infrastructure, public policy disavowed*

**End point:** A party that thinks the process has gone fatally wrong must give the tribunal fair intimation that it will take the point if the tribunal carries on. At the least it should seek a suspension until the breach is remedied. It cannot "reserve" its position and wait to see the result (*China Machine New Energy v Jaguar Energy Guatemala* [2020] SGCA 12 at [170]; *DNO v DNP* [2025] SGHC(I) 24 at [115]). It is a contradiction to claim irretrievable taint while presenting oneself as ready to proceed to an award. Likewise, public policy cannot be used to revive points a party disavowed before the tribunal, or raised and lost (*Reliance Infrastructure* [2024] SGCA(I) 10 at [75]; *DFM v DFL* [2024] SGCA 41 at [45]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p86 para 276, p89 para 288; [2020] SGCA 12 at [168]–[170]; [2025] SGHC(I) 24 at [115]; [2024] SGCA(I) 10 at [75]; [2024] SGCA 41 at [45]; SampleQs p58 Q8 (key p122: B); PYP23 p24 Q31*

### drp-m-0-144 — Ultra petita v infra petita challenges
*Keywords: ultra petita, infra petita, Art 34(2)(a)(iii), beyond scope of submission, pleadings, two-stage enquiry, Bloomberry, India Glycols, no prejudice required, Palm Grove v Hilton, DEM v DEL, natural justice, four conditions, DKT v DKU, ONI Global v GNC, Prayudh v Nestle, Glaziers, silence not failure*

**End point:**
- **Ultra petita** (the tribunal exceeded its mandate) falls under Art 34(2)(a)(iii) (*Palm Grove Beach Hotels v Hilton Worldwide* [2025] SGCA 14 at [26]). The court asks two questions: (a) what was within the submission, looking mainly at whether the issue was properly pleaded; (b) whether the award dealt with something outside it (*Bloomberry* [2021] SGCA 94 at [69(a)]; *India Glycols v Texan Minerals* [2025] SGHC 28 at [52]–[53]). Once excess is shown, no prejudice need be shown ([54]).
- **Why the label matters:** an ultra petita challenge makes the court scrutinise the arbitral record in detail (pleadings, list of issues, opening statements, evidence, closing submissions). An infra petita challenge is a sui generis natural justice breach and needs far less of that inquiry (*Palm Grove* at [27]; *CDM v CDP* [2021] 2 SLR 235 at [18]; *Maritima v Pacific Richfield* [2013] 4 SLR 972 at [125]). A non-participating party cannot raise infra petita (*DEM v DEL*).
- **Infra petita** (the tribunal failed to deal with a point) is a separate natural justice challenge (*Palm Grove* at [27]; *DEM v DEL* [2025] SGCA 1). It has four conditions (box) (*DKT v DKU* [2025] SGCA 23 at [8]; *ONI Global v GNC Holdings* [2026] SGCA(I) 3 at [60]). Silence on a pleaded point or on evidence is not itself failure to consider it (*Glaziers Engineering v WCS* [2018] SGCA 66 at [36]; *DPT v DPV* [2025] SGHC(I) 29 at [196]–[197]).

> **Infra petita: all four needed**
> - (1) the point was properly before the tribunal (*Ebixcash v Ashok Kumar Goel* [2025] SGHC(I) 23 at [36], [82]);
> - (2) it was essential to resolving the dispute (no duty to address every point);
> - (3) the tribunal completely failed to consider it, a clear and virtually inescapable inference (*Prayudh Mahagitsiri v Nestlé* [2025] SGHC 181 at [43]);
> - (4) real or actual prejudice resulted.

*Source note: ARB-SN p75–76 paras 235–242; [2025] SGCA 14 at [26]–[27]; [2021] SGCA 94 at [69(a)]; [2025] SGHC 28 at [52]–[54]; [2025] SGCA 23 at [8]; [2026] SGCA(I) 3 at [60]; [2025] SGHC 181 at [43]–[44]; [2018] SGCA 66 at [36]; [2025] SGHC(I) 29 at [196]–[197]. Conflict: ARB-SN cites ONI Global at [59] for the four conditions; they are at [60].*

### drp-m-0-145 — When prejudice must be shown
*Keywords: prejudice, jurisdictional challenge no prejudice required, ST Group v Sanum, wrong seat, procedural challenge prejudice required, natural justice prejudice*

**End point:** Jurisdictional challenges generally do not require prejudice. A tribunal wrongly construing the seat in the arbitration agreement affects enforcement without the debtor having to show prejudice (*ST Group v Sanum Investments* [2019] SGCA 65 at [103]). Procedural challenges, including natural justice (s 24(b)) and infra petita, generally require prejudice.

*Source note: ARB-SN p72 paras 228–229; [2019] SGCA 65 at [103]. Conflict: ARB-SN cites ST Group at [105]; the holding is at [103].*

### drp-m-0-146 — Copied awards (DJP v DJO)
*Keywords: copying, copy and paste, parallel arbitrations, DJO v DJP, DJP v DJO, prejudgment, apparent bias, anchoring bias, confirmation bias, information asymmetry, natural justice, DOI v DOJ*

**End point:** The complaint arises where a tribunal allegedly copied large parts of awards in parallel arbitrations on similar issues (*DOI v DOJ* [2025] SGHC(I) 15, which ARB-SN para 233 cites alongside *DJP v DJO* for this situation). Copying alone does not taint an award; the court looks at the nature, extent and effect of the copying (*DJO v DJP* [2024] SGHC(I) 24 at [98], [114]–[115]; award set aside). On appeal (*DJP v DJO* [2025] SGCA(I) 2) the CA distinguished two sources ([40]–[46]):
- **From one party's submissions:** does the copying give rise to a reasonable suspicion that the tribunal adopted that party's case without engaging fairly with both sides?
- **From a related award:** copying uncontroversial material may be acceptable. Proximity matters, such as the same tribunal or parties.
Using extraneous material that seems to have influenced the outcome, which other members did not share, can taint the whole award ([61]–[63]). Copying parallel awards may lead a fair-minded observer to suspect a closed mind through anchoring and confirmation bias ([71], [74]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p73–74 paras 233–234; [2024] SGHC(I) 24 at [98], [114]–[115]; [2025] SGCA(I) 2 at [40]–[46], [61]–[63], [71], [74]*

### drp-m-0-147 — Inadequate reasons are not a ground (CVV v CWB)
*Keywords: reasons, Art 31(2), inadequate reasons, judicial standard, error of law, CVV v CWB, natural justice, master of procedure*

**End point:** Arbitrators need not give reasons to a judicial standard. Inadequate reasons are, without more, an error of law, which cannot sustain a challenge (*CVV v CWB* [2023] SGCA(I) 9 at [33]–[35]). The CA left open whether a complete failure to give reasons under Art 31(2) is itself a ground ([32]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p72–73 para 230; [2023] SGCA(I) 9 at [32]–[35]. Conflict: ARB-SN says the CA "decided" the Art 31(2) question; at [32] it said the point is unsettled and did not decide it.*

### drp-m-0-148 — No court intervention mid-arbitration (Art 5; DMZ v DNA)
*Keywords: Art 5 Model Law, no court intervention, procedural determination, ongoing arbitration, DMZ v DNA, two-step inquiry, matter governed by Model Law, date of commencement*

**End point:** No court may intervene in matters governed by the Model Law except where it provides (Art 5). A two-step inquiry applies (*DMZ v DNA* [2025] SGCA 52 at [28]):
- (a) is the application about a "matter" governed by the Model Law? "Matter" is read broadly ([35]–[36]) and includes challenges to procedural determinations affecting an ongoing arbitration ([43]);
- (b) is intervention permitted, because legislation expressly provides for it or, if the matter is not governed, because relief is warranted?
Absent agreement on a procedural point, the parties must arbitrate in accordance with the tribunal's procedural determinations, and the court will not second-guess them ([46]). An application to set aside a decision on the arbitration's commencement date failed.

*Source note: ARB-SN p71–72 para 227; [2025] SGCA 52 at [28], [35]–[36], [43], [46]. Conflict: ARB-SN describes DMZ v DNA ([2025] 2 SLR 398) as a High Court decision; the paragraphs relied on are the Court of Appeal's, [2025] SGCA 52 (the High Court decision is [2025] SGHC 31).*

### drp-m-0-149 — Time limit: 3 months, no extension
*Keywords: setting aside time limit, 3 months, Art 34(3), from receipt of award, Art 33 request, no extension, fraud, Bloomberry [2021] SGCA 9, O 48 r 2(3), resist enforcement still available*

**End point:** An application to set aside must be made within 3 months of receiving the award or, if an Art 33 request was made, of its disposal (Art 34(3); ROC O 48 r 2(3)). The court has no power to extend time, even for fraud discovered later (*Bloomberry Resorts v Global Gaming Philippines* [2021] SGCA 9 at [11]–[13]). The award debtor can still resist enforcement after the time has passed.

*Source note: MAL Art 34(3); ROC O 48 r 2(3); ARB-SN p76–77 para 243; [2021] SGCA 9 at [11]–[13]; PYP23 p22 Q29 (no extension), p27 Q34. Note: [2021] SGCA 9 (time limit) and [2021] SGCA 94 (functus officio, ultra petita) are different Bloomberry decisions.*

### drp-m-0-150 — Court's discretion not to set aside
*Keywords: discretion, may set aside, Art 34(2), residual discretion, refuse to set aside despite ground*

**End point:** Art 34(2) says the court "may" set aside, so it keeps a discretion not to do so even where a ground is made out. An example is where the breach could not have affected the outcome.

*Source note: MAL Art 34(2); ARB-SN p77 para 244*

### drp-m-0-151 — Outcomes: set aside, sever, remit (Art 34(4))
*Keywords: consequences of setting aside, set aside in whole or part, remission, Art 34(4), suspend setting aside proceedings, eliminate grounds, CBS v CBP, Vietnam Oil and Gas v Joint Stock Company, Wan Sern Metal, unpleaded defence, fair-minded observer*

**End point:** The court may:
- set aside the award in whole or in part (severing what was not submitted, Art 34(2)(a)(iii));
- refuse to set aside;
- on a party's request and where appropriate, suspend the proceedings so the tribunal can resume or act to eliminate the grounds (remission, Art 34(4));
- vary parts of the award: ARB-SN para 245 lists this. Neither Art 34 nor the IAA gives a Singapore court an express power to vary an award, so if a limb says the court "may vary parts of the award", treat it as true by the Notes but recognise that in practice the court's tools are partial setting aside (severance) and remission.
The CA could not remit where remission was not raised below (*CBS v CBP* [2021] SGCA 4). The factors for remission are set out below.

> **Remission factors (Vietnam Oil and Gas v Joint Stock Co [2025] SGCA 50 at [111]–[130])**
> - whether the defect can be cured at all. A decision in excess of jurisdiction on an unpleaded defence should not be remitted (*Wan Sern Metal v Hua Tian Engineering* [2025] SGCA 5 at [57]; [112]);
> - whether a fair-minded observer would reasonably apprehend that the tribunal cannot give a fair process on the remitted issues ([113]);
> - how central the affected issue is ([125]);
> - whether pleadings would need amending ([128]);
> - time and costs saved, which matter little if confidence in the tribunal is lost ([130]).

*Source note: MAL Art 34(4); ARB-SN p77–78 para 245. Conflict: ARB-SN lists "vary parts of the award"; MAL Art 34 and IAA ss 24, 19B confer no express power to vary, so the pack states the Notes' list and flags the gap; [2021] SGCA 4; [2025] SGCA 50 at [111]–[130]; [2025] SGCA 5 at [57]; PYP23 p28 Q35*

### drp-m-0-152 — A foreign award cannot be set aside in Singapore
*Keywords: foreign award, set aside only at seat, Art 1(2), Singapore court cannot set aside London award, resist enforcement, adjournment*

**End point:** Setting aside is available only from a court at the seat. A Singapore court cannot set aside an award made in, say, London (Art 1(2)). The debtor's options in Singapore are to resist enforcement (s 31) or ask for an adjournment while the seat court decides (s 31(5)).

*Source note: ARB-SN p78 para 246; MAL Art 1(2); IAA s 31(5); PYP23 p26 Q33*

- **Does the NYC apply? Reservations?** Foreign or non-domestic awards (Art I(1)); reciprocity and commercial reservations (Art I(3)) → [0-155](#drp-m-0-155)

### drp-m-0-153 — Choice of remedies: setting aside and resisting are cumulative
*Keywords: choice of remedies, active and passive remedies, cumulative, First Media v Astro, set aside at seat, resist enforcement, both, Registrar's Circular No 2 of 2025*

**End point:** Under Singapore law, setting aside (only at the seat) and resisting enforcement (wherever enforcement is sought) are cumulative. The Model Law's "choice of remedies" lets a party actively seek setting aside, passively resist enforcement, or do both (*PT First Media v Astro* [2013] SGCA 57 at [71]). Not applying to set aside does not stop the debtor resisting enforcement, and vice versa. The Registrar's Circular No 2 of 2025 guides the conduct of arbitration originating applications for both.

*Source note: ARB-SN p69–70 paras 220–222; [2013] SGCA 57 at [71]; PYP23 p27 Q34*

### drp-m-0-154 — Enforcing a Singapore-seated award (s 19 IAA; First Media)
*Keywords: enforcement of Singapore award, IAA s 19, permission of High Court, judgment in terms, domestic international award, Art 36 grounds, First Media v Astro, Chapter VIII excluded, s 18 consent award*

**End point:** An award made in a Singapore-seated IAA arbitration may, with the High Court's permission, be enforced like a judgment, and judgment may be entered in its terms (IAA s 19; consent awards: s 18). The IAA lists no grounds for refusing permission. But the Art 36(1) grounds are available to resist enforcement of a Singapore-seated international award under s 19 (*PT First Media v Astro* [2013] SGCA 57 at [84], [86]–[87]). Chapter VIII was excluded only to preserve the NYC reciprocity regime, not to remove the choice of remedies.

*Source note: IAA ss 18–19; ARB-SN p79 paras 247–248; [2013] SGCA 57 at [84], [86]–[87]; PYP23 p9 Q16 (s 19 IAA read with Art 36)*

### drp-m-0-155 — New York Convention scope and reservations
*Keywords: New York Convention, Art I(1), foreign awards, non-domestic awards, Art I(3), reciprocity reservation, commercial reservation, Art III obligation to recognise and enforce, India, Indonesia, China, Malaysia, Vietnam*

**End point:** The NYC applies to awards made in another State and to awards not considered domestic where enforcement is sought (Art I(1)). A State may declare two reservations (Art I(3)):
- **reciprocity:** apply it only to awards made in another Contracting State. Many in the region have, including India, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, China, Malaysia, New Zealand, Pakistan, the Philippines, Singapore and Vietnam;
- **commercial:** apply it only to legal relationships considered commercial under its law.
Contracting States must recognise awards as binding and enforce them (Art III). The NYC deals with enforcement, not setting aside (SampleQs Arb Q4).

*Source note: NYC Arts I, III; ARB-SN p79–80 paras 249–252; SampleQs p56 Q4 (key p122: B)*

### drp-m-0-156 — Foreign awards: meaning and recognition (ss 27, 29 IAA)
*Keywords: foreign award, IAA s 27(1), Convention country other than Singapore, s 29, enforce by action, same manner as s 19, recognised as binding, defence, set-off*

**End point:** A "foreign award" is an award made under an arbitration agreement in the territory of a Convention country other than Singapore (IAA s 27(1)). It may be enforced in the High Court by action, or like a Singapore award under s 19 (s 29(1)). It is recognised as binding between the parties, and can be relied on by way of defence or set-off (s 29(2)).

*Source note: IAA ss 27(1), 29; ARB-SN p81 para 257*

### drp-m-0-157 — Documents the enforcing party must produce (s 30; Art IV)
*Keywords: documents for enforcement, IAA s 30, duly authenticated original award, certified copy, original arbitration agreement, certified translation English, sworn translator, Art IV NYC, prima facie evidence*

**End point:** The party enforcing a foreign award must produce three documents to the court (IAA s 30(1); NYC Art IV):
- (a) the duly authenticated original award, or a duly certified copy;
- (b) the original arbitration agreement, or a duly certified copy;
- (c) if either is in a foreign language, a certified English translation.
On mere production these are prima facie evidence of what they record (s 30(2)).

*Source note: IAA s 30; ARB-SN p81 para 258; ROC O 48 r 6*

### drp-m-0-158 — Procedure: permission without notice, then 14 days (ROC O 48)
*Keywords: enforcement procedure, two stages, without notice application, permission to enforce, affidavit, exhibit arbitration agreement and award, served on debtor, 14 days to set aside order, served out of Singapore, judgment in terms, ROC O 48 r 6, Hulley v Russia*

**End point:** Enforcement under IAA Parts II and III has two stages (*Hulley Enterprises v Russian Federation* [2025] SGHC(I) 27 at [30]–[31]):
- (1) the creditor applies without notice for permission, by affidavit exhibiting the agreement and the award, stating the parties' names and places of business and the extent of non-compliance. Permission is usually granted on paper within days, and the order is served on the debtor;
- (2) within 14 days of service, or a period the court fixes if served out of Singapore, the debtor may apply to set aside the order, and the award may not be enforced meanwhile (ROC O 48 r 6).
If there is no challenge, the creditor enters judgment in terms of the award and executes. If there is a challenge, an inter partes hearing decides the s 31 (or Art 36/s 19) grounds.

*Source note: ROC O 48 r 6; ARB-SN p81–82 paras 259–263; [2025] SGHC(I) 27 at [30]–[31]*

### drp-m-0-159 — Grounds the award debtor must prove (s 31(2))
*Keywords: refusal of enforcement, IAA s 31(2), Art V(1) NYC, Art 36(1)(a), incapacity, invalid arbitration agreement, law of country where award made, proper notice, unable to present case, beyond scope, s 31(3) severance, composition, arbitral procedure, not binding, set aside or suspended*

**End point:** Enforcement may be refused only on the listed grounds (s 31(1)). Those in s 31(2) are for the award debtor to prove (box). Section 31(2)(b) is the ground used when the debtor says there was no valid arbitration agreement, i.e. no jurisdiction.

> **IAA s 31(2) (NYC Art V(1); Art 36(1)(a)): the debtor proves that**
> - (a) a party was, under the law applicable to it, under some incapacity at the time the agreement was made;
> - (b) the agreement is not valid under the law the parties chose or, failing that, the law of the country where the award was made;
> - (c) it was not given proper notice of the appointment of the arbitrator or of the proceedings, or was otherwise unable to present its case;
> - (d) the award deals with a difference not within the submission, or decides matters beyond it. Under s 31(3), severable parts within the submission may be enforced;
> - (e) the composition of the tribunal or the procedure was not as agreed or, failing agreement, not in accordance with the law of the seat;
> - (f) the award is not yet binding, or has been set aside or suspended by a competent authority of the country in which, or under whose law, it was made.

*Source note: IAA s 31(1)–(3); NYC Art V(1); ARB-SN p83–84 paras 267–269*

### drp-m-0-160 — Grounds the court may raise itself (s 31(4))
*Keywords: IAA s 31(4), Art V(2), not capable of settlement by arbitration under the law of Singapore, contrary to public policy of Singapore, court's own initiative, forum's public policy*

**End point:** The court may also refuse enforcement if it finds that:
- (a) the subject matter is not capable of settlement by arbitration under Singapore law; or
- (b) enforcement would be contrary to Singapore public policy (IAA s 31(4); NYC Art V(2)).
It may raise these itself. It applies its own State's public policy, not public policy peculiar to other States.

*Source note: IAA s 31(4); ARB-SN p84 para 270, p88 para 285*

### drp-m-0-161 — Residual discretion to enforce despite a ground
*Keywords: residual discretion, may refuse, Art V(1), s 31(1), enforce despite irregularity, no prejudice, Werner A Bock v N's Co, Art 4 waiver*

**End point:** Even if a ground is proved, enforcement "may" be refused, so the court keeps a discretion to enforce (NYC Art V(1); IAA s 31(1)–(2)). For example, it may enforce where an irregularity in composition or procedure could not have affected the outcome or caused no prejudice (*Werner A Bock v N's Co*, HK CA). A party that did not object in time to a procedural irregularity may have waived it (Art 4).

*Source note: ARB-SN p83 para 268, p87 paras 280–281; IAA s 31(2) ("may refuse"); PYP23 p29 Q36 ("may"). Werner A Bock is a Hong Kong case taken from ARB-SN; not checked*

### drp-m-0-162 — Fresh examination, but no review of the merits
*Keywords: standard of review enforcement, de novo, fresh examination, First Media [164], no review of merits, errors of law or fact final, PT Asuransi [57]*

**End point:** The enforcement court cannot review the merits. Errors of law or fact are final (*PT Asuransi v Dexia* [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]). It is, however, entitled to examine afresh the issues said to establish a ground of refusal, e.g. whether there was a valid arbitration agreement (*PT First Media* [2013] SGCA 57 at [164]).

*Source note: ARB-SN p83 paras 266–267; [2006] SGCA 41 at [57]; [2013] SGCA 57 at [164]. Conflict: ARB-SN para 266 cites PT Asuransi as "[2016] SGCA 41"; the citation is [2006] SGCA 41.*

### drp-m-0-163 — Award set aside at the seat
*Keywords: award set aside at seat, s 31(2)(f), Art V(1)(e), First Media dicta, erga omnes, nullity, Hilmarton, Chromalloy, France, United States*

**End point:** Enforcement may be refused if the award has been set aside or suspended by a competent court of the seat (IAA s 31(2)(f); NYC Art V(1)(e)). The Singapore CA said, obiter, that Singapore courts will not enforce a foreign award set aside at the seat. The erga omnes effect of annulment leaves no award to enforce (*PT First Media* [2013] SGCA 57 at [76]–[77]). Some courts take a different view: France enforced an award set aside in Switzerland (*Hilmarton*), and the US one set aside in Egypt (*Chromalloy*).

*Source note: IAA s 31(2)(f); ARB-SN p69 para 221, p87–88 paras 282–284; [2013] SGCA 57 at [76]–[77] (Hilmarton and Chromalloy are foreign cases taken from ARB-SN; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-164 — Transnational issue estoppel (Deutsche Telekom)
*Keywords: transnational issue estoppel, Republic of India v Deutsche Telekom, seat court decision preclusive, Merck v Merck, elements, primacy principle, obiter, Hulley v Russian Federation, Sacofa v Super Sea Cable*

**End point:** Transnational issue estoppel applies in international arbitration. A seat court's earlier decision on the validity of an award binds the parties in Singapore enforcement proceedings, so they cannot relitigate issues the seat court decided (*Republic of India v Deutsche Telekom* [2023] SGCA(I) 10 at [64]). The majority added, obiter, a "primacy principle": the seat court's decision on validity is presumed final and determinative, subject to public policy or evidential concerns ([46] ff). The concurring judge found issue estoppel and abuse of process sufficient. It was applied in *Hulley Enterprises v Russian Federation* [2025] SGHC(I) 19 at [111], [140], where Russia was estopped by the Dutch courts' decisions. The court held (1) *Deutsche Telekom* was binding ([111]); (2) transnational issue estoppel is consistent with de novo review of jurisdiction ([140]); and (3) because a State's consent to an arbitration agreement is a private act, no consideration "trumped" the estoppel.

> **Elements (Deutsche Telekom at [64]; Sacofa v Super Sea Cable [2024] SGHC 54 at [65])**
> - (a) the foreign judgment is capable of recognition in Singapore:
>   - (i) final and conclusive on the merits;
>   - (ii) from a court of competent jurisdiction over the party to be bound;
>   - (iii) not subject to any defence to recognition;
> - (b) the same parties;
> - (c) the same subject matter as decided before.

*Source note: ARB-SN p80–81 paras 253–256; [2023] SGCA(I) 10 at [46] ff, [64]; [2024] SGHC 54 at [65]; [2025] SGHC(I) 19 at [111], [140]*

### drp-m-0-165 — Public policy: narrow; illegality (AJU v AJT matrix)
*Keywords: public policy, shock the conscience, clearly injurious to the public good, wholly offensive, most basic notions of morality and justice, PT Asuransi [59], Sanum v Laos, VV v VW, procedural fraud, CZD v CZE, illegality, AJU v AJT, CBX v CBZ four scenarios, palpable and indisputable illegality, Gokul Patnaik, GNC v ONI, deliberate falsity, DKB v DKC assumed facts*

**End point:** Singapore public policy is narrow. It applies only where upholding the award would "shock the conscience", be "clearly injurious to the public good", be "wholly offensive to the ordinary reasonable and fully informed member of the public", or violate the forum's "most basic notion of morality and justice" (*PT Asuransi* [2006] SGCA 41 at [59]; *Sanum Investments v Government of the Lao PDR* [2022] 4 SLR 198 at [45]; *Government of the Lao PDR v Lao Holdings NV* [2024] SGHC(I) 9 at [64]–[69]).
- **Three routes to a public policy challenge (ARB-SN para 289):** (1) the remedies ordered are contrary to public policy; (2) the award gives effect to an illegal or tainted underlying contract, the commonest, where the court must first decide whether to reopen the tribunal's illegality finding (matrix below); (3) the way the award was procured is contrary to public policy (procedural fraud).
- **Not a second bite:** a party cannot use public policy to undermine an award on a point it disavowed before the tribunal, or raised and lost (*Reliance Infrastructure v Shanghai Electric* [2024] SGCA(I) 10 at [75]; *DFM v DFL* [2024] SGCA 41 at [45]; *China Machine v Jaguar Energy* [2020] 1 SLR 695 at [168]–[170]).
- **Not public policy:** a disproportionate costs award (*VV v VW* [2008] SGHC 11).
- **Procedural fraud:** can qualify, but must be material to the award (*CZD v CZE* [2023] SGHC 86 at [38]). Relitigating how an award was procured needs evidence of deliberate falsity on material matters that was not reasonably available at the hearing (*GNC Holdings v ONI Global* [2025] SGHC(I) 25 at [155], [158]; affd [2026] SGCA(I) 3 at [51]).
- **Facts:** the court will not decide on assumed facts (*DKB v DKC* [2025] SGHC(I) 26 at [7]).
- **Illegality:** only a tribunal's error about the content of the forum's public policy may be reopened (*AJU v AJT* [2011] SGCA 41). The CA has not yet considered the matrix below. Even where some illegality exists, it may not reach the public policy threshold (*Gokul Patnaik v Nine Rivers* [2020] SGHC(I) 23 at [203], [206]).
Other States are broader (comparative, ARB-SN para 295): India's Supreme Court in *ONGC v Saw Pipes* added an all-encompassing ground of "patent illegality"; the Philippine CA in *Luzon Hydro* refused a Singapore-seated award applying costs-follow-the-event, because Philippine public policy does not make a bona fide litigant bear the other side's costs. Contrast *VV v VW* in Singapore.

> **CBX v CBZ [2020] SGHC(I) 17 at [67]: can the court reopen the tribunal's finding on illegality?**
> - Singapore law contract, tribunal found **not illegal** → court may intervene.
> - Singapore law, found **illegal** → no.
> - Foreign law, found **illegal** → no.
> - Foreign law, found **not illegal** → no, unless the illegality is "palpable and indisputable".

*Source note: ARB-SN p88–91 paras 285–295 (three routes: p89 paras 289–290; comparative: p91 para 295; Saw Pipes and Luzon Hydro are foreign cases taken from ARB-SN, not checked); [2006] SGCA 41 at [59]; [2022] 4 SLR 198 at [45] (cited as in ARB-SN; the earlier pack cite "[2022] SGHC(I) 9" was dropped because it could not be confirmed and may confuse Sanum with Lao Holdings [2024] SGHC(I) 9); [2024] SGHC(I) 9 at [64]–[69] (as stated in ARB-SN); [2024] SGCA(I) 10 at [75]; [2024] SGCA 41 at [45]; [2008] SGHC 11; [2023] SGHC 86 at [38]; [2025] SGHC(I) 25 at [155], [158]; [2026] SGCA(I) 3 at [51]; [2025] SGHC(I) 26 at [7]; [2011] SGCA 41; [2020] SGHC(I) 17 at [56], [67]; [2020] SGHC(I) 23 at [203], [206]; PYP23 p10 Q17 (most basic notions of morality and justice). Conflict: ARB-SN cites CZD v CZE at [42], [59] for procedural fraud; the statement is at [38].*

### drp-m-0-166 — Due process ground (Art V(1)(b)): what succeeds and fails
*Keywords: Art V(1)(b), due process, unable to present case, s 31(2)(c), natural justice examples, Front Row v Daimler, AKN v ALC, L W Infrastructure, BZW v BZV, chain of reasoning, misunderstood case, failed arguments, disguised appeal*

**End point:** A party unable to present its case (no proper notice; restricted hearing) may resist enforcement (IAA s 31(2)(c); NYC Art V(1)(b); the setting-aside counterpart is Art 34(2)(a)(ii)). The threshold is high. Some complaints have succeeded and some have failed (box). Failed complaints are often disguised attacks on errors of fact or law.

> **Succeeded (ARB-SN para 277)**
> - tribunal failed to consider an important pleaded issue (*Front Row Investment v Daimler* [2010] SGHC 80);
> - no opportunity to address a claim raised at the eleventh hour, which the tribunal knew had not been addressed (*AKN v ALC*);
> - no opportunity to address issues for an Art 33 additional award (*L W Infrastructure*);
> - a chain of reasoning the parties had no reasonable notice of, or with no nexus to their arguments (*BZW v BZV* [2022] SGCA 1).
>
> **Failed (para 278)**
> - "the tribunal misunderstood my case";
> - "the tribunal did not mention my arguments, so it ignored them";
> - "it must have overlooked part of my case because it did not engage with it".

*Source note: IAA s 31(2)(c); ARB-SN p84–87 paras 271–278; [2010] SGHC 80; [2022] SGCA 1 (AKN v ALC and L W Infrastructure are named in ARB-SN without citation; not checked)*

### drp-m-0-167 — Adjourning enforcement pending a challenge at the seat (s 31(5))
*Keywords: adjournment of enforcement, IAA s 31(5), Art VI NYC, pending setting aside at seat, suitable security, Man Diesel & Turbo v IM Skaugen, Aastar Trading v Olam, bona fide application, sliding scale merits, comity*

**End point:** If an application to set aside or suspend the award is pending before the competent authority of the seat, the enforcement court may, if it thinks it proper, adjourn the enforcement proceedings. On the creditor's application it may order the debtor to give suitable security (IAA s 31(5); NYC Art VI). The discretion is wide (*Man Diesel & Turbo v IM Skaugen Marine* [2018] SGHC 132 at [45]–[46], [62]; *Aastar Trading v Olam Global Agri* [2025] SGHC 5 at [47]). The preliminary question is whether the seat application is bona fide and not a delaying tactic (*Man Diesel* at [49], [54]; *Aastar* at [48]). In *Man Diesel* adjournment was refused and immediate enforcement allowed.

> **Factors (Man Diesel; Aastar at [59]–[61]), weighed to reach the most just or least unjust outcome**
> - the merits of the seat challenge, on a sliding scale;
> - the likely consequences and length of an adjournment;
> - international comity;
> - all other circumstances (highly fact-sensitive).

*Source note: IAA s 31(5); NYC Art VI; ARB-SN p91–92 paras 296–299; [2018] SGHC 132 at [45]–[46], [49], [54], [62]; [2025] SGHC 5 at [47]–[48], [59], [61]; PYP23 p26 Q33 (London award, set-aside pending: Singapore court may adjourn)*

### drp-m-0-168 — More favourable right provision (Art VII(1))
*Keywords: Art VII(1) NYC, more favourable right, bilateral and multilateral agreements, domestic law more favourable, IAA s 33(1)*

**End point:** The NYC does not affect other multilateral or bilateral treaties on enforcing awards. Nor does it deprive a party of any right to rely on an award as far as the law or treaties of the enforcing country allow (Art VII(1)). This is the "more favourable right" provision: where domestic law is more favourable to enforcement than the NYC, the more favourable right prevails. In Singapore, Part III does not affect other ways of enforcing an award (IAA s 33(1)).

*Source note: NYC Art VII(1); IAA s 33(1); ARB-SN p82 para 264*

## 1.I Nature of mediation

- **How does mediation differ from negotiation, arbitration and litigation?** Parties decide, interest-based, consensual, confidential, cheaper and quicker, solution- and future-focused, binding settlement agreement v rights-based adjudication → [1-1](#drp-m-1-1)
- **What is mediation? Possible outcomes?** Consensual, neutral mediator, parties decide, confidential, without prejudice, voluntary; full / partial / no settlement, or narrowed issues → [1-2](#drp-m-1-2)
- **Benefits?** Client: speed, control, more solutions, cost, confidentiality, relationship, choice of mediator. Counsel: client satisfaction, testing the case → [1-3](#drp-m-1-3)
- **Disadvantages?** No certainty of settlement, no precedent, no formal discovery → [1-4](#drp-m-1-4)
- **Facilitative or evaluative mediator?** Facilitative: process expert, interests, less intervention. Evaluative: subject expert, legal rights, likely court outcome, more intervention → [1-5](#drp-m-1-5)
- **What does the advocate do before and during a dispute?** Advise on the O 5 duty and ADR clauses; assess and propose mediation; prepare the client and opening statement; assist the mediator; draft the settlement → [1-6](#drp-m-1-6)

### drp-m-1-1 — Mediation compared with negotiation, arbitration and litigation
*Keywords: mediation v negotiation, mediation v arbitration, mediation v litigation, decision-maker, interest-based, rights-based, consensual, adversarial, confidential, public forum, binding settlement agreement, binding award, binding judgment, costs, duration, future relationship, past events*

**End point:** In negotiation and mediation the parties decide. The process is interest-based, consensual, problem-solving and confidential, relatively inexpensive and short. It focuses on a solution and a possible future relationship. The MED-1 table lists "Binding Settlement Agreement" only under mediation, not under negotiation, so a limb saying negotiation ends in a binding settlement agreement should be treated as wrong by the deck (in law a negotiated agreement can still be a binding contract, 1-39). In arbitration and litigation a neutral arbitrator or judge decides. The process is rights-based and adversarial, more expensive and longer, and looks at legal rights and past events. Arbitration is confidential and ends in a binding award; litigation is public and ends in a binding judgment.

*Source note: MED-1 p4 (comparison table); SampleQs p60 Q2 (key p122: D, mediation focuses on solutions and possible future relationship); PYP21-MED Q1.9(a)*

### drp-m-1-2 — What mediation is; possible outcomes
*Keywords: what is mediation, consensual, party-initiated, neutral independent mediator, parties decide outcome, confidential, without prejudice, voluntary, outcomes, full settlement, partial settlement, no settlement, narrowing of issues*

**End point:** Mediation is a consensual, party-initiated dispute resolution process. Neutral, independent mediators facilitate it, the disputants decide the outcome, and it is confidential, without prejudice and voluntary. The possible outcomes are a full or partial settlement agreement, no settlement, or a narrowing of the issues. Failing to settle does not end the chance to settle later (SampleQs Med Q3). The statutory definition is in MA s 3 (1-28).

*Source note: MED-1 p5; SampleQs p60 Q3 (key p122: C is the untrue statement — no settlement does not mean the parties must litigate without further chance to settle); PYP23 p32 Q39*

### drp-m-1-3 — Benefits for the client and for counsel
*Keywords: benefits of mediation, quicker resolution, client control of outcome, more possible solutions, less costly, confidential, preserve relationship, choice of mediator, counsel benefits, client satisfaction, test strengths and weaknesses*

**End point:** For the client, mediation offers:
- quicker resolution;
- control of the outcome;
- more possible solutions;
- lower cost;
- confidentiality;
- preserving or improving the relationship;
- a choice of mediator.
For counsel, it may bring greater client satisfaction and a chance to test the strengths and weaknesses of the client's case if litigation or arbitration follows.

*Source note: MED-1 p9–10; PYP23 p33 Q40*

### drp-m-1-4 — Disadvantages of mediation
*Keywords: disadvantages of mediation, no certainty of settlement, no legal precedent, confidentiality, no formal discovery, disclosure not compellable*

**End point:** Mediation has three disadvantages:
- no certainty of settlement, whereas litigation or arbitration ends in an order or award;
- no legal precedent, because the process is confidential, so good solutions do not guide future disputes;
- no formal discovery to compel disclosure of pertinent information.

*Source note: MED-1 p11*

### drp-m-1-5 — Facilitative v evaluative mediation
*Keywords: facilitative mediation, interest-based, problem-solving, evaluative mediation, advisory, managerial, legal rights, anticipated range of court outcomes, mediator expertise, process expertise, subject-matter expertise, intervention, party control*

**End point:** The two styles differ in aim, expertise and intervention (box). The 2021 paper asked which type suits a given dispute: for example, partners in a business with a relationship to preserve.

> **Facilitative v evaluative (MED-1 p12)**
> - **Facilitative** (interest-based, problem-solving):
>   - aim: avoid legal positions; negotiate on underlying needs and interests;
>   - mediator: expert in the mediation process, not necessarily the subject matter;
>   - role: conducts the process, keeps dialogue constructive, enhances negotiation;
>   - less intervention; the parties fashion creative outcomes.
> - **Evaluative** (advisory, managerial):
>   - aim: settle according to legal rights, within the likely range of court outcomes;
>   - mediator: expert in the subject matter, not necessarily trained in mediation;
>   - role: gives information, advises and persuades;
>   - more intervention; less party control.

*Source note: MED-1 p12; PYP21-MED Q1.4*

### drp-m-1-6 — The advocate's evolving role, before and during a dispute
*Keywords: role of advocate, before dispute, Order 5 duty to consider amicable resolution, ADR clause, assess viability of mediation, propose mediation, prepare client, opening statement, assist mediator, draft settlement agreement*

**End point:**
- **Before a dispute:** advise on the duty to consider amicable resolution under ROC 2021 O 5, and on ADR clauses in contracts.
- **When a dispute arises:**
  - assess whether mediation is viable and propose it where appropriate;
  - prepare for it, including the client and opening statements;
  - assist the mediator while protecting the client's interests;
  - draft any settlement agreement with the other side.

*Source note: MED-1 p8; MED-Plan p1 (CS1 live lecture on the advocate's roles and duties, pre-mediation and during mediation); PYP23 p30 Q37*

### drp-m-1-16 — Mediation by agreement; stay pending ADR
*Keywords: mediation agreement, mediation clause, multi-tier dispute resolution clause, Cable & Wireless v IBM, stay pending ADR, Mediation Act s 8, Heartronics, unitary mechanism, repudiatory breach, Maxx Engineering, specific performance*

**End point:** Mediation can rest on a mediation agreement or a multi-tier clause, and the courts give effect to it in three ways:
- **Stay pending ADR:** proceedings may be stayed pending agreed ADR (*Cable & Wireless v IBM UK* [2002] EWHC 2059 (Comm)). In Singapore a party to a mediation agreement may apply for a stay under MA s 8 (1-31).
- **Repudiation:** repudiating the mediation tier of a unitary med-arb clause can make the arbitration tier inoperative, so a stay is refused (*Heartronics* [2017] SGHCR 17; 0-94).
- **Specific performance:** a clear obligation to mediate may be specifically enforced (*Maxx Engineering* [2023] SGHC 71; 0-93).

*Source note: MED-3 p9–10, p41; MA s 8; [2017] SGHCR 17 at [78]–[80], [168]–[169], [214]; [2023] SGHC 71 (Cable & Wireless is an English case taken from MED-3; not checked)*

## 1.IV Duty to consider amicable resolution; costs

- **Before suing, must a party offer ADR?** Duty to consider amicable resolution before and during proceedings; make an offer before commencing unless reasonable grounds; not reject one without reasonable grounds (O 5 r 1) → [1-19](#drp-m-1-19)
- **Form and life of an offer?** In writing; open at least 14 days; not disclosed to the court until costs; lapses on judgment unless stated (O 5 r 2) → [1-20](#drp-m-1-20)
- **Party refuses to mediate: what can the court do?** Order ADR; order sealed reasons, opened only at costs; suggest solutions (O 5 r 3) → [1-21](#drp-m-1-21)
- **Costs consequences?** Court considers efforts at amicable resolution (O 21 r 2(2)(a)); may reduce a winner's costs for breaching O 5 (O 21 r 4(c)) → [1-22](#drp-m-1-22)
- **Supreme Court PDs?** PD 53: duty to advise ADR, App D, PCQ, warn of adverse costs; PD 54: ADR Offer (Form B4) and Response (B5), sealed document in 7 days → [1-23](#drp-m-1-23)
- **State Courts?** "Presumption of ADR" for all civil claims; opting out for unsatisfactory reasons → costs (PD 34(5), (8)) → [1-24](#drp-m-1-24)
- **Family cases?** Solicitors' duty to advise on mediation at the earliest possible stage (FJC PD 81(2)); PCR r 15A → [1-25](#drp-m-1-25)
- **When is refusing ADR unreasonable?** *Halsey* factors; *Churchill* (court can order ADR); *PGF II* (silence unreasonable); timing (*Nigel Witham*) → [1-26](#drp-m-1-26)
- **Are mediation costs recoverable? Small claims?** Yes (*Legis Point*); advise settling paltry claims at minimum cost (*Lock Han Chng*) → [1-27](#drp-m-1-27)

### drp-m-1-19 — Duty to consider amicable resolution; pre-action offer (O 5 r 1)
*Keywords: ROC 2021 O 5 r 1, duty to consider amicable resolution, before commencement, during proceedings, offer of amicable resolution, reasonable grounds, must not reject, offer to settle, resolve other than by litigation*

**End point:** Under ROC 2021 O 5 r 1, a party has the duty to consider amicable resolution of its dispute before commencing and during any action or appeal (box).

> **ROC 2021 O 5 r 1**
> - (1) A party has the duty to consider amicable resolution before the commencement and during the course of any action or appeal.
> - (2) A party is to make an offer of amicable resolution before commencing the action unless it has reasonable grounds not to.
> - (3) An "offer of amicable resolution" means an offer to settle the action or appeal, or to resolve the dispute other than by litigation, in whole or in part.
> - (4) A party must not reject an offer of amicable resolution unless it has reasonable grounds.

*Source note: ROC O 5 r 1 (in force from 1 Apr 2026 version); MED-3 p13; PYP23 p41 Q48 (O 5 r 1(2))*

### drp-m-1-20 — Terms of an offer of amicable resolution (O 5 r 2)
*Keywords: ROC O 5 r 2, offer in writing, rejection in writing, open for acceptance, 14 days, not made known to court, until costs, expiry, merits determined*

**End point:** The terms of an offer are governed by ROC O 5 r 2 (box).

> **ROC 2021 O 5 r 2**
> - (1) An offer and any rejection must be in writing.
> - (2) The offer must be open for acceptance for a reasonable period, and in any case at least 14 days, unless the parties agree otherwise.
> - (3) The terms of an offer made and not accepted must not be relied on or made known to the Court until it has decided the merits and is dealing with costs.
> - (4) An offer with no expiry date expires once the Court has determined the merits, unless the offeror stated otherwise.

*Source note: ROC O 5 r 2; MED-3 p14. Conflict: CEL and NIC-MED cite ROC 2014 (O 22A offers to settle; O 34A r 6; O 59 r 5(1)(c)); those rules were replaced by ROC 2021 on 1 Apr 2022.*

### drp-m-1-21 — Court's powers: order ADR, sealed reasons for refusal (O 5 r 3)
*Keywords: ROC O 5 r 3, court may order amicable resolution, Ideals, refused to attempt, sealed document, reasons for refusal, opened after merits, costs, suggest solutions, SCPD 54(3), 7 days, ADR Sealed Document*

**End point:** The Court's powers are set out in ROC O 5 r 3 (box). In the Supreme Court the sealed document is filed within 7 days of the order as an "ADR Sealed Document". It is not served, and is seen only when costs are considered (SCPD 54(3)–(4)).

> **ROC 2021 O 5 r 3**
> - (1) The Court may order the parties to attempt to resolve the dispute by amicable resolution.
> - (2) In deciding, it must have regard to the Ideals and all relevant circumstances, including any refusal to attempt amicable resolution.
>   - The Ideals (O 3 r 1(2)): (a) fair access to justice; (b) expeditious proceedings; (c) cost-effective work proportionate to the nature and importance of the action, its complexity and the difficulty or novelty of the issues, and the amount or value of the claim; (d) efficient use of court resources; (e) fair and practical results suited to the parties' needs. The Court must seek to achieve them in all orders (r 1(3)); all parties must help achieve them (r 1(4)).
> - (3) If a party says it does not wish to attempt amicable resolution, the Court may order it to submit a sealed document giving its reasons.
> - (4) The sealed document is opened only after the merits are determined, and may be referred to on costs.
> - (5) The Court may suggest solutions for amicable resolution at any time.

*Source note: ROC O 5 r 3, O 3 r 1(2)–(4); MED-3 p15; SCPD para 54(3)–(4)*

### drp-m-1-22 — Costs consequences (O 21 rr 2(2)(a), 4(c))
*Keywords: costs, ROC O 21 r 2(2)(a), efforts at amicable resolution, O 21 r 4(c), adverse costs order against successful party, duty to consider amicable resolution, disallow or reduce costs, unreasonable refusal*

**End point:** In fixing or assessing costs the Court must consider all relevant circumstances, including the parties' efforts at amicable resolution (ROC O 21 r 2(2)(a)). It may disallow or reduce a *successful* party's costs, or order it to pay costs, if that party has not discharged its duty to consider amicable resolution or to make an offer under O 5 (O 21 r 4(c)). So unreasonably refusing ADR can cost even a winner.

*Source note: ROC O 21 rr 2(2)(a), 4(c); MED-3 p16, p38 (SCPD 53(6) refers to O 21 r 4(c)), p42*

### drp-m-1-23 — Supreme Court PD 53 and 54
*Keywords: Supreme Court Practice Directions 2021, PD 53, professional duty to advise ADR, Appendix D, PCR r 17(2)(e), Pre-Case Conference Questionnaire, PCQ, O 21 r 4(c), adverse costs, PD 54, ADR Offer, Form B4, Response to ADR Offer, Form B5, sealed document 7 days*

**End point:** Supreme Court PD 53 sets out the advocate's duties on ADR; PD 54 the mechanics of an ADR Offer (box).

> **SCPD para 53 (General Division, Appellate Division, Court of Appeal)**
> - (2) It is the professional duty of advocates and solicitors to advise clients to consider ADR and to give them enough information about the different ways ADR could resolve their dispute (see PCR r 17(2)(e)).
> - (3) The Appendix D guidelines on advising clients about ADR apply.
> - (4) ADR must be considered before and during any action or appeal, especially where it may save costs, settle faster and meet the parties' needs better.
> - (5) Advocates must state in the Pre-Case Conference Questionnaire (Section 4) whether, when and how amicable resolution was attempted.
> - (6) Advocates should warn clients of potential adverse costs orders under O 21 r 4(c) for unreasonably refusing ADR.
>
> **SCPD para 54**
> - (1) A party wishing to attempt ADR files and serves an ADR Offer (Form B4); the other side responds with Form B5.
> - (2) If all agree, the Court may give directions, including adjourning proceedings.
> - (3)–(4) A sealed document under O 5 r 3(3) is filed within 7 days as an "ADR Sealed Document", unseen until costs.

> **SCPD Appendix D: Guidelines for advising clients about ADR (summary)**
> - **Alternatives (1):** mediation, neutral evaluation, expert determination, conciliation.
> - **Choosing (2):** consider what the client wants to achieve, advise which route is quickest, most economic and most likely to achieve it, then seek the other side's consent. Neutral evaluation (binding or not) where specialised legal or technical expertise is needed; expert determination where the dispute turns on conflicting experts; conciliation for a broken commercial or personal relationship, notably workplace disputes; mediation for all disputes, provided the disputants are willing. The advocate's duty is to explain mediation's advantages, its process, and what it can achieve beyond court remedies.
> - **Mediation (3):** the client is an active partner, shapes the outcome and has the final say. Ideal cases: ongoing commercial relationships; small construction claims; neighbour disputes; partnership dissolutions; liquidators with limited assets; clinical negligence (the victim is heard and the doctor can apologise); most employment and discrimination cases; any claim where costs would equal or exceed its value. Fraud can be mediated. A test case or precedent-setting case may be unsuitable, but mediation can handle class actions. "A matter of principle" and revenge cases are better suited to mediation than litigation.
> - **Timing (4):** there is no "right" time; the wrong time is when costs have become disproportionate to the claim. Know both sides' cases well enough to advise; agree in advance what documents, quantum details or expert evidence are needed, seeking court directions if needed. Mediation can be at any time, need not finish in a day, and may need more than one attempt; even without settlement, issues are narrowed.
> - **Comparison table:** litigation is public, protracted, formalistic, limited to pleaded issues, expensive, loser often pays, and destroys future relationships; mediation is private and confidential, early, informal, open to any issue, lets emotions be expressed, each party controls its spend and pays its own costs, and often improves future relationships.

*Source note: SCPD paras 53–54 (last updated 15 Jul 2026); SCPD Appendix D (4 pp, OpenDoc mirror of the Supreme Court PDF, read 18 Nov 2026); MED-3 p38–39, p43. Conflict: NIC-MED and CEL cite "Supreme Court Practice Directions 35B" and "Appendix I" (pre-2021 PDs); the current provisions are SCPD 53 and Appendix D, as MED-3 says.*

### drp-m-1-24 — State Courts PD 34: presumption of ADR

### drp-m-1-25 — Family Justice Courts PD 81(2)

### drp-m-1-26 — Unreasonable refusal of ADR: Halsey and later cases
*Keywords: unreasonable refusal of ADR, Halsey v Milton Keynes, Halsey checklist, nature of dispute, merits, other settlement methods, costs disproportionate, delay prejudicial, reasonable prospect of success, Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil, court may order ADR, PGF II v OMFS, silence unreasonable, Nigel Witham v Smith, timing of mediation, happy medium*

**End point:** The courts look to English authority on when refusing ADR is unreasonable (box).

> **Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust [2004] 1 WLR 3002 at [16] (non-exhaustive)**
> - (a) the nature of the dispute;
> - (b) the merits of the case;
> - (c) the extent to which other settlement methods have been tried;
> - (d) whether the costs of ADR would be disproportionately high;
> - (e) whether delay in setting up and attending ADR would be prejudicial;
> - (f) whether ADR had a reasonable prospect of success.
>
> **Later cases**
> - *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil* [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 at [74]: a court may stay proceedings for, or order, non-court ADR, provided it does not impair the essence of the right to a judicial hearing and is proportionate.
> - *PGF II v OMFS* [2014] 1 WLR 1386 at [34]: silence in the face of an invitation to ADR is, as a general rule, itself unreasonable.
> - *Nigel Witham v Smith* [2008] EWHC 12 (TCC) at [32]: timing. Too early wastes time and can harden positions; too late and the costs incurred become the obstacle. Aim for the "happy medium" when both sides know the claim and response but costs are not yet prohibitive.

*Source note: MED-3 p47–51 (English cases taken from MED-3; not checked)*

### drp-m-1-27 — Mediation costs are recoverable; settle small claims (Legis Point; Lock Han Chng)
*Keywords: Legis Point v Tay Choon Ai, costs of mediation work, party-and-party costs, Lock Han Chng v Goh Jessiline, officer of the court, paltry claims, settle with minimum fuss and minimum cost*

**End point:**
- **Legis Point:** the costs of counsel's mediation work can be taken into account in party-and-party costs, like other negotiation work (*Legis Point LLC v Tay Choon Ai* [2017] SGHC 325).
- **Lock Han Chng:** where paltry sums are claimed in court, counsel should exercise the responsibility of an officer of the court and advise clients to settle with minimum fuss and minimum cost (*Lock Han Chng Jonathan v Goh Jessiline* [2007] SGCA 56 at [45]).

*Source note: MED-3 p8, p40, p52; [2017] SGHC 325 (MED-3 cites [2018] 3 SLR 1269); [2007] SGCA 56 at [45]; PYP23 p40 Q47 (Legis Point), p43 Q50 (Lock Han Chng)*

## 1.V Mediation Act 2017

- **Statutory meaning of mediation?** One or more sessions in which mediators help the parties identify issues, generate options, communicate, voluntarily agree (s 3) → [1-28](#drp-m-1-28)
- **Must a mediation agreement be in writing?** Yes, but any recorded form; incorporation by reference (s 4) → [1-29](#drp-m-1-29)
- **Does the Act apply?** Mediation under a mediation agreement wholly or partly in Singapore, or agreed to apply; not statutory or court mediation (s 6) → [1-30](#drp-m-1-30)
- **Sued despite a mediation clause?** Court *may* stay, on terms (s 8) → [1-31](#drp-m-1-31)
- **Can anyone disclose what was said?** Not to a third party (s 9(1)); "mediation communication" = anything said or done, document prepared, information provided, incl. the settlement agreement (s 2(1)) → [1-32](#drp-m-1-32)
  - Without permission: consent; public; danger or child abuse; anonymised research; legal advice; arbitrator-conciliator; court order or law; law enforcement; regulator; offence or illegal purpose (s 9(2)) → [1-33](#drp-m-1-33)
  - With permission: enforcing or disputing the settlement; misconduct complaint; discovery; other justifiable purpose (s 9(3)) → [1-34](#drp-m-1-34)
- **Use it as evidence?** Only with the permission of the court or tribunal; factors: s 9(2), public interest or administration of justice, other matters (ss 10–11) → [1-35](#drp-m-1-35)
- **Without prejudice at common law?** Applies to negotiations to settle even without the label (*Mariwu*); waiver by production (*Greenline-Onyx*; cf *Krishna Kumaran*) → [1-36](#drp-m-1-36)
- **Pre-action settlement: record as a court order?** All parties consent; within 8 weeks; designated provider or certified mediator; written and signed; prescribed information (s 12(1)–(3); MR r 2) → [1-37](#drp-m-1-37)
- **Court may refuse? Effect?** Void or voidable; not capable of settlement; unenforceable term; child's best interests; public policy; recorded → enforced as a judgment (s 12(4)–(5)) → [1-38](#drp-m-1-38)

### drp-m-1-28 — Meaning of "mediation" (s 3)
*Keywords: meaning of mediation, Mediation Act s 3, one or more sessions, mediators assist, identify issues, explore and generate options, communicate, voluntarily reach agreement, session, electronic meeting*

**End point:** "Mediation" is a process of one or more sessions in which one or more mediators help the parties to a dispute to do any of four things, with a view to resolving all or part of it (MA s 3(1); box). A "session" is a meeting of mediator and parties, including preparatory and follow-up activity (s 3(2)), and may be held electronically or by video (s 3(3)).

> **MA s 3(1)**
> - (a) identify the issues in dispute;
> - (b) explore and generate options;
> - (c) communicate with one another;
> - (d) voluntarily reach an agreement.

*Source note: MA s 3; MED-3 p18*

### drp-m-1-29 — Mediation agreement (s 4)
*Keywords: mediation agreement, Mediation Act s 4, refer dispute to mediation, clause or separate agreement, in writing, recorded in any form, incorporation by reference, bill of lading*

**End point:** A "mediation agreement" is an agreement by two or more persons to refer all or part of an existing or future dispute to mediation (MA s 4(1)). It may be a clause or a separate agreement (s 4(2)). It must be in writing (s 4(3)), which is satisfied if its content is recorded in any form, even if concluded orally or by conduct (s 4(4)). A reference to a document containing a mediation clause can incorporate it (s 4(5)–(6)).

*Source note: MA s 4; MED-3 p25 (s 4 query on whether mediation counts as negotiations to settle)*

### drp-m-1-30 — Application of the Act (s 6)
*Keywords: application of Mediation Act, s 6, mediation agreement, wholly or partly in Singapore, Singapore law applies, excluded, written law schemes, court-directed mediation, Minister's order, international settlement agreement s 6(3)*

**End point:** The Act applies to mediation under a mediation agreement where the mediation is wholly or partly conducted in Singapore, or the agreement says the Act or Singapore law applies (MA s 6(1)). It does not apply to:
- (a) mediation or conciliation under any written law;
- (b) mediation conducted by, or directed by, a court, unless extended by Ministerial order;
- (c) excluded classes (s 6(2)).
An international settlement agreement under the SCMA can still be a mediated settlement agreement under the Act (s 6(3)).

*Source note: MA s 6; MED-3 p19*

### drp-m-1-31 — Stay of court proceedings (s 8)
*Keywords: stay of proceedings, Mediation Act s 8, party to mediation agreement, institutes proceedings, court may stay, terms or conditions, interim or supplementary orders, discretionary*

**End point:** If a party to a mediation agreement sues another party on a matter within it, any party may apply to that court for a stay (MA s 8(1)). The court *may* stay on such terms as it thinks fit (s 8(2)). It may make interim or supplementary orders to preserve the parties' rights (s 8(3)). "Party" includes a person claiming through or under one (s 8(4)). The stay is discretionary, unlike the mandatory IAA s 6 stay.

*Source note: MA s 8; MED-3 p20*

### drp-m-1-32 — Confidentiality and "mediation communication" (ss 2(1), 9(1))
*Keywords: confidentiality, Mediation Act s 9(1), must not disclose, mediation communication, third party, s 2(1) definition, anything said or done, document prepared, information provided, includes mediation agreement, mediated settlement agreement, common law confidentiality*

**End point:** Subject to s 9(2)–(3), a person must not disclose any mediation communication relating to a mediation to a third party to it (MA s 9(1)). A "third party" is anyone not a party, not the mediator and not the mediation service provider (s 2(1)). This is wider than common-law confidentiality.

> **MA s 2(1): "mediation communication", in relation to a mediation, means**
> - (a) anything said or done;
> - (b) any document prepared; or
> - (c) any information provided,
> - for the purposes of or in the course of the mediation, and includes a mediation agreement or mediated settlement agreement.

*Source note: MA ss 2(1), 9(1); MED-3 p21–22, p24; MED-5 p13; PYP23 p34 Q41. Conflict: MED-3 p22 and p24 cite the definition as "s 1"; it is in s 2(1).*

### drp-m-1-33 — Disclosure allowed without permission (s 9(2))
*Keywords: exceptions to confidentiality, Mediation Act s 9(2), consent of all parties, maker's consent, public domain, danger of injury, child abuse neglect, research evaluation educational anonymised, legal advice, arbitrator conciliator, order of court, written law, law enforcement, regulatory authority, offence, illegal purpose*

**End point:** A mediation communication may be disclosed to a third party without permission in the cases listed in MA s 9(2) (box).

> **MA s 9(2): disclosure allowed if**
> - (a) all parties consent, and the maker too if it is not a party;
> - (b) the information is already public, other than through an unlawful disclosure;
> - (c) there are reasonable grounds to believe disclosure is necessary to prevent or minimise (i) danger of injury to any person or (ii) abuse, neglect, abandonment or exploitation of a child or young person;
> - (d) it is for research, evaluation or education without revealing identities;
> - (e) it is to seek legal advice;
> - (f) it is by an arbitrator acting as mediator or conciliator, in line with AA s 63 or IAA s 17;
> - (g) it is required by court order or required or authorised by written law;
> - (h) it is to help a law enforcement agency investigate an offence;
> - (i) it complies with a regulator's request or requirement and is necessary for its functions;
> - (j) the communication relates to an offence or was made to further an illegal purpose.

*Source note: MA s 9(2); MED-3 p21, p29 (exceptions noted). The slides only point to s 9(2)–(3); the list is from the statute.*

### drp-m-1-34 — Disclosure with permission of a court or tribunal (s 9(3))
*Keywords: Mediation Act s 9(3), permission of court or arbitral tribunal, enforcing mediated settlement agreement, disputing settlement, professional misconduct complaint against mediator, discovery, justifiable purpose*

**End point:** With the permission of a court or arbitral tribunal under s 11, a mediation communication may be disclosed to a third party for four purposes (MA s 9(3); box). This is how a party proves or attacks a settlement agreement in later proceedings.

> **MA s 9(3)**
> - (a) to enforce or dispute a mediated settlement agreement;
> - (b) to establish or dispute a professional misconduct allegation against the mediator or another professional participant;
> - (c) for discovery in court or arbitral proceedings already instituted;
> - (d) for any other purpose the court or tribunal considers justifiable.

*Source note: MA s 9(3); MED-3 p29*

### drp-m-1-35 — Admissibility in evidence; the permission test (ss 10, 11)
*Keywords: admissibility, Mediation Act s 10, not admitted in evidence, court arbitral or disciplinary proceedings, permission, s 11 factors, public interest, administration of justice, which forum to apply to, General Division of the High Court*

**End point:** A mediation communication is not admissible in evidence in any court, arbitral or disciplinary proceedings except with the permission of a court or arbitral tribunal under s 11 (MA s 10). The application goes to the court or tribunal hearing the proceedings or, otherwise, to the General Division of the High Court (s 11(3)).

> **MA s 11(2): in deciding whether to grant permission the court or tribunal must consider**
> - (a) whether the communication may be or has been disclosed under s 9(2);
> - (b) whether disclosure or admission is in the public interest or the interests of the administration of justice;
> - (c) any other relevant circumstances or matters.

*Source note: MA ss 10–11; MED-3 p23; MED-5 p13. Conflict: MED-3 p23 and MED-5 p13 say "leave"; the current ss 10–11 say "permission" (Act 25 of 2021 wef 1 Apr 2022).*

### drp-m-1-36 — Without prejudice privilege at common law
*Keywords: without prejudice privilege, Mariwu Industrial v Dextra Asia, Evidence Act s 23, negotiations to settle, need not be labelled, Greenline-Onyx v Otto Systems, waiver by producing document in court, Krishna Kumaran v Kuppusamy, inadvertent disclosure, Mediation Act s 4*

**End point:**
- **Mariwu:** a statement made in negotiations to settle a dispute is without prejudice even if not expressly labelled (*Mariwu Industrial v Dextra Asia* [2006] SGCA 37 at [24]). The slides ask whether mediation counts as "negotiation to settle"; the Mediation Act's own ss 9–11 now protect mediation communications regardless.
- **Greenline-Onyx:** producing the document in court proceedings waived the privilege (*Greenline-Onyx Envirotech v Otto Systems* [2007] SGCA 25).
- **Krishna Kumaran (contrast):** waiver needs an express or implied agreement, and inadvertence is not enough (*Krishna Kumaran v Kuppusamy* [2014] SGHC 158 at [22]–[23]).

*Source note: MED-3 p25–26; [2006] SGCA 37 at [24]; [2007] SGCA 25 at [22]; [2014] SGHC 158 at [22]–[23] (MED-3 cites the SLR reports [2006] 4 SLR(R) 807, [2007] 3 SLR(R) 40, [2014] 4 SLR 232)*

### drp-m-1-37 — Recording a mediated settlement as an order of court (s 12(1)–(3))
*Keywords: record mediated settlement agreement as order of court, Mediation Act s 12, no proceedings commenced, consent of all parties, 8 weeks, longer period court allows, designated mediation service provider, certified mediator, in writing, signed, prescribed information, Mediation Rules 2017 r 2*

**End point:** Where a mediated settlement agreement is made in a dispute for which no court proceedings have started, any party may, with all other parties' consent, apply to court to record it as an order of court (MA s 12(1)). The application must be made within 8 weeks of the agreement, or a longer period the court allows (s 12(2)). The court may record it if three conditions are met (box). The 2021 paper asked these requirements on a settlement in Chinese between unrepresented parties.

> **MA s 12(3): the court may record it if**
> - (a) the mediation was administered by a designated mediation service provider or conducted by a certified mediator;
> - (b) the agreement is in writing and signed by or on behalf of all parties;
> - (c) it contains the prescribed information.
>
> **Mediation Rules 2017 r 2: prescribed information**
> - (a) each party's name;
> - (b) each mediator's name;
> - (c) the mediation service provider's name, if any;
> - (d) each certification scheme under which each mediator is certified, if any;
> - (e) the date of the agreement;
> - (f) the terms of the settlement.

*Source note: MA s 12(1)–(3); MR r 2 (2025 Rev Ed); MED-3 p30–32; PYP21-MED Q1.2, Q1.9(h). Conflict: MED-3 p31 cites the Mediation Rules "s 2"; it is r 2. CYR-MED cites "s 12(4)" for the s 12(3) conditions and reads the 8 weeks as a minimum wait; the statute makes it a deadline ("within 8 weeks").*

### drp-m-1-38 — Refusal to record; enforcement (s 12(4)–(5))
*Keywords: Mediation Act s 12(4), refuse to record, void or voidable, incapacity, fraud, misrepresentation, duress, coercion, mistake, not capable of settlement, term not enforceable as court order, child welfare custody best interests, public policy, s 12(5), enforced as judgment*

**End point:** The court may refuse to record the agreement on the grounds in MA s 12(4) (box). Once recorded, it may be enforced like a judgment or order of court (s 12(5)). For Family Justice Courts matters, only prescribed classes of agreement may be recorded (s 12(6)(a)).

> **MA s 12(4): the court may refuse if**
> - (a) the agreement is void or voidable for incapacity, fraud, misrepresentation, duress, coercion, mistake or any other contractual vitiating ground;
> - (b) the subject matter is not capable of settlement;
> - (c) a term is not capable of enforcement as a court order;
> - (d) in a dispute involving a child's welfare or custody, a term is not in the child's best interest;
> - (e) recording would be contrary to public policy.

*Source note: MA s 12(4)–(6); MED-3 p33, p35*

## 1.VI Mediated settlement agreements

- **Is the settlement binding?** Ordinary contract principles: offer, acceptance, consideration, certainty, intention; vitiating factors apply (*Tiong Swee Eng*; *Sita Jaswant Kaur*) → [1-39](#drp-m-1-39)
- **What should the settlement agreement cover?** Parties and third parties, scope, formality, disposal of proceedings, warranties, conditions, default, payment / tax / costs, confidentiality, law and jurisdiction, authority → [1-40](#drp-m-1-40)
- **Divorce settlement reached at mediation?** The court hearing ancillary matters considers whether and how far it binds (*UJN v UJO*) → [1-41](#drp-m-1-41)
- **Other side does not pay: how to enforce?** Court order (MA s 12(5)), sue on the contract, consent award (Arb-Med-Arb), Singapore Convention → [1-42](#drp-m-1-42)
- **Cross-border settlement: Singapore Convention?** International, commercial, written settlement from mediation; excludes consumer, family, inheritance, employment, and court- or award-enforceable settlements → [1-43](#drp-m-1-43)
- **How is it recorded and when refused in Singapore?** High Court records it (SCMA ss 4–5); evidence it came from mediation (s 6); Art 5-type refusal grounds (s 7) → [1-44](#drp-m-1-44)

### drp-m-1-39 — A settlement agreement is a contract
*Keywords: settlement agreement contract, offer, acceptance, consideration, certainty, intention to create legal relations, Tiong Swee Eng v Yeo Khee Siang, Lim Koon Park, misrepresentation, Sita Jaswant Kaur v Surindar Singh, subject to contract*

**End point:** A mediated settlement agreement is simply a species of contract. Formation needs offer, acceptance, consideration, certainty and intention to create legal relations. General principles, such as actionable misrepresentation, apply (*Tiong Swee Eng v Yeo Khee Siang* [2015] SGHC 116 at [19]–[21], applying *Lim Koon Park v Yap Jin Meng Bryan*). Whether an agreement made after mediation binds depends on the usual contractual principles and on an intention to be legally bound (*Sita Jaswant Kaur v Surindar Singh* [2013] SGHC 176 at [5]–[6]). "Subject to contract" wording can prevent a binding agreement arising. Disclosure to enforce or dispute the agreement needs permission under MA s 9(3)(a).

*Source note: MED-3 p8, p27, p29, p34; [2015] SGHC 116 at [19]–[21]; [2013] SGHC 176 at [5]–[6]. Sita Jaswant Kaur was reversed on appeal (Surindar Singh s/o Jaswant Singh v Sita Jaswant Kaur [2014] SGCA 37); MED-3 relies on the High Court only for the general contractual point stated here. MED-3 cites the SLR reports [2015] 3 SLR 1141 and [2013] 4 SLR 838.*

### drp-m-1-40 — Drafting checklist
*Keywords: settlement agreement checklist, parties, third parties, scope of claims settled, formality, disposal of court proceedings, warranties, conditional settlement, default provisions, payment arrangement, tax, legal costs, confidentiality, governing law, jurisdiction, capacity, authority to settle*

**End point:** A settlement agreement should be clear and specific and deal with all issues, so as to minimise future disputes (MED-4 p25). The checklist below covers what it should address.

> **Checklist (MED-3 p28)**
> - the parties, and any third parties;
> - the scope of the claims settled;
> - formality;
> - disposal of court proceedings;
> - warranties;
> - conditional or unconditional settlement;
> - default provisions;
> - payment arrangements, tax and legal costs;
> - confidentiality;
> - governing law and jurisdiction;
> - capacity and authority to settle.

*Source note: MED-3 p27–28; MED-4 p25*

### drp-m-1-41 — Settlement agreements in family proceedings (UJN v UJO)
*Keywords: UJN v UJO, family settlement agreement, ancillary matters, binding, extent given effect, divorce mediation, Family Justice Courts, MA s 12(6)*

**End point:** A settlement agreement reached at mediation in family proceedings is not to be ignored. The court hearing the ancillary matters considers whether it binds and how far to give it effect (*UJN v UJO* [2018] SGFC 16). This is consistent with *Sita Jaswant Kaur* at [5]–[6]. Recording family mediated settlements as court orders under MA s 12 is limited to prescribed classes (s 12(6)(a)).

*Source note: MED-3 p8, p34 (UJN v UJO was not available on eLitigation on 9 Nov 2026; the holding is as quoted in MED-3 p34); MA s 12(6)(a)*

### drp-m-1-42 — Ways to enforce a mediated settlement
*Keywords: enforcement of mediated settlement agreement, order of court, Mediation Act s 12(5), contract, breach, repudiation, consent award, Arb-Med-Arb, New York Convention, Singapore Convention on Mediation, SCMA*

**End point:** A mediated settlement can be enforced in four ways:
- as a court order, if recorded under MA s 12 and then enforced like a judgment (s 12(5));
- as a contract, suing for breach or repudiation;
- if made in an Arb-Med-Arb, as a consent award enforceable under the NYC (0-90);
- for an international commercial settlement, under the Singapore Convention on Mediation Act 2020 (1-43, 1-44).

*Source note: MED-3 p35; MA s 12(5); SIAC25 pp85–87; SCMA ss 4–5; CYR-MED s 3.3 (Singapore Convention)*

### drp-m-1-43 — Singapore Convention on Mediation: scope and exclusions
*Keywords: Singapore Convention on Mediation, United Nations Convention on International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation, 7 August 2019, international settlement agreement, commercial dispute, in writing, places of business in different States, Art 1, exclusions, consumer, family, inheritance, employment, court-approved settlement, recorded as arbitral award*

**End point:** The Convention (opened for signature in Singapore on 7 Aug 2019) applies to a written agreement resulting from mediation that resolves a commercial dispute and is international when concluded (Art 1(1)). Singapore gives it effect through the Singapore Convention on Mediation Act 2020 (SCMA s 3). "Mediation" means a third person helps the parties without authority to impose a solution (SCMA s 2(1); Art 2(3)). Not in the 2026 slides; covered because the Convention is part of Singapore's mediation framework.

> **Convention Art 1**
> - (1) International if (a) at least two parties have their places of business in different States; or (b) the parties' State differs from the State where a substantial part of the obligations is performed, or with which the subject matter is most closely connected.
> - (2) Excluded: consumer disputes (personal, family or household purposes); family, inheritance or employment law.
> - (3) Excluded: settlements approved by a court or concluded in court proceedings and enforceable as a judgment there; settlements recorded and enforceable as an arbitral award (so an Arb-Med-Arb consent award goes under the NYC).

*Source note: SCMA ss 2–3 and Schedule (Convention Arts 1–2); CYR-MED s 3.3 (one line). Not in the 2026 Course Materials.*

### drp-m-1-44 — Singapore Convention on Mediation Act 2020: recording and refusal
*Keywords: SCMA s 4, apply to High Court, record international settlement agreement as order of court, invoke agreement as defence, s 5, enforce as judgment, s 6 evidence resulted from mediation, mediator's signature, institution attestation, s 7 grounds for refusing, incapacity, null and void, not binding, modified, performed, not clear, serious breach by mediator, failure to disclose impartiality, public policy*

**End point:** A party to an international settlement agreement may apply to the High Court to record it as an order of court, to enforce it, or to invoke it to prove a matter already resolved (SCMA s 4). Once recorded it is enforceable like a High Court judgment (s 5). The applicant files the signed agreement and evidence that it resulted from mediation, e.g. the mediator's signature or an institution's attestation, with a certified translation if needed (s 6). Refusal follows Convention Art 5 (box).

> **SCMA s 7(2): refusal grounds (to be proved by the resisting party)**
> - (a) incapacity of a party;
> - (b) the agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of performance under its law; not binding or not final by its terms; or subsequently modified;
> - (c) the obligations have been performed, or are not clear or comprehensible;
> - (d) granting relief would be contrary to its terms;
> - (e) a serious breach of mediator standards without which the party would not have settled;
> - (f) the mediator failed to disclose circumstances raising justifiable doubts as to impartiality or independence, and the failure had a material impact or undue influence without which the party would not have settled.
> - s 7(3): the court may also refuse if (a) granting it would be contrary to Singapore public policy, or (b) the subject matter is not capable of settlement by mediation under Singapore law.
> - s 8: an order recorded in the resisting party's absence may be set aside on any refusal ground.

*Source note: SCMA ss 4–8 and Schedule Art 5. Not in the 2026 Course Materials.*

## 1.VII The seven elements of effective negotiation

- **Interests or positions?** A position is a demand; an interest is the need behind it (rest v piano). Ask "why?" / "why not?"; prioritise; use joint interests → [1-45](#drp-m-1-45)
- **Options?** Ways to satisfy interests; separate inventing from deciding; brainstorm (no commitment, attribution, criticism or evaluation); expand the pie; commit only at the end → [1-46](#drp-m-1-46)
- **Criteria?** Objective standards (precedent, SOP, law, custom, industry practice, market value, likely court outcome) or fair procedures (one cuts / other chooses, turns, lots, neutral decides); sword and shield → [1-47](#drp-m-1-47)
- **Alternatives?** Unilateral (options are bilateral); BATNA / WATNA / MLATNA; reality-test; estimate theirs; accept if offer ≥ BATNA → [1-48](#drp-m-1-48)
- **Communication?** Listen first to understand; speak so they know they were heard → [1-49](#drp-m-1-49)
- **Relationship?** Separate people from problem; perception, emotion (venting), communication (rapport); build early → [1-50](#drp-m-1-50)
- **Commitment?** Only after interests, options, criteria and alternatives; preferably in writing; realistic; clear and complete → [1-51](#drp-m-1-51)

### drp-m-1-45 — Interests, not positions
*Keywords: interests, positions, motivation behind demand, needs and concerns, piano neighbour early shift, focus on interests not positions, ask why, why not, prioritise interests, joint interests, goodwill, reputation, relationships, security, business opportunities, recognition*

**End point:** A position is a demand, and only one way to satisfy an interest. An interest is the motivation behind it, the party's needs and concerns. For example, the position "I want my neighbour to stop playing the piano" may hide the interest "I need rest because I work the early shift". The approach is:
- focus on interests, not positions;
- dig under positions by asking "why?" and "why not?";
- clarify and prioritise the interests;
- capitalise on joint interests;
- help parties see that the other side's interests must be addressed.
Typical interests are goodwill, reputation, relationships, security, business opportunities and recognition.

*Source note: MED-4 p3–8; PYP23 p46 Q53; PYP21-MED Q1.1, Q2.1*

### drp-m-1-46 — Options
*Keywords: options, ways to satisfy interests, possible solutions, separate inventing from deciding, brainstorming, ground rules, no commitment, no attribution, no criticism, no evaluation, expand the pie, package, commit at the end*

**End point:** An option is a way to satisfy an interest, a possible solution guided by the parties' interests. The method is:
- separate inventing from deciding;
- brainstorm under ground rules: no commitment, no attribution, no criticism, no evaluation;
- expand the "pie", so a smaller slice of a big pie beats a larger slice of a small one;
- aim for an agreement among the best of many options, packaged together;
- make commitments only at the end.

*Source note: MED-4 p9–11; SampleQs p62 Q6 (key p122: D, separate inventing from deciding)*

### drp-m-1-47 — Criteria (legitimacy)
*Keywords: objective criteria, legitimacy, fairness, sword and shield, face-saving, precedents, standard operating procedure, position at law, custom, industry practice, market value, likely court outcome, fair procedures, one cuts other chooses, taking turns, drawing lots, neutral decides, why is that fair*

**End point:** Objective criteria (legitimacy) evaluate an option, because parties want to be treated fairly. They act as a sword, to persuade the other side an option is fair, and a shield, against an unfair one. They also give face-saving, justifiable results, especially for representatives. Ask "why is that fair?" and frame the issue as a search for an objective standard. Fair procedures do not include "one party decides" (PYP23 Q55).

> **Sources of criteria (MED-4 p13–14)**
> - Standards: precedents; standard operating procedure; the position at law; custom or tradition; industry practice; market value; the likely outcome in court.
> - Fair procedures, absent a standard: one cuts, the other chooses; taking turns; drawing lots; letting a neutral decide.

*Source note: MED-4 p12–15; SampleQs p62 Q7 (key p122: C, persuades the other party they are being treated fairly); PYP23 p48 Q55*

### drp-m-1-48 — Alternatives: BATNA, WATNA, MLATNA
*Keywords: alternatives, BATNA, best alternative to a negotiated agreement, WATNA, worst alternative, MLATNA, most likely alternative, unilateral, options bilateral, evaluate whether to accept, reality testing, sure to win in court, estimate other party's BATNA, bargaining power, accept if offer equal or better than BATNA, walk away*

**End point:** Alternatives are what a party can do if there is no settlement. Their purpose is to evaluate whether to accept an option. Options are bilateral (they need the other side); alternatives are unilateral. The three measures are:
- BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement;
- WATNA, the worst;
- MLATNA, the most likely.
In use:
- reality-test a party's BATNA, e.g. a party "sure to win in court";
- estimate the other side's BATNA to gauge its bargaining power;
- accept if the offer is at least as good as the BATNA, and walk away if the BATNA is better.

*Source note: MED-4 p16–18; SampleQs p62 Q8 (key p122: C — III and IV: gauge relative bargaining power; decide whether to accept or walk away); PYP23 p47 Q54; PYP21-MED Q1.10, Q2.1*

### drp-m-1-49 — Communication
*Keywords: communication, receiving, listening, understand not debate, positive voices, conveying, speaking, let parties know they have been heard, help parties hear each other*

**End point:** Communication has two sides:
- **receiving (listening):** first and foremost to understand, not to debate or attack, and listening for "positive" voices that help parties see the positives;
- **conveying (speaking):** to let the parties know they have been heard, and to help them hear each other.

*Source note: MED-4 p19*

### drp-m-1-50 — Relationship
*Keywords: relationship, substantive issues, relationship issues, separate people from problem, working relationship, mediator-party relationship, perception, put yourself in their shoes, emotion, allow venting, rapport, build early, unconditionally constructive*

**End point:** Every dispute has substantive issues and relationship issues, and relationship issues can block settlement of the substance. "Separate the people from the problem": look at the issues objectively. Think also about the relationship between the mediator and the parties. At minimum the parties need a working relationship. To improve it, work on three things:
- perception: put yourself in the other's shoes;
- emotion: allow venting;
- communication: build rapport, verbal and non-verbal.
Build the relationship early, even before the mediation, and be unconditionally constructive.

*Source note: MED-4 p20–23; SampleQs p63 Q9 (key p122: D, all four: objectivity, rapport, empathy, venting)*

### drp-m-1-51 — Commitment
*Keywords: commitment, timing, interests understood, options generated, evaluated by objective criteria, alternatives considered, in writing, realistic, clear and specific language, all issues dealt with, settlement terms*

**End point:** Commit only when four things are done:
- interests are understood and prioritised;
- options that satisfy them have been generated;
- options have been evaluated against objective criteria or a fair procedure;
- alternatives have been considered.
Commitments should preferably be in writing and realistic. Use clear, specific language and check that all issues are dealt with, to minimise future disputes.

*Source note: MED-4 p24–25*

## 1.VIII Role and ethics of the mediation advocate

- **What does the mediation advocate do?** Prepare the client; support the client's account and proposals; negotiate; assist the mediator (understand the dispute, keep it on track, manage the client); informed decisions; settlement terms; follow-up → [1-52](#drp-m-1-52)
- **Zealous or conciliatory?** Protect the client no less robustly than in litigation, but non-adversarial, collaborative and constructive → [1-53](#drp-m-1-53)
- **Competence?** Objective, realistic assessment of merits, BATNA and WATNA, and the costs, time and goodwill of not settling (PCR r 5) → [1-54](#drp-m-1-54)
- **Confidentiality?** Do not disclose information designated confidential; get consent when unsure, including at pre-mediation conferences; protect the other side's information (PCR r 6; MA s 9) → [1-55](#drp-m-1-55)
- **Who decides whether to settle?** The client; stay within authority; never commit without express authority → [1-56](#drp-m-1-56)
- **Conflicts? Former mediator acting for a party?** Avoid personal conflicts (r 22); ex-mediator must not act on the subject or matters discussed (r 8(6)); no threats or undue pressure → [1-57](#drp-m-1-57)
- **Conduct towards the mediator?** Respectful, courteous, good faith; not knowingly mislead, or let the client mislead, the mediator (PCR r 8A) → [1-58](#drp-m-1-58)
- **Negotiation tactics?** Any strategy, but no underhanded tactics and no false information; keep it constructive; unrepresented party: no legal advice (r 8(2)) → [1-59](#drp-m-1-59)
- **Duties to the client?** Competence, best interests, all legal means (r 5(2)(j)), knowledge and skills (r 5(2)(d)), explain offers (r 17(2)(c)), evaluate ADR (r 17(2)(e)), confidentiality, costs → [1-60](#drp-m-1-60)
- **Family proceedings?** Inform of all DR options from when proceedings are contemplated; advise amicable resolution; be constructive; child's welfare paramount (PCR r 15A) → [1-61](#drp-m-1-61)
- **Preparing the client?** Explain the process; assess the case and BATNA/WATNA; identify interests and options; settlement range and authority; opening statement → [1-62](#drp-m-1-62)

### drp-m-1-52 — Role of the mediation advocate
*Keywords: role of mediation advocate, prepare client, support client, present account, perspectives, proposals, negotiate, persuade, assist mediator, understand dispute, keep negotiation on track, manage client's emotions, informed judgment, settlement terms, client's best interest, follow-up*

**End point:** The mediation advocate's role is set out in MED-5 (box). Drafting pleadings, litigation advocacy and verbatim note-taking are not the advocate's role in mediation (PYP23 Q37).

> **MED-5 p4–5**
> - prepare the client for mediation;
> - support the client in presenting its accounts, perspectives and proposals, and in negotiating with and persuading the other party;
> - assist the mediator in understanding the dispute, keeping the negotiation on track and constructive, and managing the client's emotions, conduct and expectations;
> - ensure decisions are well considered and informed;
> - ensure settlement terms are in the client's best interest, reflect its intentions accurately, and are clearly and effectively prepared;
> - support any necessary follow-up.

*Source note: MED-5 p4–5; PYP23 p30 Q37, p44 Q51, p52 Q59; SampleQs p60 Q1*

### drp-m-1-53 — Approach: zealous but non-adversarial
*Keywords: approach of mediation advocate, protect client's interest robustly, zealously, non-adversarial, collaborative problem solving, constructive, mutually acceptable, not antagonistic, not constantly rebutting, not steadfast on legal positions*

**End point:** A lawyer must protect the client's interests in mediation no less robustly or zealously than in litigation. The approach should be:
- less adversarial, or non-adversarial;
- focused on collaborative problem-solving;
- constructive in reaching a mutually acceptable resolution;
- consistent with the purpose and tone of mediation.
So remaining steadfast on the merits to preserve rights is not the advocate's role (SampleQs Med Q1). Mediation advocacy means not interrupting, exploring concerns and not being antagonistic, not "protecting rights and winning" (Med Q4).

*Source note: MED-5 p6; SampleQs p60 Q1 (key p122: C is NOT true), p61 Q4 (key p122: C — I, II and III); PYP23 p45 Q52, p51 Q58*

### drp-m-1-54 — Competence in advising (PCR r 5)
*Keywords: competence, PCR r 5, honesty competence diligence, objective realistic assessment, strengths and merits, BATNA, WATNA, costs, time, convenience, goodwill, relationships, reputation, informed decision*

**End point:** Under PCR r 5 (honesty, competence, diligence) the advocate enables the client to make objective, well-considered and informed decisions at the mediation. That means an objective, realistic assessment of three things:
- the strengths and merits of each side's case;
- the client's BATNA and WATNA;
- the implications of not settling confidentially: costs, time, convenience, goodwill, relationships and reputation.
Rule 5(2)(b) also requires the lawyer to tell the client all information known that may reasonably affect its interests, subject to confidentiality. So "only inform the client of information the client is prepared to accept" is wrong (PYP23 Q57).

*Source note: MED-5 p8–10; PCR r 5(2)(b)–(d); PYP23 p50 Q57*

### drp-m-1-55 — Confidentiality (PCR r 6)
*Keywords: confidentiality, PCR r 6, information designated confidential, mediator, other party, obtain consent, preliminary conferences, do not share outcome, other party's information, Mediation Act s 9, s 10, s 11*

**End point:** The advocate's duties of confidentiality in mediation are:
- do not disclose information the client has designated confidential, whether to the mediator or the other side;
- if unsure whether something may be shared, at any stage including preliminary conferences with the mediator, get consent first;
- do not share information about the mediation or its outcome without explicit authorisation;
- do not disclose the other party's information without that party's authorisation, and stress the need for confidentiality.
These rest on PCR r 6 and Mediation Act ss 9–11.

*Source note: MED-5 p11–13; PCR r 6(2); MA ss 9–11*

### drp-m-1-56 — Party autonomy and the advocate's authority
*Keywords: party autonomy, decision to settle lies with client, respect client's decision, differs from advice, authorisation, scope of authority, no agreement without express authority, mandate*

**End point:** Whether, and on what terms, to settle is ultimately the client's decision. Respect it even if it differs from your advice. Keep every disclosure and proposal within the authority given. Never commit to an agreement for the client without clear and express authority.

*Source note: MED-5 p14–15; PYP23 p51 Q58*

### drp-m-1-57 — Conflicts of interest (PCR r 22); acting after being the mediator
*Keywords: conflict of interest, PCR r 22, personal interests, inconsistent interests, former mediator, PCR r 8(6), must not act for party, subject of mediation, matter discussed, threats, pressure, undue influence*

**End point:**
- **Personal conflicts:** avoid situations where personal interests may conflict with the client's (PCR r 22(2)), and do not act for anyone whose interests are inconsistent.
- **Former mediator:** a lawyer who has acted as mediator must not later act for any party to that mediation on its subject or any matter discussed in it (PCR r 8(6)).
- **No pressure:** do not use threats, pressure or undue influence to steer the client's decisions.

*Source note: MED-5 p8, p16; PCR rr 8(6), 22(1)–(2). MED-5 lists the former-mediator rule under conflicts without a rule number; it is PCR r 8(6).*

### drp-m-1-58 — Conduct in ADR: respect, courtesy, good faith, not misleading (PCR r 8A)
*Keywords: PCR r 8A, conduct of alternative dispute resolution process, respectful of ADR process and mediator, courteous, good faith, must not knowingly mislead mediator, must not knowingly assist or permit client to mislead, wheelchair neck brace exaggeration*

**End point:** PCR r 8A governs conduct in any ADR process (box). A client planning to exaggerate an injury at mediation, e.g. arriving in a wheelchair, must be advised against it, and the lawyer cannot assist (PYP21-MED Q1.7).
- **MED-5 slide 8 rule map (which PCR rule?):** r 5 competence in advising; r 6 protecting client confidentiality; r 7 courtesy and fairness (to other lawyers: r 7(2) "must treat other legal practitioners with courtesy and fairness"; r 7(1) principles of respect, good faith and dignified, courteous dealing; r 7(3) no direct contact with a represented party without consent or another listed exception); r 8 honesty, honourability and good faith in conducting the negotiation; r 22 avoiding conflicts of interest. r 8A (box) governs conduct in the ADR process itself.

> **PCR r 8A**
> - (1) Always be respectful of the ADR process and of the adjudicator, mediator or other person conducting it.
> - (2) Always be courteous in conducting or presenting the client's case.
> - (3) Act in good faith throughout.
> - (4) Do not knowingly mislead, or attempt to mislead, the adjudicator, mediator or other person conducting the process.
> - (5) Do not knowingly assist or permit the client to mislead them.

*Source note: PCR rr 7, 8A (S 69/2017 wef 1 Mar 2017); MED-3 p37; MED-5 p8 (rule map), p17; PYP21-MED Q1.7*

### drp-m-1-59 — Honesty in negotiation: no underhanded tactics
*Keywords: honesty, good faith, PCR r 8, negotiation strategy, underhanded tactics, sharp tactics, false information, mislead other party, constructive and collaborative, unrepresented party, PCR r 8(2), no legal advice, unfair advantage, r 8(3)*

**End point:** The advocate may use any negotiation strategy that serves the client's best interests, with limits:
- no underhanded tactics;
- do not mislead the other party or its counsel with false information;
- keep the negotiation constructive and collaborative.
The advocate may not resort to "sharp negotiation tactics" (SampleQs Med Q10). PCR r 8 requires honesty and courtesy towards everyone and forbids taking unfair advantage or acting deceitfully (r 8(3)). With an unrepresented party, give no legal advice beyond advice to get independent advice, and make sure they do not think you protect their interests (r 8(2)); the 2021 paper set this on an unrepresented, unwell opponent.

*Source note: MED-5 p17–18; PCR r 8(1)–(3); SampleQs p63 Q10 (key p122: D, sharp tactics is the untrue statement); PYP21-MED Q1.8*

### drp-m-1-60 — Duties to the client; advising on ADR (PCR rr 5(2), 17(2))
*Keywords: duties to client, competent advice, best interests, PCR r 5(2)(j), use all legal means, r 5(2)(d), knowledge skills attributes, best outcome, keep costs within budget, avoid conflict, confidentiality, r 17(2)(c) explain proposal of amicable resolution, r 17(2)(e) evaluate ADR, cost-benefit*

**End point:** The advocate owes the client the duties set out below.

> **Duties to the client (MED-5 p19–20; PCR)**
> - give competent legal advice and representation, in the client's best interests (MED-3 p37: PCR rr 5(2)(j), 17(1); r 17(1) principle: act in the client's best interests and charge fairly for work done);
> - use all legal means to advance the client's interests, as far as reasonably expected (r 5(2)(j));
> - have, and apply appropriately, the knowledge, skills and attributes the matter needs (r 5(2)(d));
> - secure the best outcome in negotiation or litigation, and keep costs within the client's budget;
> - avoid conflicts, and protect confidential information (rr 6, 22);
> - explain clearly any proposal of amicable resolution or offer by another party, in a language the client understands (r 17(2)(c)–(d));
> - in an appropriate case, evaluate with the client whether pursuing the matter justifies the expense and risk, and evaluate the use of ADR (r 17(2)(e)).

*Source note: MED-5 p19–20; MED-3 p37; PCR rr 5(2), 17(1), 17(2)(c)–(e) (r 17(2)(c)–(d) S 240/2022 wef 1 Apr 2022); PYP23 p49 Q56, p50 Q57. Conflict: CEL says some notes wrongly cite r 5(2)(f) for "all legal means"; the rule is r 5(2)(j), as MED-5 says.*

### drp-m-1-61 — Family proceedings (PCR r 15A)
*Keywords: PCR r 15A, family proceedings, inform client of all dispute resolution options, mediation, counselling, from the time proceedings are contemplated, advise amicable resolution, constructive and reasonable, child welfare and best interests take precedence, below 21 years*

**End point:** PCR r 15A sets the lawyer's duties in family proceedings (box). The duty starts when proceedings are *contemplated*, not only at commencement (PYP23 Q60).

> **PCR r 15A(2)–(3)**
> - (2)(a) From the time proceedings are contemplated, inform the client of all reasonably available dispute resolution options, including mediation and counselling, so the client can decide on an informed basis.
> - (2)(b) From that time, and whenever reasonably possible, advise the client to consider resolving the proceedings amicably.
> - (2)(c) Advise the client to be constructive and reasonable in the proceedings.
> - (3) Where a child is involved: advise that the child's welfare and best interests take precedence over the parents' and the child's wishes, and advise the client to consider the child's welfare and the potential adverse impact on the child. "Child" means under 21 (r 15A(4)).

*Source note: PCR r 15A (S 82/2018 wef 12 Feb 2018); MED-5 p7; FJCPD 81(2); PYP23 p53 Q60; PYP21-MED Q1.6*

### drp-m-1-62 — Preparing the client for mediation
*Keywords: preparing client for mediation, explain mediation process, what to expect, assess case, BATNA, WATNA, identify interests, options meeting both parties' interests, settlement range, authority to settle, opening statement, case statement, who attends, client preparation*

**End point:** Preparation draws together the advocate's role and the seven elements:
- explain the process and what to expect (1-52);
- assess the merits, BATNA and WATNA objectively (1-54);
- identify the client's and the other side's interests and options that meet *both* (1-45, 1-46). Considering only options that meet the client's interests is wrong (PYP23 Q38);
- identify objective criteria and a realistic settlement range;
- settle the client's authority and who will attend (1-56);
- prepare the opening statement with the client (1-6).
The 2026 Course Materials have no further process detail. The CS1 live lecture covered pre-mediation preparation (MED-Plan); no recording or handout was available, so for stage-by-stage process questions rely on the SCPD Appendix D guidance (1-23): know both sides' case well enough to advise, agree beforehand what documents, quantum details and expert evidence the mediator needs, and remember mediation can happen at any time and need not finish in a day.

*Source note: MED-1 p8; MED-5 p4–5, p10, p14–15; MED-4 p3–18; MED-Plan p1; PYP23 p31 Q38 (preparing client: considering options that meet only the client's interests is the untrue statement); PYP21-MED Q1.9(c)–(f)*
