## 1. Data Protection & Cyber Regulation (~17 MCQs)

**Weighting:** about ⅓ data protection, ⅓ cybersecurity, ⅓ AI and online threats [DP-1 p3].

**Core law:**
- PDPA Parts 1–6, 6A, 9B–9D, 10, and Schedules 1, 2, 11;
- the Breach Notification Regulations 2021;
- PDP Regulations 2021 Parts 2–4;
- the Cybersecurity Act (CYSA) Parts 1, 3, 4, ss 16A and 17, and Schedule 1;
- POHA ss 3–7, 11, 12, 15, 15A–15E [DP-Plan p5].

**Non-core:** POFMA, the POHA subsidiary orders, and the Online Safety (Relief and Accountability)
Bill 2025 [DP-Plan p6].

Statute page references are to the Go Pack copies: PDPA in force from 1/10/2022, CYSA from
11/4/2022, POHA from 1/5/2023. All three were checked against SSO on 3 Nov 2026:
- **CYSA:** amended (see above).
- **PDPA:** only two minor changes since the Go Pack copy, neither affecting a block:
  - s 48C was amended for public-agency data sharing (Act 2 of 2026, from 16 Oct 2026);
  - Second Sch Part 3 para 3(a) was deleted (Act 19 of 2025).

  Data portability (Part 6B) is still not in force [SSO-PDPA].
- **POHA:** substantially amended by Act 23 of 2025 (from 29 Jun 2026) and Act 21 of 2025
  (s 6(1A), from 17 Aug 2026). The changes are:
  - "humiliation" and sexual or indecent conduct added to ss 3, 4, 6 and 7, with a
    legitimate-purpose exception in s 4(1A);
  - online stalking examples (s 7);
  - doxxing of public servants (s 6(1A));
  - enhanced damages (s 11A);
  - administrator orders (ss 15CA, 15CB).

  Penalties are unchanged. See clk-m-0-55 to 0-62, 0-67 and 0-71 [SSO-POHA].

### 1.1 PDPA — scope and personal data

- The PDPA governs collection, use and disclosure (CUD) of personal data by organisations. It
  balances individuals' protection against organisations' needs on a "reasonable person would
  consider appropriate" standard, and creates no property in data [DP-1 p16; PDPA s 3 p13,
  s 11(1) p19].
- **Personal data** = data, **"whether true or not"**, about an individual identifiable from that
  data alone or with other information the organisation has or is likely to access. It includes
  opinions [PDPA s 2(1) p11; DP-1 p17].
- **"Individual"** = a natural person, **living or deceased** [PDPA s 2(1) p10].
  - Records 100+ years old are out of scope.
  - For someone dead more than 10 years, the Act does not apply.
  - For someone dead 10 years or less, only the **disclosure** provisions and **s 24
    (protection)** apply [PDPA s 4(4) p14].
- **Business contact information** is outside Parts 3–6A [PDPA s 4(5) p14].
- **"Organisation"** includes any individual, company, association or body of persons, **whether
  or not formed or resident in Singapore** [PDPA s 2(1) p11].
- **Excluded** from Parts 3–6B [PDPA s 4(1) p13]:
  - individuals acting in a personal or domestic capacity;
  - **employees acting in the course of employment**;
  - **public agencies**.
  - Reed v Bellingham: the employee exclusion is a defence the employee must prove [Reed p21].
- **Data intermediaries** (process for another organisation under a written contract) owe only
  **s 24 (protection), s 25 (retention), s 26C(3)(a)** (tell the controller of a breach) and
  s 26E. The controller remains fully liable as if it processed the data itself [PDPA s 4(2)–(3)
  p13; DP-1 p18].
- A contract is no excuse for breaching the PDPA; other written law prevails if inconsistent
  [PDPA s 4(6) p14].
- PDPC advisory guidelines (s 49) are **not binding** [PDPA p78; DP-1 p13].

### 1.2 Obligations and legal bases

- **Accountability (ss 11–12)** [PDPA p19–20; DP-1 p28]:
  - designate a DPO and publish the DPO's business contact information;
  - develop policies and practices;
  - set up a complaints process;
  - communicate the policies to staff;
  - make the policies available on request.
  - A **data inventory or DPIA is NOT an express requirement** [official key: SampleQs p90 Q4,
    key p130].
- **Consent (ss 13–14)**: consent, deemed consent, or authorisation by the Act or other written
  law. Consent is invalid if obtained by insufficient information, demanded beyond what is
  reasonable for the product, or obtained by deception [PDPA p20–21].
- **Deemed consent** comes in three forms:
  - by **conduct** (s 15(1)) — data voluntarily provided;
  - by **contractual necessity** (s 15(3)–(8)) — onward disclosure reasonably necessary to
    conclude or perform a contract [PDPA p21–23];
  - by **notification** (s 15A) — the organisation must assess that there is no likely adverse
    effect, notify the individual with a reasonable opt-out period, and keep the assessment.
    **Not available for sending marketing messages** [PDPA p24–25; PDPR reg 13 p15].
- **Withdrawal (s 16)**: on reasonable notice. The organisation must explain the consequences,
  may not prohibit withdrawal, and must stop (and make its intermediaries and agents stop) unless
  the law requires otherwise [PDPA p25–26].
- **Exceptions to consent (s 17; First and Second Schedules)** [PDPA p26–27, p95–108; DP-1 p26]:
  - **Vital interests** (First Sch Part 1): clearly in the individual's interests where consent
    can't be obtained in time; emergencies; contacting next of kin.
  - **Matters affecting the public** (Part 2): publicly available data; national interest;
    artistic or literary; archival or historical; news.
  - **Legitimate interests** (Part 3 para 1): the interests must outweigh any adverse effect,
    with a prior assessment, mitigation, and reasonable access to information about the
    processing. **Not for marketing messages.** Part 3 also covers investigations and
    proceedings, debt recovery, legal services and employment [PDPA p98–100].
  - **Business asset transactions** (Part 4) [PDPA p100–102].
  - **Business improvement** (First Sch Part 5): improving goods, services or methods;
    understanding customers; personalising. Data must be needed in identifiable form, and the
    use must be appropriate to a reasonable person. **Not for marketing** [PDPA p103–105].
  - **Research** (Second Sch): needs identifiable form, a clear public benefit, no decisions
    about the individual, and no identification in published results. **Disclosure** for
    research also requires that consent be impracticable [PDPA p106–108].
  - **"Notification of purposes" is NOT a legal basis** — it is a separate obligation [official
    key: SampleQs p92 Q8, key p130].
  - **Consent is still required for marketing** unless an exception applies; the statement
    "consent is not needed for marketing" is false [official key: SampleQs p90 Q5].
- **Purpose limitation (s 18)** — only for purposes a reasonable person would consider
  appropriate and that were notified [PDPA p27].
- **Notification (s 20)** — of purposes on or before collection. Not needed for deemed consent
  or s 17 exceptions, but still needed for employment purposes [PDPA p27–29].
- **Accuracy (s 23)** — reasonable effort, but only if the data is likely to be used for a
  decision affecting the individual or disclosed to another organisation [PDPA p32–33].
- **Retention (s 25)** — stop retaining (or anonymise) once **both** the purpose is no longer
  served **and** retention is no longer needed for legal or business purposes [PDPA p33].
- **Transfer limitation (s 26)** — the recipient must be bound by legally enforceable
  obligations giving **comparable protection**: law, contract, binding corporate rules, or APEC
  CBPR/PRP certification. Valid consent, contractual necessity and data in transit also suffice
  [PDPA p33–34; PDPR Part 3 p10–14].

### 1.3 Individual rights

- The rights are [DP-1 p31–32]:
  - **withdraw consent** (s 16);
  - **access** (s 21);
  - **correction** (s 22);
  - **private action** (s 48O).
  - **Data portability** is legislated but **not in force**.
  - **There is no right to deletion or erasure** [official key: SampleQs p92 Q9].
- **Access (s 21)** — provide the data plus how it was used or disclosed in the past year, "as
  soon as reasonably possible" [PDPA p29–31].
  - *Need not* provide: Fifth Schedule items such as evaluative opinions, exam scripts,
    privileged material, confidential commercial information, and frivolous requests.
  - *Must not* provide (s 21(3)): where access would threaten someone's safety or health, cause
    grave harm, reveal another individual's data, or be against the national interest.
  - Must preserve a copy of the data after refusing a request (s 22A) [PDPA p32].
  - If the organisation can't respond within **30 days**, it must say in writing when it will.
    It may charge a reasonable fee for access (with a prior estimate), but **no fee for
    correction** [PDPR regs 5, 7 p5–8].
- **Correction (s 22)** — correct the error, and send the correction to organisations it
  disclosed the data to in the past year; or annotate the data if it doesn't correct. **Opinions
  need not be altered** [PDPA p31–32].
- **Private action (s 48O)** — for loss or damage "directly" caused by breach of **Parts 4, 5,
  6, 6A, 6B** (not Part 3) or the Do Not Call provisions. It can only be brought after any PDPC
  decision has become final [PDPA p73–74].
  - **Reed v Bellingham [2022] SGCA 60**: **emotional distress** counts as loss or damage
    [107]; mere **loss of control** of the data does not [108] [Reed p47–48].

### 1.4 Enforcement and offences

- Investigation under s 50; enforcement under Part 9C [DP-1 p35]. The powers are:
  - mediation or other dispute resolution (s 48G);
  - review of access or correction responses (s 48H);
  - directions (s 48I);
  - financial penalties (s 48J);
  - voluntary undertakings (s 48L) [PDPA p61–70].
- **Penalty cap**: **10% of annual Singapore turnover if turnover exceeds $10m, otherwise $1m**
  [PDPA s 48J(3) p64; DP-1 p35]. Only for **intentional or negligent** breaches, and not where
  the breach is itself an offence [PDPA s 48J p64].
- Challenges go to reconsideration (s 48N), then the Data Protection Appeal Panel (s 48Q), then
  the High Court (s 48R) [PDPA p71–75].
- **Part 9B offences by individuals** (fine up to $5,000 and/or up to 2 years' jail) [PDPA
  p54–61]:
  - **unauthorised disclosure** of personal data, knowing or reckless (s 48D);
  - **improper use** for a gain, or to cause harm or loss (s 48E);
  - **unauthorised re-identification** of anonymised data (s 48F).
  - Defences include: the data was already public (unless it became public through a breach),
    the law permitted it, or the individual consented [PDPR regs 15A–15B p17].
- **SingHealth [2019] SGPDPC 3**: data of 1.5m people was taken. SingHealth was fined $250k
  and IHiS (its data intermediary) $750k [SingH p7, p49]. The controller's s 24 duty is not
  delegated away by using an intermediary [Nic-DP p7–8].

### 1.5 Cybersecurity under the PDPA

- **Protection obligation (s 24)** — make **reasonable security arrangements** against:
  - (a) unauthorised access, collection, use, disclosure, copying, modification or disposal;
  - (b) **loss of any storage medium or device**.

  It applies to controllers and intermediaries alike [PDPA p33; DP-2 p40–44].
  - "No one size fits all." Measures may be administrative, technical or physical, weighed
    against the nature of the data, its form, and the impact on the individual if it were
    compromised [DP-2 p42–43].
  - **No mandated standard such as ISO 27001**, and "all necessary" overstates the duty
    [official key: SampleQs p91–92 Q6, Q10].
- **Data breach notification (Part 6A)** [PDPA p34–38; DP-2 p46–50]:
  - A breach is notifiable if it causes (or is likely to cause) **significant harm**, **or** is
    of **significant scale — 500 or more** individuals [PDPA s 26B; DBNR reg 4 p2].
  - Significant harm is deemed for a name or ID number plus prescribed data (financial,
    medical, child-protection and similar), or for an account identifier plus password
    [DBNR reg 3 p2].
  - A breach confined **within** the organisation is not notifiable [PDPA s 26B(4)].
  - **Assess** the breach reasonably and expeditiously (s 26C(2)); PDPC guidance says generally
    within 30 days, but that is not in the statute [KC p130].
  - An intermediary must tell the controller **without undue delay** (s 26C(3)(a)).
  - Notify the **PDPC** as soon as practicable and **no later than 3 calendar days after the
    day of the assessment** (not after the breach) [PDPA s 26D(1) p36].
  - Notify **individuals** **on or after** notifying the PDPC, and **only for harm-type
    breaches**, not scale-only ones (s 26D(2)). Exceptions: remedial action taken, prior
    technological protection, a direction from the PDPC or a law-enforcement agency not to
    notify, or a PDPC waiver (s 26D(5)–(7)) [PDPA p36–37].

### 1.6 Cybersecurity Act 2018 — critical information infrastructure (CII)

- The Act regulates CII owners (Part 3), cyber threats and incidents (Part 4), and service
  providers (Part 5, not covered) [DP-2 p14]. Part 3 applies to CII **wholly or partly** in
  Singapore [CYSA p9–10].
- **Designation (s 7)** — a computer system is designated by notice if it is necessary for the
  continuous delivery of an **essential service** and its compromise would have a debilitating
  effect. Designation lasts **5 years** [CYSA p13–15; DP-2 p20–21].
  - Essential services (First Schedule) span 11 sectors: energy, infocomm, water, healthcare,
    banking and finance, security and emergency, aviation, land transport, maritime,
    government, media [CYSA p69–71].
- **Owner duties** [CYSA p17–25]:
  - provide information on request, and report **material changes within 30 days** (s 10);
  - comply with **codes of practice** — these have no legislative effect, but compliance is
    required unless waived (s 11);
  - comply with directions (s 12);
  - report changes in **beneficial or legal ownership within 7 days** (s 13);
  - report **prescribed cybersecurity incidents** and keep detection mechanisms in place
    (s 14);
  - carry out an **audit at least once every 2 years** and a **risk assessment at least once a
    year** (s 15);
  - take part in **cybersecurity exercises** when directed (s 16);
  - appeals go to the **Minister**: s 17 in the Go Pack copy, now **s 35B**, within 30 days
    [SSO-CYSA].
  - **Running the essential service is NOT a CII-owner duty** [official key: SampleQs p89 Q2].
- **Code of Practice s 3 (governance)** — board and senior management must include a member with
  cybersecurity knowledge; there must be a risk framework and register, policies reviewed
  **every 12 months**, and the owner stays responsible when outsourcing or using the cloud
  [CoP p17–24; DP-2 p35–36].
- **Incident response (Part 4)** [CYSA p28–34; DP-2 p29–31]:
  - **s 19 investigation powers** apply to anyone. Legal privilege is respected, but a
    contractual duty is no excuse for withholding information.
  - **s 20 serious incidents** (a severity threshold must be met) add further powers: remedial
    directions, entry, scanning and seizure.

### 1.7 Online threats — Protection from Harassment Act (POHA)

- **Offences** [POHA p8–18; DP-3 p8–14]:
  - **s 3** — *intentionally* causing harassment, alarm or distress, including by doxxing; up to
    $5k fine and/or 6 months.
  - **s 4** — conduct *likely* to cause harassment, alarm or distress; **fine only**, up to $5k.
  - **s 5** — fear or provocation of violence; up to $5k and/or 12 months.
  - **s 6** — conduct against **public servants and public service workers**; up to $5k and/or
    12 months.
  - **s 7** — **unlawful stalking**; up to $5k and/or 12 months.
  - "Reasonable conduct" is a common defence. Repeat offenders face enhanced penalties (s 8).
- **Civil remedies** [POHA p23–26; DP-3 p16–17]:
  - **s 11 statutory tort** for breaches of ss 3, 4, 5 or 7;
  - **s 12 protection order** — needs a contravention, a likelihood that it will continue or
    repeat, and that the order is just and equitable;
  - the **common-law tort of harassment is abolished** (s 14).
- **False-statement orders**, available to the person who is the **subject** of a false
  statement of fact [POHA p37–44; DP-3 p19–22]:

  | Section | Order | Against |
  |---|---|---|
  | s 15A | **Stop publication order** | the publisher |
  | s 15B | **Correction order** | the publisher |
  | s 15C | **Disabling order** | an internet intermediary |
  | s 15D | **Targeted correction order** | an internet intermediary |
  | s 15E | **General correction order** | a *prescribed* respondent, where the statement causes *serious harm* to reputation |

  - The respondent **need not know** the statement is false, and an order can issue even if the
    statement has since been amended or taken down [POHA ss 15(3), 15A(2)].
  - **There is no "specific correction order"** [official key: SampleQs p89 Q3].
  - The slides mislabel two orders; the statute names above are correct [DP-3 p21–22].

### 2.1 What is L&T? (Lecture 1)

- Technology = the practical, especially industrial, use of scientific discoveries; in economic
  terms, whatever raises output without being a factor of production [LT-1 p12–13].
- The **Big Question**: is the problem with the law (fix the law), the technology (fix the
  technology), or both? [LT-1 p16].
- **Easterbrook, "Law of the Horse" (1996)**: a course on the "law of the horse" is "doomed to
  be shallow and to miss unifying principles" — study general rules in context [LT-1 p19].
  Lawyers' predictions about computers are likely to be false, so mostly keep doing what you
  have been doing. His three prescriptions: make rules clear, create property rights, and
  facilitate bargaining [LT-1 p19–21].
- **Lessig, "What Cyberlaw Might Teach" (1998)**:
  - behaviour is regulated by **law, social norms, markets and architecture (incl. code)**;
  - law acts **directly**, or **indirectly** by changing the other three [LT-1 p23];
  - code is plastic and chosen by coders, so it can substitute for law or norms (AOL vs Counsel
    Connect) [LT-1 p24];
  - so cyberspace is **special enough that specialised study is useful and possible** — because
    its architecture regulates, not merely because it is new [LT-1 p25–26].
- **B2C2 v Quoine [2020] SGCA(I) 2**:
  - Facts: a thin market after Quoine's botched update triggered margin-call buys against
    B2C2's hard-coded 10 BTC:1 ETH "virtual price" orders; Quoine reversed the trades
    [LT-1 p29–31].
  - Majority, on deterministic algorithms:
    - the relevant knowledge is the **programmer's** [98];
    - test: when programming, did the programmer have actual or constructive knowledge that the
      offer would only ever be accepted by someone under a mistake, and act to take advantage
      of it [103];
    - assessed from **programming to contract formation**; Quoine's "hypothetical meeting on
      the trading floor" rejected [104];
    - no operative mistake — the algorithms "operated as they were meant to" [115]
      [LT-1 p32–35].
  - Mance IJ (dissenting) asked what a reasonable trader would have thought [200] [LT-1 p35].

### 2.2 Technology regulation (Lecture 2)

- **Ex ante regulation** (speed limits) vs **ex post liability** (tort) (Shavell). Spectrum
  (Moran): formal regulation → quasi-regulation (state-backed soft law such as codes of
  practice) → self-regulation [LT-2 p3].
- Lessig uses "regulation" broadly, to include liability. The **pathetic dot** sits among
  Market (supply, demand, price), Architecture (physical space, code), Law (formal
  regulations, liability rules, property rules etc.) and Norms (culture, custom, soft law)
  [LT-2 p5–6].
  - **Trap:** "regulations" sit *inside* Law; they are not a fifth modality [official key:
    SampleQs p94, key p130].
- Car-radio example: the same constraint can be imposed through different modalities at
  different cost [LT-2 p7]. Technology is architecture, including choice architecture
  (Thaler & Sunstein) [LT-2 p8].
- **Bennett Moses**: "technology regulation" really means reducing harm from technology. The
  better framing is protecting values and minimising harm in a changing socio-technical
  landscape. She distinguishes technology-specific, risk-specific and technology-neutral rules
  [LT-2 p10–12].
- **Yeong Zee Kin**: "technology regulation" is a misnomer — law regulates how technology is
  **applied and supplied**. Laws are overarching and enforced ex post; regulations target
  specific roles with ex ante standards [LT-2 p13–14].
- **Collingridge dilemma** (David Collingridge, not Bennett Moses): early on there is too little
  information to regulate well; later the technology is entrenched and costly to change
  [LT-2 p17; LT-CS1 p68].
- **Legal tortoise and technological hare** — but "is it always true that law crawls?"
  [LT-2 p18].

### 2.3 Automobiles (Lecture 3)

- Courts first applied horse-carriage negligence rules to cars (the "law of the metal horse").
  Liability alone failed because accidents were many and defendants judgment-proof, so the UK
  Road Traffic Acts of the 1930s brought **compulsory insurance**. Compensation still required
  proof of negligence [LT-3 p6].
- Norms (jaywalking stigma, "accidents"), architecture (roads, sidewalks) and law (jaywalking
  offences) all shifted — a US-specific account [LT-3 p7]. "Lessig's theory plays out nearly note for note" — so look for "we've
  handled this before" [LT-3 p9].

### 2.4 Electronic transactions (Lecture 4)

- The ETA is the lecturer's case study in regulating the early internet [LT-CS1n p5; LT-4 p4]:
  - s 3 states its purposes;
  - ss 6–8 give electronic records, writing and signatures functional equivalence;
  - ss 11–16 say contract formation is not undermined solely because it is electronic,
    including automated message systems;
  - carve-outs (wills, trusts, real property) are in s 4. Bills of lading and negotiable
    instruments are no longer carved out (removed in 2021; see clk-m-1-24) [SSO-ETA First
    Sch].
- Its roots are the UN Model Law on E-Commerce (1996). Principles: non-discrimination,
  functional equivalence, technology neutrality. The Model Law on Electronic Transferable
  Records was adopted recently [LT-4 p9].
- **Why the ETA:** the rules were clear offline but uncertain in *application* online, so the
  ETA **clarified / reaffirmed** that familiar contract rules apply [LT-4 p5, p13; LT-CS1 p67].
  It was not a response to a court ruling and did not rewrite offer and acceptance — per the
  official sample key, not the slides [SampleQs p93 Q1, key p130].
- **Chwee Kin Keong v Digilandmall [2005] 1 SLR(R) 502**: a typo priced printers at $66
  instead of $3,854.
  - It was "common ground" that ordinary formation principles apply to internet contracts
    [29] [LT-4 p11]; the court did not refer to the ETA, which may have played a background
    role — per the official sample key [SampleQs p95 Q8, key p130].
  - The lecturer's point: did the ETA play a background role? [LT-4 p10].
  - Mistake holding (actual knowledge, including wilful blindness, at common law; equity
    wider): seniors' headnote only [Nic-LT Cases p2–3].
- **SM Integrated Transware v Schenker [2005] 2 SLR(R) 651**: the ETA's exclusion of Civil Law
  Act s 6 matters does not mean electronic means can't satisfy s 6. Construing CLA s 6(d)
  itself, the emails were "in writing" [85] [LT-4 p12].
- **ETA provisions worth knowing.** These cite the 2011 Rev Ed. On 3 Nov 2026 the section
  numbers (ss 4, 6–8, 13–16, 26) were checked against the current Act and are unchanged; the
  2021 amendments inserted Part 2A (ss 16A onward) on electronic transferable records
  [SSO-ETA]:
  - **s 13**: an email is received when it can be retrieved at a designated address; at an
    undesignated address, only when it can be retrieved **and** the addressee is aware of it
    [Nic-ETA p14–15];
  - **s 14**: a proposal open to the public, including interactive ordering, is an
    **invitation to make offers** unless it clearly shows intention to be bound
    [Nic-ETA p15];
  - **s 16**: an input error made by a natural person with no chance to correct it can be
    withdrawn if notified promptly and no material benefit was received [Nic-ETA p15–16];
  - **s 26**: a network service provider is not liable for third-party material it merely
    gives access to [Nic-ETA p24–25].

### 2.5 Digital platforms (Lecture 5)

- Platforms cut transaction costs as middlemen through four sources:
  - economies of concentration;
  - economies of scale;
  - predictability;
  - **network externalities** — more participants make the platform more valuable to everyone
    else [LT-5 p3–5].
- Network effects can be cross-side (more buyers help sellers) or same-side; Tinder has
  negative same-side effects. Critical mass matters [LT-5 p6–8].
- **Uber v Aslam [2021] UKSC 5** (principles only; UK law not examined) [LT-5 p10]:
  - the statute protects vulnerable workers, so the written contract is not the starting point
    [76]–[77];
  - control shows subordination [87]; freedom to log on doesn't defeat worker status [90];
  - five findings [93] [LT-5 p13–19]:
    1. Uber fixes pay;
    2. it dictates the contract terms;
    3. it constrains ride acceptance;
    4. it controls delivery of the service, including ratings;
    5. it restricts driver–passenger contact.
  - L&T lesson: platforms "always cry 'no control'", but code-as-law reveals control
    [LT-5 p20].
  - Drivers were held "workers"; whether they are also employees was left open [LT-CS2n p2].
- **Platform Workers Act 2024 (Singapore)**: the Tripartite Workgroup (20,000+ workers
  consulted) recommended that platform workers **not** be classed as employees, but that
  platforms with management control give basic protections such as WICA-level work-injury
  compensation; the government accepted all 12 recommendations and passed the PWA [LT-5 p21].
  - s 5(1) platform worker: (a) agreement with operator; (b) subject to its management
    control; (c) paid or benefited; (d) in Singapore [LT-5 p22].
  - **s 6(1) management control requires all three** [LT-5 p23]:
    - (a) the platform uses data on service users and non-employee workers for a specified
      purpose — eligibility, task allocation, payment, or any other prescribed purpose;
    - (b) without regular or routine human intervention;
    - (c) it imposes a Second Schedule restriction.
  - s 6(3): requirements imposed under written law don't count. s 6(4) presumes control from
    a platform work agreement plus requiring tasks to be accessed via the operator's **app or
    website** [LT-5 p24].
  - Second Schedule: how or when the work is done or its quality; price negotiation; building
    one's own clientele; hours or number of tasks; systematic incentives or penalties
    [LT-5 p25].
- **Online defamation**: Singapore has no equivalent of US s 230 [LT-CS2n p2–3].
  - **LHL v Leong Sze Hian [2021] SGHC 66**: sharing a link with no comment = publication of
    the article. Platform liability was not in issue [LT-5 p29–30].
  - **Fairfax v Voller [2021] HCA 27** (5:2): **Fairfax, not Facebook**, published the users'
    comments on its page. Intentional participation means voluntary and active, regardless of
    knowledge of the content [62], [66]. It ran the page for commercial readership [101]–[102].
    So **business users** of platforms can be liable [LT-5 p31–36].

### 2.6 Blockchain (Lecture 6)

- Original definition: a **solution to the double-spending problem** using a peer-to-peer
  distributed timestamp server [LT-6 p4]. More generally: secure, decentralised storage without
  a trusted third party — **"trust in code"** instead of banks, law or reputation
  [LT-6 p9–10, 24; official key: SampleQs p95, key p130].
- Hash chaining (SHA-256) plus a consensus mechanism (proof of work: find a hash starting
  "00000"; the longest chain wins). Altering a block means redoing it and every later block
  [LT-6 p16–19]. Other mechanisms include proof of stake [LT-6 p21].
- Tamper-resistance is **probabilistic**: an attacker with 50%+ of compute, an easy puzzle or
  luck can win (Ethereum Classic suffered a 51% attack) [LT-6 p23].
- Uses: tokens, NFTs, land registers, smart contracts [LT-6 p24].
- **ByBit Fintech v Ho Kai Xin [2023] SGHC 199**: crypto assets satisfy Ainsworth and are a
  **thing in action**, an incorporeal property right enforceable in court [31]–[36]
  [LT-6 p25–26].

### 2.7 Artificial intelligence (Lecture 7)

- Turing replaced "can machines think?" with the imitation game. Russell & Norvig classify AI
  as thinking or acting, humanly or rationally [LT-7 p5–6].
- **Symbolic** (rules-based: explicit rules and knowledge bases, e.g. Susskind's 1987 divorce
  expert system) vs **statistical / machine learning** (implicit patterns learned from data)
  [LT-7 p7–12].
  - Rule-writing is costly, so ML codes by example [LT-7 p11].
  - Simple ML models can be "very explainable" [LT-7 p15].
- **"Learning"** = adjusting numerical coefficients (weights or parameters) until the fit is
  stable [LT-7 p16–18]. Example: a sentencing regression on Vasentha, 8.98 g → about 12.2
  years [LT-7 p17–19].
- **Language model**: delete words from a corpus and train the model to predict them.
  **"Large"** = a large number of parameters (GPT-3.5: 175 billion). Generative AI is a subset
  of predictive AI and is not new (attention 2017, BERT 2019) [LT-7 p22–25].
- All of it is matrix maths; "learning" and "neurons" are **metaphors**, but "just maths" ≠
  nothing to worry about [LT-7 p26, p33].
- **Legal dispositionism** (Soh — author per [LT-Plan p5]):
  - we attribute behaviour to an agent's internal "disposition" (the Fundamental Attribution
    Error) and overlook the "situation" — regulators, makers, programmers, operators, users;
  - the "missing person problem": law assumes a person drives, contracts or argues;
  - **DABUS** (AI as patent inventor) failed everywhere [LT-7 p29–32].

### 3.1 Civil law and the codes

- Common law: largely precedent-based. Civil law: **codified**; the code is the first port of
  call; case law "does not carry the same weight" [Cmp-1 p3; Cmp-1T p2].
- **No stare decisis**: no single decision binds. Once case law is uniform, courts treat it as
  **persuasive** — *jurisprudence constante* (from French law). Conflicting decisions carry no
  weight [Cmp-1 p15; Cmp-1T p9].
- **Doctrine** (scholarship) is a significant source. In Indonesia, old Dutch scholars'
  readings of the 1847 Code are a source of law [Cmp-1T p4].
- **Indonesia**: the Dutch Civil Code (KUHPerdata) has been in force since 1847, and its
  Napoleonic-based text is still the backbone of contract law. It has **four books** [Cmp-1 p6;
  Cmp-1T p3]:
  - Book 1 — persons;
  - Book 2 — goods/assets;
  - Book 3 — obligations/contracts (Arts 1233–1456);
  - Book 4 — evidence and prescription.

  There is no official Indonesian text; the Dutch text is authoritative, so the Code is rarely
  amended. New statutes override parts of it instead (1960 agrarian law, 1974 marriage law)
  [Cmp-1T p4]. Breach of contract ("default") and an unlawful act are separate causes of action
  that are often pleaded together [Cmp-1T p3–4].
- Indonesian statute style: "Law No x of year", with **implementing regulations** that may come
  late. Example: the toll-road law (Law 2/2022 amends Law 38/2004; GR 23/2024 replaced
  GR 15/2005) [Cmp-1 p7–9; Cmp-1T p4–5]. Language Law 24/2009: contracts must be in Indonesian.
  A 2023 Supreme Court circular says a contract without an Indonesian version is annullable only
  if the omission was not in good faith [Cmp-1T p5–6].
- **Thailand**: the 2017 Constitution is supreme; statute is the main source. The Civil and
  Commercial Code (CCC) combines civil and commercial law in one code, is German-model, and has
  **six books** [Cmp-1 p11–12; Cmp-1T p6–8]:
  - Book 1 — general principles;
  - Book 2 — obligations, including torts (Title 5);
  - Book 3 — specific contracts;
  - Book 4 — property;
  - Book 5 — family;
  - Book 6 — succession.

  The CCC is not exhaustive: there are separate statutes and separate procedure codes
  [Cmp-1T p7].
- The **"Obligations"** book covers obligations from contract, from law and relationships, and
  statutory and tort claims [Cmp-1 p11; official key: SampleQs p98, key p130].

### 3.2 Contract

- **Formation (civil law)**: (1) consent; (2) capacity — for juristic persons, limited to acts
  useful for their purpose; (3) lawful and certain content that does not derogate from public
  order. **No consideration** [Cmp-2 p2; Cmp-2T p1]. Common law needs offer, acceptance,
  consideration and intention, judged objectively [Cmp-2T p1].
- **Interpretation, Indonesia** (Arts 1342–1351) [Cmp-2 p3]:
  - Art 1342: clear words → no interpretation;
  - only if unclear: 1343 intent of the parties over the literal sense; 1344 prefer the
    enforceable meaning; 1345 nature of the agreement; 1346 local custom; 1347 customary terms
    implied; 1348 read as a whole; **1349 in doubt, against the party who requested the
    agreement and in favour of the party who bound himself**; 1350 general words cover what was
    clearly intended; 1351 an example does not restrict.

  The transcript applies Art 1343 to *clear* words; the slide says it applies where wording
  is open to several interpretations — follow the slide [Cmp-2T p2 vs Cmp-2 p3].
- **Interpretation, Thailand**:
  - **s 171**: real intention over literal words (SC 11107/2555: "investment" contracts were
    really loans) [Cmp-2 p4];
  - **s 368**: interpret as good faith requires, taking custom into account (SC 1375/2505:
    jute-packing custom) [Cmp-2 p4];
  - clear wording means no need to look outside the document (SC 2210/2526) [Cmp-2 p4];
  - gap-fillers [Cmp-2 p5]: **s 10** prefer the meaning that gives effect; **s 11** in doubt,
    in favour of the obligor; **s 12** words beat figures; **s 13** the lowest amount governs;
    **s 14** if language versions differ, the **Thai text** prevails.
- **Pre-contract negotiations**: civil law is "much more expansive" in admitting them, but they
  are not conclusive; common law generally excludes them [Cmp-2 p6; Cmp-2T p5].
- **Good faith, Thailand**:
  - **s 6**: good faith is *presumed* (SC 9158/2539) [Cmp-2 p7];
  - **s 368**: absent custom, read terms as an ordinarily prudent person in good faith would
    (SC 478/2544: "use the path" includes vehicles) [Cmp-2 p7; Cmp-2T p6];
  - good faith governs interpretation **and performance**, applies in contract and tort, and
    can override express terms or imply a duty [Cmp-2T p6–7];
  - **s 5** (duty of good faith in exercising rights and performing obligations) appears only
    in the optional Pongsapan article, not the slides [Pongsapan p17].
- **s 421 (abuse of rights)**: exercising a right whose only purpose is to injure another is
  wrongful (SC 6599/2559 supermarket coupons [Cmp-2 p8]). The actor must *aim* to cause
  unilateral injury (SC 1992/2538) [Cmp-3 p4].
- **Remedies** [Cmp-2 p9; Cmp-2T p7–9]:
  - civil law **prefers specific performance** (refused in France, Germany and systems
    influenced by the French code only if performance is impossible); common law makes damages the rule and specific performance the exception;
  - no punitive damages in most civil-law systems;
  - lost profits are compensable;
  - termination generally needs a **material/fundamental breach**, but damages do not —
    except damages *in lieu of performance*, which are tantamount to termination (Germany)
    [Cmp-2T p9];
  - common law: direct vs consequential loss; indirect loss only if contemplated as probable
    by both parties at contracting [Cmp-2T p8].
- **Reducing damages**: contributory fault (conduct before or at the time of damage) is distinct
  from mitigation (conduct after awareness); benefits gained from the breach also reduce
  recovery. France has no general duty to mitigate in tort and only exceptionally in contract;
  common law requires mitigation and reimburses mitigation costs even if unsuccessful
  [Cmp-2T p9].
- **Notice / last chance**: civil law commonly requires notifying the breach or requesting
  performance to give a "last chance"; France: no damages until the defaulting party is put on
  formal notice, even with a fixed deadline [Cmp-2T p10].
- **Election of remedies is critical**: the code fixes what relief follows termination.
  Failing to elect and notify promptly can mean you are **deemed to insist on performance**,
  with only delay damages left. To claim damages the obligee must **immediately** tell the
  obligor it renounces performance [Cmp-2 p11]. Positive damages = position as if performed
  (TCO art 126); negative = as if never made (art 125/3); art 112 is general, arts 117–125
  govern default, art 235 confirms rescission and the duty to notify. The case study is Turkish
  law; the
  lecturer says the Indonesian and Thai codes are "similar" but gives no article numbers
  [Cmp-2 p9–11; Cmp-2T p10–11].

### 3.3 Delict

- **Indonesia, Art 1365**: every unlawful act causing damage obliges the wrongdoer to
  compensate [Cmp-3 p2].
  - Elements: an unlawful act (intentional or not), fault (intent or negligence), damage
    (material **or immaterial**), causation [Cmp-3 p2].
  - Compensation: money for lost assets, repair, cost of restoration, and compensation for
    **suffering or reputational harm** [Cmp-3 p3].
- **Thailand, s 420**: a person who wilfully or negligently and unlawfully injures the life,
  body, health, liberty, property or any right of another must compensate [Cmp-3 p4; Cmp-1
  p13].
  - Vicarious liability: s 425 employer jointly liable for acts in the course of employment;
    ss 427–428 principal/agent and employer/contractor; s 437 vehicles (unless force majeure
    or the victim's fault) [Cmp-1 p13].
  - s 438: the court fixes the manner and extent of compensation [Cmp-1 p13].
  - **s 448 prescription**: 1 year from knowledge of the act and of the wrongdoer, or 10 years
    from the act, whichever is first; a longer criminal prescription applies if the act is also
    a crime [Cmp-1 p13; Cmp-1T p8–9].
- Thai torts sit **inside** Book 2 (Obligations) [Cmp-1 p11].

### 3.4 Civil litigation

- **Jurisdiction, Indonesia**: **absolute competence** (subject matter) vs **relative
  competence** (geography or domicile) [Cmp-4 p2]. Art 118(1) HIR: sue where the defendant is
  domiciled [Cmp-4 p3].
  - The defendant "can file an exception to this competence", which "must be decided first
    before the judge rules on the merits" [Cmp-4 p4] — read with p3 as relative competence;
    Cyril instead ties "decided first" to absolute competence (Art 134 HIR), not on the slide.
  - Lack of absolute competence can be raised **at any time** and the court recognises it on
    its own motion [Cmp-4 p4].
  - District courts can only register, set aside or enforce international awards under the
    Arbitration Law [Cmp-4 p4].
- **Jurisdiction, Thailand**: CPC s 4 — sue where the cause of action arose or where the
  defendant is domiciled. There are **four specialised first-instance courts with nationwide
  jurisdiction**: Central Labour; Central IP & International Trade; Central Tax; Central
  Bankruptcy. Most appeals go straight to the Supreme Court [Cmp-4 p5].
- **Ex aequo et bono, Indonesia** [Cmp-4 p6–7]:
  - parties may ask for relief on justice and fairness;
  - judges must explore society's sense of justice (Art 5(1)) and may not refuse a case because
    the law is absent or unclear (Art 10(1));
  - arbitrators may decide ex aequo et bono (Arbitration Law Art 56(1)), but **mandatory law
    cannot be overridden**; without that mandate they apply the law like a judge.
- **Discovery**: civil law gathers evidence before filing, has very limited disclosure, and
  decides on what each party chooses to produce [Cmp-4 p8–9].
  - Indonesia: no compulsory disclosure; the court rarely orders specific documents [Guide p6].
  - Thailand: no duty to disclose; list relied-on documents ≥7 days before the hearing;
    originals only; a request succeeds only if you hold a copy or a reference to the document
    [Guide p9].
- **Privilege**: civil law imposes a **lawyer's duty of confidentiality** rather than client
  privilege, and in-house lawyers are "generally not bound by confidentiality" [Cmp-4 p9]. The
  APAC table gives Singapore ✓ for advice, litigation and in-house; China ✗ for all three
  [Cmp-4 p10].
  - **Indonesia** (Advocates Law 18/2003): no privilege, but a special right of
    confidentiality over all communications with **licensed Indonesian lawyers** — in-house
    only if licensed. It does **not** stop the KPK seizing documents. "Sans prejudice" letters
    between lawyers are inadmissible [Guide p5–7].
  - **Thailand**: no legal professional privilege. The **lawyer** (not the client) may refuse
    under the Penal Code / procedure codes, but the **court can compel** if refusal is
    unreasonable. The slides say in-house lawyers are generally not bound by the
    confidentiality obligation [Cmp-4 p9]; Cyril agrees [Cyril-Exam CMP §4.4.2]. Checked
    3 Nov 2026, resolved for the slides and the key; the conflict is recorded in clk-m-2-44. The official
    sample key says a Thai in-house counsel *cannot* claim privilege and the police *can*
    access the documents [SampleQs p98, key p130], while the 2019 Guide says disclosure "can only
    be compelled by the court" [Guide p10]. Go with the key in the exam; ask at the contact
    session if possible.

### 3.5 Fact pattern (used in both contact sessions)

- Sophie Publishing (SP, Jakarta) signed a one-year advertising deal with Suey Mak Mak (SMM,
  Thai leather goods). The editor SMM relied on, Ms Moreau, had told SP's lawyer by email two

- Clauses: US$100k per edition, payable even if SMM supplies no material (cl 8.2–8.3); entire
  agreement and non-reliance (cl 15); **Thai law** (cl 17.1); good-faith negotiation, then
  HK-seated ICC arbitration (cl 18) [Fact p2–3].
- Issues [Fact p3]:
  - can SMM terminate or avoid the contract (default, mistake/fraud, non-disclosure, s 420/421,
    Art 1365, good faith vs cl 15)?

## 4. Conflict of Laws (~14 MCQs)

**Scope rule:** "All examinable content will come solely from the lectures and the slides"; the
reading list is for information only [CoL-Plan p4]. Common-law rules, contract and tort claims
only [CoL-1 p5]. The slides do **not** cover REFJA/REFJ registration, COCAA (mentioned "good to
know" only), property, restitution or equitable claims [CoL-2 p6; CoL-3 p28; CoL-5 p3–21].

**Four stages** of a conflicts dispute: jurisdiction → interlocutory (e.g. ASIs) → merits
(choice of law) → post-trial (foreign judgments) [CoL-1 p4]. Choice of law rules apply to
precise *issues*, not whole claims or disputes [CoL-2 p5].

### 4.1 Choice of law

**Contract — 3-stage rule (Pacific Recreation)**: express → implied → objective choice
[CoL-2 p8].
- Express choice governs even with "absolutely no connection" to the dispute; the only limit is
  that it be *bona fide and legal* — not bona fide only if the *sole* purpose was to evade the
  operation of a foreign law (Peh Teck Quee) [CoL-2 p9].
- Implied and objective choice use the **same factors**: jurisdiction/arbitration clause,
  language/terminology, form of documents, preceding transaction, currency, parties'
  residence/business, place of performance [CoL-2 p10]. Implied = what the parties actually
  (impliedly) chose; objective = what reasonable businesspeople would have chosen [CoL-2 p10].
- Weight: place of performance (First Laser) and law of related transactions usually **very
  important**; "internationalised" language, forms and currencies (English, USD) usually **not
  important** [CoL-2 p11].
- Reach: validity, interpretation, enforceability, incorporation of terms, mode of performance,
  consequences of non-performance, third-party rights. **Not**: formation and agency (a similar
  3-stage rule, Solomon Lew) or capacity (this rule *or* D's domicile) [CoL-2 p12].

**Tort — double actionability (Rickshaw)**: the claim must succeed under **both** the lex fori
(Singapore) **and** the lex loci delicti — "double or nothing" [CoL-2 p14].
- Lex loci delicti = where *in substance* the cause of action arose, looking back once the tort
  is complete; **not** where C suffered damage (Man Diesel) [CoL-2 p15].
- Specific rules ("exhaustive for this course") [CoL-2 p16]:
  - negligence → where D's negligent conduct occurred (Man Diesel);
  - misrepresentation → where it was **received and acted upon** (JIO Minerals);
  - conspiracy → most-connected place on five factors (EFT Holdings): identity/location of
    conspirators, where agreements made, where concerted acts done, C's location, where losses
    suffered.
- **Flexible exception**: may disapply the lex fori, the lex loci delicti, or both (applying a
  third, more-connected law) [CoL-2 p17] — but only where the disapplied law is **"purely
  fortuitous"**; rare, strictly applied, must not "overwhelm" the rule [CoL-2 p18].

**Three limits on choice of law** [CoL-2 p20]
- *Procedure* → lex fori only. Includes evidence (admissibility, weight, presumptions) and
  availability of certain remedies (Goh Suan Hee) [CoL-2 p21–22]. **Limitation** is "now mostly
  governed by the lex causae" (Foreign Limitation Periods Act 2012 ss 3–4) [CoL-2 p22].
  Checked 3 Nov 2026: no library source gives FLPA detail beyond that pointer (CoL-Plan p4 and
  the seniors' notes repeat it), so stop at what the slides and key say; the official sample key
  treats a foreign 2-year period as barring a claim under FLPA s 3 [SampleQs p103, key p131].
- *Forum mandatory rules*: choice of law rules are common law, so a statute can override them,
  expressly (rare) or impliedly where it performs a **"regulatory" or "protective"** function
  rather than merely supplementing private rights (JIO Minerals); the slide calls the test
  "ambiguous" [CoL-2 p23].
- *Public policy*: a specific foreign rule is disapplied (treated as non-existent) only if
  enforcing it offends Singapore's **fundamental** public policy (Burswood: "fundamental
  principle of justice… deep-rooted tradition of the common weal") [CoL-2 p24]. Examples: race
  discrimination (Oppenheimer v Cattermole); separate legal personality; statutory policy —
  e.g. against unregulated gambling (Desert Palace) — marked with a "?" [CoL-2 p25].

### 4.2 Jurisdiction

Two questions: does jurisdiction **exist** (can) and should it be **exercised** (should)
[CoL-3 p5–6]. Four routes: service in, service out, jurisdiction clauses, submission by conduct
[CoL-3 p3].

**Service in**
- Existence: D served in Singapore (SCJA 1969 s 16(1)(a)(i)) [CoL-3 p8]. Individuals: present
  even fleetingly (Burgundy) [CoL-3 p9]. Companies: Singapore-incorporated (Companies Act s 387),
  or foreign with a place of business / carrying on business here (ss 368, 376); "carrying on
  business" = an agent carrying on the company's business from a fixed place for a sufficient
  period (Adams v Cape) [CoL-3 p9].
- Exercise: forum non conveniens (Spiliada); **D bears the burden** of showing Singapore is NOT
  the natural forum [CoL-3 p10]. Factors: party connections, witnesses and evidence, governing
  law, other proceedings [CoL-3 p11] — not mechanical (Lakshmi): party connections matter
  little in transnational disputes; witnesses and evidence matter little where teleconferencing
  and e-documents are available and the evidence is compellable; governing law matters little in
  factual disputes or where the laws are similar [CoL-3 p12].
- Stage 2: even if Singapore is not prima facie more appropriate, it keeps jurisdiction if C
  faces a **real and material risk of injustice** abroad (burden on C) — e.g. very short
  limitation, severe delay, blatantly unfair evidence rules, threats to safety [CoL-3 p13].
  "Yes" at either stage → Singapore exercises jurisdiction [CoL-3 p14].

**Service out**
- ROC 2021 O 8 r 1(1): service out **with the Court's approval** [CoL-3 p16]. Affidavit must show
  (PD 2021 para 63(2)): (a) good arguable case of a **sufficient nexus**; (b) Singapore is the
  **forum conveniens**; (c) a **serious question** to be tried [CoL-3 p16].
- Nexus heads (PD 63(3)) [CoL-3 p17–19]:
  - (d)(i) contract made in Singapore / essential step here (DJS Solutions); (d)(ii) made through
    an agent in Singapore; (d)(iii) governed by Singapore law; (d)(iv) Singapore jurisdiction
    clause;
  - (e) breach in Singapore, or breach elsewhere making performance in Singapore impossible
    (Recovery Vehicle 1);
  - (f)(i) tort with an act/omission in Singapore as an element; (f)(ii) damage suffered in
    Singapore — for a company, at its **place of incorporation**, not where its bank account is
    (MAN Diesel);
  - (p) cause of action arising in Singapore (Singapore is the lex loci delicti);
  - (m) enforcing a judgment/award — not covered beyond enforcement [CoL-5 p18].
- Exercise: **burden on C** to show Singapore IS the natural forum (Spiliada) [CoL-3 p20].

**Jurisdiction clauses**
- Existence: clause provides for service out (O 8 r 1(3)) or supplies the nexus (PD 63(3)(d)(iv),
  63(3)(r)) [CoL-3 p23].
- If the clause provides for service out: **no Court approval** needed and the court "will
  invariably exercise" jurisdiction [CoL-3 p24].
- If silent: **"strong cause"** test (Vinmar) — the FNC factors, plus substantial justice, plus
  abuse of process (D invoking the clause only to dodge conceded liability) [CoL-3 p25]. Stricter
  than natural forum; only factors unforeseeable at contracting get significant weight; applies
  in reverse — a Singapore court **stays** in favour of an exclusive foreign clause unless strong
  cause [CoL-3 p26].
- **Non-exclusive** clauses (Shanghai Turbo) [CoL-3 p27]: foreign court named → no stay unless
  Singapore is not the natural forum (the clause is just a factor); Singapore named → no stay
  unless strong cause ("a forum bias in favour of Singapore courts").
- COCAA 2016 / Hague 2005 — "not covered in this course" [CoL-3 p28].

**Submission by conduct** — its own head (SCJA s 16(1)(b)); cures defective service; not the
same as submission by agreement [CoL-3 p30]. Test: a "clear and unequivocal" step inconsistent
with objecting to jurisdiction (Reputation Administration v Spamhaus) [CoL-3 p31].
- **Submits**: defence on the merits; contesting summary judgment on the merits; seeking
  status-quo interim injunctions; contesting interim injunctions *if D could not avoid them by
  challenging jurisdiction* [CoL-3 p32].
- **Does not**: notice to contest (O 6 r 6(4)); challenging existence or natural forum
  (O 6 r 7(6)) [CoL-3 p32].
- Conflict: Isabel's notes say contesting interim injunctions never submits — follow the slide
  [Isabel p14 vs CoL-3 p32].

### 4.3 Anti-suit injunctions (ASIs)

- An ASI restrains its target from starting/continuing foreign proceedings; it prevents
  fragmentation and outflanking of the Singapore court [CoL-4 p3].
- Lakshmi factors: (a) D amenable to jurisdiction; (b) natural forum; (c) vexation/oppression to
  C; (d) injustice to D from losing foreign advantages; (e) breach of a jurisdiction agreement
  [CoL-4 p4]. Not a "test": (a) is a **prerequisite** for any injunction; (b)–(d) are the
  non-contractual route; (e) is the contractual route [CoL-4 p5].
- **Contractual**: Singapore jurisdiction clause → ASIs "readily granted" (Sun Travels) [CoL-4 p6]
  unless strong cause, or **unreasonable delay** with foreign proceedings advanced (delay is
  excused only if spent objecting to the foreign court's jurisdiction) [CoL-4 p7]. Where the
  parties chose a **foreign** court, Singapore will not act as an "international busybody"
  (Akai) [CoL-4 p6].
- **Non-contractual**: courts "generally reluctant" (comity) [CoL-4 p8]; need jurisdiction +
  Singapore the natural forum + vexatious or oppressive foreign proceedings (factor (d) usually
  subsumed); delay relevant [CoL-4 p9]. "Vexatious or oppressive" ≈ unconscionable (Kirkham v
  Trane): oppressive procedure, extreme inconvenience, or bad faith (suing abroad only to
  frustrate Singapore proceedings) [CoL-4 p10].

### 4.4 Foreign judgments (common-law action)

- A recognised judgment is enforced (money judgments) or founds estoppel [CoL-5 p4].
- Recognition needs (Giant Light): (1) the foreign court had **international jurisdiction** over
  the debtor; (2) judgment **final and conclusive on the merits** [CoL-5 p6].
- International jurisdiction exists only where the debtor was **claimant**, **present**,
  **submitted by conduct**, or bound by a **jurisdiction clause** for that court [CoL-5 p7].
  Service is not required (Pemberton v Hughes); a PD 63(3)-type nexus is **not** enough
  [CoL-5 p8].
- Final and conclusive (Bunga Melati 5): cannot be varied, reopened or set aside by the court
  that gave it; interim status-quo orders and "good arguable case" findings are not [CoL-5 p9].
  A first-instance judgment is final even if appealable; an interlocutory order is final only on
  the issues it had to finally decide (Lakshmi) [CoL-5 p10].
- Defences [CoL-5 p12]:
  - natural justice — notice + opportunity to be heard (Paulus Tannos) [CoL-5 p13];
  - fraud (Hong Pian Tee v Les Placements) — **intrinsic** (forged evidence, perjury) only on
    evidence the foreign court did **not** consider; **extrinsic** (bribing witnesses, tricking
    D into settling) even if it did [CoL-5 p14];
  - public policy — as in choice of law; also a "manifest, patent or egregious" error of
    Singapore law (Merck), or a judgment obtained in breach of a Singapore ASI (WSG Nimbus)
    [CoL-5 p15].
- Enforcement: action for a debt, usually summary judgment; nexus PD 63(3)(m); only for a
  **definite sum** — not liability findings, injunctions, specific performance or proprietary
  relief (Desert Palace) [CoL-5 p18].
- Estoppel: issue estoppel needs the same parties and the same issue finally decided (Merck)
  [CoL-5 p20]; Henderson extended res judicata covers issues that should have been raised (Merck)
  and parties who should have been joined (Humpuss), absent fresh evidence or a bona fide reason
  [CoL-5 p21].
