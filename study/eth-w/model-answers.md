## 2023 Q1 — Archie, Betty, Cheryl and the RPL facility

Question: PYP23 p2–3 (= SQ p4–5). Marks: 1.1 18, 1.2 18, 1.3 14. The official answer key is
SQ p109–113. It is written in numbered points; this is the same content in exam prose, with a
few points the key does not make (marked *beyond the key*). Pre-writes used: 0-11, 0-10,
0-6, 0-7, 0-8, 0-12.

### 1.1 Can Archie act for L? (18 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). In my view Archie may act for L only with RPL's informed written
consent. Without it, adequate safeguards would be very hard to achieve, because Archie himself
holds RPL's confidential information.

**Is RPL a former or a current client?** RPL has decided not to engage Archie for the bidding
process. On that basis RPL is a former client, and the governing rule is r 21 PCR, which is how
the examiners analyse it (SQ p109). *Beyond the key:* Archie has advised RPL "for many years".
If RPL still instructs him or Tate Law (TL) on other matters, it is a current client. Acting
for a bidder against it on the sale of its main asset would then also engage the firm's duty
of loyalty under r 20(1) PCR, which makes the case for declining stronger still.

**The r 21(2) test.** A legal practitioner or law practice must decline to act for a current
client if three things are true (r 21(2)(a)–(c) PCR):
(a) it holds confidential information of a former client that is protected by r 6;
(b) the current client's interest is, or may reasonably be expected to be, adverse to the
former client's; and
(c) that information may reasonably be expected to be material to acting for the current
client.

The duty of confidentiality survives the end of the retainer (r 21(1) PCR).

All three limbs are met. Archie knows RPL's minimum sale price, and he assisted RPL in
earlier confidential negotiations with interested buyers. That information is confidential
in nature (*Law Society of Singapore v Ravi s/o Madasamy* [2015] 3 SLR 1187 at [33]) and was
acquired in the engagement (r 6(2) PCR). L, as a bidder, wants to pay as little as possible,
and RPL, as seller, wants as much as possible, so their interests are directly adverse. In a
sealed-bid process, the seller's minimum price is the most material information a bidder could
have. Archie must therefore decline unless an exception applies.

**The exceptions.** First, under r 21(3) PCR, Archie may act if he has adequately advised RPL
to obtain independent legal advice and RPL gives informed written consent. That is the proper
first step, and given that RPL has engaged other lawyers, it may well agree. Second, if
consent is not forthcoming despite reasonable efforts, r 21(4) PCR allows Archie to act only
if two conditions are met. TL must put adequate safeguards in place at once to protect RPL's
information, in practice an information barrier. TL must also make reasonable efforts to notify
RPL of those safeguards and that TL will act for L.

Whether a barrier is adequate depends on:
(a) the nature of the work done for the former client;
(b) when the barrier was set up;
(c) the size of the firm;
(d) the physical location of its teams; and
(e) the number and seniority of the tainted lawyers
(*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at [68]).

The difficulty here is fundamental. A barrier works by keeping information away from the
lawyer who acts, but Archie is both the person who holds RPL's information and the person
who would act for L. No barrier can separate a lawyer from his own memory. Professor Pinsler's
commentary (optional reading, cited in the key) says safeguards must be "iron-tight" to be
effective. On these facts, r 21(4) is very unlikely to be satisfied with Archie as the acting
lawyer. *Beyond the key:* if consent is refused, a different TL partner with no access to RPL's
file might act behind a barrier set up immediately. Even then, the firm must weigh the risk
that RPL applies to restrain TL from acting. A court may restrain a firm where a fair-minded,
reasonably informed observer would think the administration of justice requires it, and a long
history between the firm and the former client is relevant (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann
Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29).

In conclusion, Archie may act for L only with RPL's informed written consent, given after RPL
has been advised to take independent advice. Without that consent he should decline. If TL
nevertheless acts under r 21(4), the barrier must be in place immediately and RPL must be
notified.

### 1.2 Was it acceptable for Betty to act for M? (18 marks)

The issue is whether TL may act for M through Betty while acting for L through Archie in the
same bidding process. It may do so only if it complies with r 20 PCR. Betty's decision to act
after only "discussing the matter with Archie" falls short of r 20.

**Rule 20 applies.** Rule 20 applies where a law practice intends to act for two or more
parties to a matter and a diversity of interests exists or may reasonably be expected to exist
between them (r 20(2)(a)–(b) PCR). L and M are rival bidders for the same facility. Each wants
to win at the lowest price, and each bid is sealed and confidential. TL is one law practice, so
Archie's client and Betty's client are both TL's clients in the same transaction. A diversity
of interests plainly exists.

**What r 20(3) requires before Betty acts.** Before accepting M's instructions, TL must
communicate directly with each of L and M (r 20(3)(a) PCR) to explain:
(a) how their interests diverge;
(b) that TL may be unable to disclose relevant information to one, or give one advice that
prejudices the other; and
(c) that TL must cease to act if it has difficulty advising on and dealing with their divergent
interests competently, evenly and consistently.

TL must also do three further things:
(a) advise each party to obtain independent legal advice (r 20(3)(b));
(b) if a party does not obtain it, keep a written record or written confirmation of that
(r 20(3)(d)); and
(c) obtain each party's informed consent in writing (r 20(3)(e)).

*The point that earns extra marks* (SQ p111): before Archie can even approach L to seek its
consent, Betty must first obtain M's consent to disclose to L that M is an interested bidder.
The fact that M has approached TL is itself confidential to M (r 6(2) PCR), so it cannot be
revealed to L without M's authority (r 6(3)(a) PCR). The same applies the other way. Betty
knows that Archie acts for L, and she may not tell M that without L's consent.

**Authorities.** A solicitor acting for several clients owes each at least the standard of
care owed to a single client, and cannot prefer one over another (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong Tan
& Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). Acting for multiple clients is a fiduciary
position. A firm that fails to manage the conflict risks being disqualified from acting for all
of them (*Law Society of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram* [2009] 4 SLR(R) 674 at [34]–[53]).
Consent must be genuinely informed. The courts have set out the steps needed to obtain it
(*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36 at [63]–[80]; *Kuek
Siang Wei v Kuek Siew Chew* [2015] 5 SLR 357). Preferring one client's interests over another's
is misconduct (*Law Society of Singapore v Ezekiel Peter Latimer* [2019] SGHC 92).

**Continuing duties and safeguards.** TL must remain vigilant throughout the retainer, tell
each client of any conflict that arises, and cease to act if it can no longer advise them
evenly (r 20(4), (6) PCR). In practice it must put effective information barriers between
Archie's team and Betty's team. These should include:
(a) physical and IT separation of the teams and files;
(b) written instructions to, and signed acknowledgements from, every lawyer and staff member
involved; and
(c) internal monitoring of whether the barrier is working (SQ p112).
*Beyond the key:* Archie and Betty are partners in the same corporate department, and Betty has
already "discussed the matter with Archie". The barrier must be set up at once, and TL should
check that nothing about L's bid or RPL's minimum price passed to Betty. If anything did, Betty
cannot act.

In conclusion, if TL obtains each client's informed written consent after the r 20(3)
explanations, secures M's consent before disclosing M's interest to L, and puts adequate
barriers in place, Archie may continue for L and Betty may act for M. Betty's decision to act
the next day without these steps was not acceptable.

### 1.3 Can Cheryl act for the DSA? (14 marks)

The issue is whether TL may also act for the DSA in the same sale while Archie acts for L and
Betty for M. In my view it should not. This multiple representation is unworkable even with
safeguards.

**Rule 20 applies again.** The DSA is a party to the same transaction, because it must approve
the sale. Its interests diverge from L's and M's, so r 20(2) PCR is engaged and the r 20(3)
steps would be required. The question is whether TL could advise all three competently,
evenly and consistently. If it cannot, it must not act, or must cease to act (r 20(3)(a),
(4), (6) PCR).

**Why it is unworkable.** The DSA can veto any sale at its sole discretion. It will approve only
if it is satisfied that the purchaser can operate the facility, and only if the successful
bidder provides a $5 million on-demand performance bond. Cheryl's role would therefore be to
advise the regulator on whether to approve each bidder and on what terms. Archie and Betty would
need to advise their clients on how to satisfy, negotiate with, or even challenge the DSA's
exercise of its discretion. M imports and exports drugs and has no manufacturing experience, so
the DSA has a particular reason to doubt M's capability and to veto M's bid. L's position is
also uncertain, as we are not told the nature of its business. If the DSA takes a position
against L or M, Archie and Betty would have to advise their clients against TL's own client.
Cheryl would have to advise the DSA on how to maintain that position (SQ p112–113).
Information barriers protect confidential information. They cannot cure the inability of one
firm to advise each side on opposite positions evenly (*Lie Hendri Rusli* at [48]–[50]).

*Beyond the key:* Cheryl already knows that Archie acts for L and Betty for M. She must not
tell the DSA without L's and M's consent (r 6(2) PCR), yet the DSA's assessment of bidders is
exactly where that knowledge would matter. Acting for the regulator would also risk the
appearance that TL's bidder clients have privileged access to the decision-maker.

In conclusion, Cheryl should decline to act for the DSA. If TL had already accepted the DSA's
instructions, it would have to cease to act for all the parties whose interests diverge,
unless it could continue for one with the others' informed written consent (r 20(7) PCR).
Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and
solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession
Act 1966.

*Source note: r 4, 6, 20, 21 PCR (PCR p9, p12–13, p35–40); SQ p109–113 (official key); Ravi
Madasamy [2015] SYL p3; Harsha SQ p110; Lim Oon Kuin L2 p22; Lie Hendri Rusli L2 p9;
Uthayasurian L2 p10–11; Mahidon SYL p3; Kuek Siang Wei and Ezekiel Peter Latimer [2019] SQ
p111.*

## 2023 Q2 — Yeager, Shigan Partners and KP's website

Question: PYP23 p3–4 (handwritten corrections on the scan: 2.1 and 2.2 ask about acting for
**R**, not B). Marks: 2.1 18, 2.2 18, 2.3 14. Pre-writes used: 0-9, 0-1, 0-22, 0-13, 0-12,
0-15, 0-23, 0-29, 0-30. No official answer exists.

### 2.1 Should Y cease to act for R? (18 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). In my view Y should cease to act for R and apply to be
discharged. Y is now a material witness on contested issues (r 11(3)(a) PCR), and Y's and
SP's own conduct is in issue (r 22 PCR). Before reaching that, it is necessary to rule out
the more absolute bar that would apply if B had also been Y's client.

**Was B also a client?** The first issue is whether Y or SP acted for B as well as R on the
JVA. If so, r 20(8) PCR and r 21 PCR would bar SP outright. B is unlikely to have been a
client. Where a law practice has prepared a document on behalf of two or more parties and
they become involved in a dispute arising from it, the practice must not represent any of
them (r 20(8) PCR), and no consent can cure this. A retainer need not be express. It may be
implied where, viewed objectively, an intention to form a solicitor-client relationship
ought fairly to be imputed to both parties (*Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin
Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64]–[73]). Here Y was instructed by and advised R. The
JVA recites that both parties took independent legal advice, and B told Y that a lawyer
friend in Germany would review the draft. On Y's account he never advised B, so the JVA
was prepared on R's behalf alone and r 20(8) does not apply. However, B now pleads that Y
told him it would be "fine" to sign without a Singapore lawyer. If that is proved, it is
legal advice given to B and could support an argument that B looked to Y for protection
(*Ahmad Khalis* at [74]). I therefore conclude that B was probably not a client, but the
point is itself a disputed question of fact. That leads to the decisive problem.

**Y as a material witness.** The next issue is whether Y will be required to give evidence
material to a contested issue. He will, so he must apply to be discharged. Where it is
known or appears that a legal practitioner will be required to give evidence material to
the determination of any contested issue, the practitioner must not accept instructions
from any party to the case. If already acting in proceedings, the practitioner must apply
to be discharged (r 11(3)(a)(i)–(ii) PCR). PD 1.7.1 likewise provides that a practitioner
must not act where they have reason to believe they will be a witness on a material and
disputed question of fact. It permits an affidavit only on formal or undisputed facts. The
rule rests on the principle that the duty to advance the client's case is subject to the
duties owed as an officer of the court (r 11(1) PCR). The witness's duty to tell the whole
truth and the advocate's duty to present the client's best case should not be combined in
one person. In *Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], the solicitor
whose account of the transaction would be contested should not have acted. Here the
pleadings put two things directly in issue. The first is what Y said to B at the Meeting:
B says Y told him he need not appoint a Singapore lawyer and never pointed out the
Independent Advice Clause. The second is what B told Y on the telephone: R pleads that B
said a German lawyer would advise him. These matters go to whether B can resist the JVA or
the Independent Advice Clause, so they are material. Y is the only person who can give
R's side. The call was between B and Y alone. The former associate is overseas and
presumed uncontactable. Nobody knows who B's German lawyer was. The contemporaneous
attendance notes are Y's own and can only be proved through Y, who will be cross-examined
on them. None of this is formal or undisputed, so the PD 1.7.1 exception does not help.
It is also not a bare assertion by KP, because the dispute is on the pleadings. Y is
therefore a material witness and must apply to be discharged under r 11(3)(a)(ii).

**Conflict between Y's/SP's interests and R's.** The third issue is whether Y and SP have
their own interest in the matter that may conflict with R's. They do, and this is an
independent reason to step aside. A legal practitioner or law practice must not act where
there is, or may reasonably be expected to be, a conflict between the duty to serve the
client's best interests and its own interests (r 22(2) PCR). Where the practitioner or the
practice has an adverse interest, it must withdraw unless it makes full and frank
disclosure, advises the client to obtain independent legal advice, ensures the client does
not think the practice is protecting the client's interests, and obtains the client's
informed written consent (r 22(3)(a), (4)(a) PCR). In *Ho Kon Kim* at [61]–[63], the firm
whose handling of the transaction was itself in issue should not have acted. In *Then Khek
Khoon v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2012] 2 SLR 451 at [30]–[36], the court held that r 22
protects the client, so it is R's position that matters, not KP's complaint. Here B's
Defence attacks the JVA "drafted by Y/SP" as unfair and says Y misled him. If B's account
is accepted, Y would face findings that he gave an unrepresented, adverse party legal
advice and let him think his interests were protected, in breach of r 8(2)(a)–(b) PCR. SP
might also face a claim from R if the Independent Advice Clause fails. Y therefore has a
personal interest in vindicating his own drafting, conduct and notes, which may not match
R's best interests: for example, on whether to settle or whether to concede points about
the Meeting. Even apart from r 11, Y could continue only after full disclosure, advice to
take independent advice, and R's informed written consent. Consent cannot cure the r 11(3)(a)
problem in any event.

**KP's letter.** The fourth issue is how to treat KP's demand that SP withdraw. Y should
take it seriously, although the reason to withdraw is the PCR, not KP's demand. Apart from
the PCR, the court has a supervisory jurisdiction to restrain a lawyer from acting where a
fair-minded, reasonably informed member of the public would conclude that the proper
administration of justice requires it (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022]
SGCA 29). KP's letter names no rule, and an opponent cannot dictate a party's choice of
counsel. But a fair-minded observer would doubt that the lawyer whose own words at the
Meeting are in issue can also present R's case. If Y stays on, KP is likely to apply to
restrain SP, which would cost R time and money later in the case. Y should reply
courteously that he is applying to be discharged, without accepting that any conflict
exists between R and SP.

**How to withdraw.** Withdrawal is permitted where it is necessary to avoid contravening the
Rules (r 26(5)(f) PCR). When withdrawing, Y must take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable
harm to R. That includes giving reasonable notice, giving R reasonable time to instruct
new lawyers, and cooperating with them (r 26(6)(a) PCR). Y must also abide by R's choice
of new counsel (r 26(6)(b) PCR). Y should explain to R in writing why he must stop
(r 5(2)(b) PCR), apply to court to be discharged, preserve the file and the attendance
notes and hand them over, and make himself available as R's witness. Withdrawing now, at
the pleadings stage, causes R the least harm.

In conclusion, Y should cease to act for R and apply to be discharged. Continuing would
breach r 11(3)(a) PCR and PD 1.7.1, risks breaching r 22, and invites an application to
restrain SP. Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an
advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the
Legal Profession Act 1966.

### 2.2 Can A take over conduct and act for R? (18 marks)

The issue is whether, with Y gone, A as SP's other partner can take over. Rule 11(3)(b) PCR
allows this in principle, but on these facts A should not. SP should advise R to instruct
another firm.

**Rule 11(3)(b): would A acting prejudice the administration of justice?** Even where one
lawyer will be a material witness, "the law practice in which the legal practitioner
practises, or a member of that law practice, may represent or continue to represent any
party to that case, unless doing so would prejudice the administration of justice"
(r 11(3)(b) PCR). The PCR therefore does not disqualify the whole firm automatically. The
question is whether A's involvement would prejudice the administration of justice. In
*Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], the Court of Appeal said that
neither the conveyancing partner, who was a material witness, nor the litigation partner
of the same firm should have acted. This was because the firm's own handling of the
transaction was in issue. The court's wider supervisory jurisdiction asks the same kind of
question: whether a fair-minded, reasonably informed observer would think the proper
administration of justice requires the firm to be restrained (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah &
Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29).

Several facts favour A. He was not involved in the JVA at any point, has no personal
knowledge of the Meeting or the telephone call, and will not be a witness. Rule 11(3)(b)
expressly contemplates a colleague carrying on. R's choice of counsel deserves respect
(r 26(6)(b) PCR), and SP already knows the file, which saves R costs.

The facts against A are stronger. First, the firm's own conduct is attacked. B pleads that
the JVA drafted by "Y/SP" was unfair and that SP's partner misled him. A is one of only two
partners, so he shares SP's interest in defeating those allegations. That interest covers
the firm's reputation, its possible liability to R, and Y's disciplinary exposure. This is
an interest of the law practice adverse to R's (r 22(2), (4)(a) PCR). Second, A would have
to lead evidence-in-chief from his own senior partner, who is also his former supervisor
(Y has 18 years' standing and trained A; A has 8 years and was only recently made
partner). A would have to present Y's credibility and cross-examine B, whose whole case is
that Y misled him. In a two-partner firm, A cannot credibly be seen as independent of Y.
The risk is that A runs the case in the way that best protects Y and SP rather than R, for
example by resisting a sensible settlement or fighting over the attendance notes. Third,
information barriers are no answer. The problem is not confidential information but the
firm's own interest and A's relationship with the witness, and a two-partner firm cannot
be meaningfully separated anyway. Fourth, if the court later found that B had looked to Y
for advice, the r 20(8) and r 21 PCR objections would bind the whole law practice, A
included. Finally, a fair-minded observer would very likely see SP's continued
involvement as compromising the appearance of justice. A restraint application succeeding
mid-trial would harm R far more than a change of firm now.

In conclusion, A should not take over. If R still wishes to keep SP after full and frank
disclosure of the firm's interest, advice to take independent legal advice, and R's
informed written consent (r 22(4)(a)(i)–(iv) PCR), A could act only if satisfied that this
would not prejudice the administration of justice under r 11(3)(b). On these facts that is
doubtful. The prudent course is for R to instruct an independent firm, with SP cooperating
in the handover (r 26(6) PCR).

### 2.3 Are the statements on KP's website permissible? (14 marks)

The issue is whether KP's website statements comply with Part 5 of the PCR. Neither does.
A legal practitioner must ensure that publicity about their practice complies with the
Rules, whether they publish it or someone else does on their behalf. On becoming aware of
an impropriety, the practitioner must use best endeavours to have it rectified or
withdrawn (r 42(1)–(2) PCR). When publicising a practice, a lawyer must ensure that any
claim to expertise or specialisation can be justified. The publicity must not mention the
firm's success rate, and must not compare or criticise the fees charged by, or the quality
of services of, any other legal practitioner (r 43(1)(a)–(c) PCR). A claim to expertise
may be justified by qualifications, experience, the proportion of working time spent,
level of success, the complexity and significance of matters handled, and peer assessment
(r 43(2) PCR). Publicity must not be likely to diminish public confidence in the
profession, and must not be such that it may reasonably be regarded as misleading,
deceptive, inaccurate, false or unbefitting the dignity of the profession (r 44(1)(a)–(b)
PCR). Under r 44(2) PCR, publicity is misleading if it contains a material
misrepresentation, contains unverifiable information, or is likely to create an
unjustified expectation about results.

**Statement (i).** "We are the best of the best because unlike other lawyers, we never give
up fighting for our clients for ten years at least" is not permissible. "Best of the best"
claims superiority over every other practice. No r 43(2) factor could justify it, and it
cannot be verified (r 43(1)(a), r 44(2)(c)). "Unlike other lawyers, we never give up" is an
express comparison with other lawyers, and implies they do give up, which criticises the
quality of their services. This is the very thing r 43(1)(c) forbids. "Fighting … for ten
years at least" cannot be verified either. It also presents prolonged litigation as a
virtue, which sits badly with a lawyer's duty to assist in the administration of justice
(r 9(1)(a) PCR) and is likely to diminish public confidence in the profession (r 44(1)(a)).
Statement (i) breaches r 43(1)(a), r 43(1)(c) and r 44.

**Statement (ii).** "Work injuries, employment claims? Our expert litigators will crush your
enemies and recover millions for you!" is also not permissible as written. "Expert
litigators" is a claim to expertise. It is allowed only if KP can justify it on the
r 43(2) factors, such as the experience of its litigators in work-injury and employment
claims and the proportion of their time spent on such work. We are not told that it can,
and the burden is on KP. "Crush your enemies" presents litigation as a personal vendetta.
It is aggressive language unbefitting the dignity of the profession (r 44(1)(b)) and
likely to diminish public confidence in it (r 44(1)(a)). "Recover millions for you"
promises a result that no lawyer can promise. What a claimant recovers depends on the
facts and the law, not the lawyer. The phrase is therefore likely to create an unjustified
expectation about results (r 44(2)(d)) and is unverifiable (r 44(2)(c)). That is
especially serious because the advertisement targets injured workers and employees, who
are likely to be vulnerable.

In conclusion, both statements breach r 43 and r 44 PCR. The KP lawyers responsible must
have them withdrawn or rewritten in factual, verifiable terms (r 42(2) PCR). The Council

misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under
s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

### Model answer

**(a) Priya's conduct in Part A (15 marks)**

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). Priya's conduct breaches r 25, r 22, r 6 and r 8(2) PCR.

*Gift to Mei.* The first issue is whether Priya could draft a will leaving $300,000 to her
own daughter. She could not. Where a client intends to make a significant gift, by will or
otherwise, to an immediate family member of the legal practitioner, the practitioner must
not act for the client in relation to the gift and must advise the client to obtain
independent legal advice about it (r 25(1)(a)(iii)(A), r 25(2) PCR). The course materials
take this from *Wan Hui Hong James* (L3 p21–22). The proper steps are to explain why
independent advice is needed, let the client choose another lawyer, and record the advice
in writing. Mei is Priya's daughter. A gift of $300,000 out of an estate of about $900,000
is a third of Mr Goh's assets and plainly significant, especially as it displaces his
nephew Kelvin. Drafting the very will that makes the gift is acting "in relation to the
gift". Telling Mr Goh that independent advice is "honestly … not necessary" is the
opposite of advising him to obtain it. Priya breached both limbs of r 25(2) and should not
have drafted the will.

*Priya's own interest.* The second issue is whether Priya's family interest conflicted
with her duty to Mr Goh. It did. A lawyer must not act where there is, or may reasonably be
expected to be, a conflict between the duty to serve the client's best interests and the
lawyer's own interests. Where an immediate family member has an interest adverse to the
client's, the lawyer must withdraw unless they make full and frank disclosure, advise
independent legal advice, ensure the client does not think the lawyer is protecting the
client's interests, and obtain informed written consent (r 22(2), (3)(a) PCR). In *Lee
Suet Fern* the court described a lawyer whose family stood to benefit as having patently
divided loyalties (L3 p25). Mei benefits from every dollar Mr Goh leaves her, and Priya
gave Mr Goh none of the protections r 22(3)(a) requires. That breached r 22. Priya may
continue on the flat sale only if that matter is unaffected by the gift.

*An elderly and recently bereaved client.* Priya also had to take particular care because
Mr Goh is 81, recently widowed and emotionally reliant on Mei's visits. Those are classic
signs of a risk of undue influence. The cases on elderly clients (*Low Ah Cheow*, *Chee Mu
Lin Muriel*, L1 p9), together with the duty to keep proper contemporaneous records
(r 5(2)(k) PCR), point to seeing Mr Goh alone, checking his capacity and understanding,
and keeping a full attendance note. Priya did none of this, which compounds the r 25 and
r 22 breaches.

*What Priya told Kelvin.* The third issue is whether Priya could reveal the effect of the
new will to Kelvin. She could not. A lawyer must not knowingly disclose information that is
confidential to the client and acquired in the course of the engagement (r 6(2) PCR),
unless an exception in r 6(3) applies, such as the client's authority. The contents of a
living client's will are confidential, and Mr Goh did not authorise disclosure. Telling
Kelvin that "your share is only a bit smaller" disclosed them, in breach of r 6(2).

*Kelvin as an unrepresented person.* The fourth issue is whether Priya dealt properly with
Kelvin, who had no lawyer. She did not. When dealing on a client's behalf with someone not
represented by a lawyer, a lawyer must decline to give that person any legal advice other
than to obtain independent advice, if the lawyer knows their interests are or may be
adverse to the client's. The lawyer must also take reasonable steps so that the person
does not think the lawyer is protecting their interests (r 8(2)(a)–(b) PCR). Non-clients
who may reasonably think the lawyer is looking after them must be clearly disabused (*Law
Society of Singapore v Ganesan Krishnan* [2003] 2 SLR(R) 251; *Law Society of Singapore v
Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [74]). Kelvin's interest is adverse,
because he was the main beneficiary and has lost out to Mei. "I'm looking after the whole
family's interests" creates exactly the impression r 8(2)(b) forbids. "The new will is fair
to everyone" is a view on the merits that he should have been told to get independent
advice about. Given that her own daughter benefits, it is also misleading, contrary to
r 8(1)(a) and r 8(3)(b) PCR.

In conclusion, Priya breached r 25(2), r 22(3)(a), r 6(2) and r 8(2). She should stop
acting on the will and tell Mr Goh to see another firm about it. She should correct
Kelvin's impression that she acts for him, advise him to get independent advice, and
reveal nothing more about the will. Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or
misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under
s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

**(b) Advice to Priya in Part B (15 marks)**

*(i) Bail.* The issue is whether Priya may stand surety for Arun. She may not. A lawyer who
represents an accused person must not provide any surety or bail for them (r 14(3)(a) PCR).
The prohibition is absolute. It would give Priya a personal financial stake in Arun's
attendance, which could conflict with her duty to advise him, and it compromises her
independence (L4 p37). Her long friendship with Arun's family makes no difference. If
anything, it is a reason for her to be careful about her professional independence
(r 11(2)(a) PCR). Priya must withdraw the offer.

*(ii) Continuing after the confession.* The issue is whether Priya may keep acting now that
Arun has admitted taking the money. She may, within limits. Where an accused person
confesses to the lawyer, the lawyer may continue to represent them but must not adduce any
evidence or make any submission inconsistent with the confession (r 14(4)(a)–(b) PCR). The
confession is confidential (r 6(2) PCR), and no r 6(3) exception allows her to disclose it.
Priya may continue to put the prosecution to proof, for example by testing whether its
evidence establishes each element of cheating. She cannot suggest that Arun did not take
the money, by calling evidence or in submissions. (TODO: verify. No source states the "put
to proof" point in terms; it follows from the limit in r 14(4)(b) applying only to
*inconsistent* evidence or submissions.)

*(iii) Conduct of the trial and Arun's wish to testify.* The issue is what Priya must do if
Arun insists on giving evidence that he never took the money. She must advise him on whether
to give evidence or remain silent, and must abide by his decision (r 14(5)(a)(ii), (b)
PCR). She must also tell him that he has a duty to be truthful to the court (r 10(2)(a)
PCR). This creates a tension, because her duty to the client is subject to her paramount
duty to the court (r 4(a) PCR). She must, so far as she is able, prevent Arun from giving
false evidence and must not assist him in doing so (r 10(3)(b) PCR). She must not knowingly
mislead the court (r 9(2)(a) PCR). Solicitors must never mislead the court, actively or
passively (*Public Trustee v By Products Traders Pte Ltd* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 449 at [26]–[36]).
Where she knows the client is about to give false evidence, she must either cease to act or
continue in a way that does not perpetuate the falsehood (r 10(4) PCR). Priya should
first warn Arun that she cannot call him to say he never took the money. If he decides to
testify, his evidence will be his decision (r 14(5)(b)), but she cannot lead it or rely on
it. Because his whole defence would be the false denial, she cannot carry on without
perpetuating the falsehood, so she must cease to act (r 10(4)(a)).

Withdrawal is justified because it is necessary to avoid a breach of the Rules (r 26(5)(f)
PCR). Priya must take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to Arun. That means giving
reasonable notice, giving him time to instruct new counsel, and cooperating with them
(r 26(6)(a) PCR). She must apply to the court to be discharged. When doing so she may say
only that she has professional reasons, and must not reveal the confession (r 6(2) PCR).

In conclusion, Priya must not stand bail. She may continue to defend Arun after his
confession, but only by testing the prosecution's case. If Arun insists on testifying to a
false denial, she must cease to act without disclosing why. Such a breach may amount to
improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause
for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

## 2021 paper — Vega Bank, Gary's Mareva, and the engagement letter

Question: PYP21 p2–5 (Q1 = PS1 Q1, Q3 = PS3 Q1). Marks: Q1 40 (12 + 18 + 10), Q2 30,
Q3 30 (5 × 6). No official answer exists. Pre-writes used: 0-1, 0-5, 0-6, 0-7, 0-8, 0-10,
0-11, 0-13, 0-14, 0-3, 0-17, 0-18, 0-19, 0-21, 0-4, 0-32, 0-33, 0-15, 0-2, 0-31.

### Q1.1 Liability for the loan two years ago (12 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). Neither Andy nor Beth is likely to have breached any rule in
relation to the loan.

**Beth: competence and diligence.** The first issue is whether Beth should have discovered
that Delta Tech belonged to Xavier. She was not required to. A lawyer must act with
reasonable diligence and competence (r 5(2)(c) PCR). Compliance is judged in light of the
circumstances, nature and terms of the engagement (r 5(3) PCR). The test is what a
reasonably competent solicitor would have done; only gross incompetence or indifference
amounts to misconduct (*Law Society of Singapore v K Jayakumar Naidu* [2012] 4 SLR 1232
at [85]–[91]). Vega Bank expressly instructed Beth to prepare the loan documents only and
not to carry out due diligence, to save fees. Xavier's 60% interest was held indirectly
through several holding companies and intermediaries, so it would have taken real
investigation to uncover. Beth also ran a firm-wide conflict check naming the client and
the counterparty and received no responses. A reasonably competent solicitor working
within that limited scope would not have done more. Beth did not breach r 5(2)(c).

**Was there a conflict at the time?** The second issue is whether the firm was acting for
two parties with diverging interests (r 20 PCR), against a former client (r 21 PCR), or
against its own interests (r 22 PCR). It was not. Rule 20 applies only where the firm acts
or intends to act for two or more parties to the same matter (r 20(2) PCR). Delta Tech had
its own lawyers, and Xavier was not a party to the loan and did not instruct the firm on
it, so the firm acted for only one party. Rule 21 does not arise because Xavier was a
current client and no former client's confidential information was in play. Andy did not
know of the loan or Delta Tech's link to Xavier. Xavier gave him information only on a
need-to-know basis, so there is nothing to suggest that any confidential information of
Xavier's reached Beth or was used for Vega Bank (r 6(2) PCR). At most, the firm had a
commercial conflict, meaning a business preference not to act against a valued client's
group. That is not a PCR conflict, which needs two clients in the same matter (INTRO2 p26).

In conclusion, neither Andy nor Beth is liable for the loan transaction. The episode shows
the limits of a conflict check that names only the immediate parties, which is a point of
good practice rather than a breach.

### Q1.2 Andy's and Beth's proposals (18 marks)

The issue is how the firm should deal with Vega Bank's new enquiry while acting for Xavier.
Andy's proposal to decline Vega Bank is acceptable if it is done properly, and the firm
cannot give Vega Bank the real reason. Beth's proposal to act on both sides is not
workable.

**Is Vega Bank a current client?** The firm first needs to know whether declining is a
refusal of new instructions or a withdrawal from a continuing retainer. A retainer may be
implied where, viewed objectively, an intention to form a solicitor-client relationship
ought fairly to be imputed to both parties (*Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin
Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64]–[73]). The loan retainer ended two years ago. In
the five-minute call, Beth only pointed the in-house counsel to existing terms of the loan
agreement and suggested a follow-up call. That leans against a fresh retainer, although the
suggestion of a further call with a litigator may have led Vega Bank to think the firm was
taking the matter on. Either way, the firm should act promptly. If a retainer exists, it
must give reasonable notice and avoid foreseeable harm when ending it (r 26(5)(a), (6)
PCR). If not, it should tell Vega Bank at once that it cannot act, so the bank can instruct
others.

**Andy's approach.** A firm is free to decline new instructions for commercial reasons,
including to preserve a long-standing client relationship. That is a commercial choice,
not a PCR conflict. There is also a real PCR reason for the firm not to act for Vega Bank.
Andy holds Xavier's confidential information about his asset-holding structures, which
were set up partly to protect his assets from claims. If Vega Bank seeks to enforce against
Delta Tech and its controllers, that information would be directly material. Xavier is a
current client whose interests are adverse to Vega Bank's (r 20(1) PCR; r 6(2) PCR). In
that respect, Andy's preference coincides with what the rules point to. One caution
applies: if Andy later advises Xavier on the loan, as he does in Q1.3, Vega Bank becomes a
former client. The firm then holds Vega Bank's confidential information, namely the loan
file and its recent enforcement enquiry, and that information is material and adverse to
Xavier. Rule 21(2) PCR would then require the firm to decline unless Vega Bank gives
informed written consent after being advised to obtain independent advice (r 21(3) PCR).
Failing that, there must be adequate safeguards and reasonable efforts to notify Vega Bank
(r 21(4) PCR).

**Beth's need to give a reason.** A lawyer must not knowingly disclose information
confidential to a client and acquired in the engagement, unless an exception applies, such
as the client's authority (r 6(2)–(3) PCR). The fact that Andy acts for Xavier, and
anything about Xavier's interest in Delta Tech, is confidential to Xavier. The ACRA
results are public, but linking them to the firm's client is not. Beth may only tell Vega
Bank that the firm is unable to act. She must not explain why without Xavier's consent.

**Beth's alternative: Andy for Xavier, Beth and Clara for Vega Bank.** The firm would then
act for two parties in the same matter with directly opposed interests. Vega Bank wants to
enforce against Delta Tech, and Xavier, who controls it, wants to protect his businesses
and assets. Before acting for both, the firm would have to explain the divergence to each,
advise each to take independent advice (keeping a written record if either declines), and
obtain each one's informed written consent (r 20(2)–(3) PCR). That consent must be genuinely
informed (*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36 at [63]–[80];
*Kuek Siang Wei v Kuek Siew Chew* [2015] 5 SLR 357). It must cease to act if it cannot advise each party competently, evenly
and consistently (r 20(4), (6) PCR). The standard owed to each of several clients is at
least that owed to a single client, and one cannot be preferred (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong
Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). A firm that fails to manage the
conflict risks losing all its clients (*Law Society of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram*
[2009] 4 SLR(R) 674 at [34]–[53]). Here the firm could not even make the r 20(3)
disclosures, because explaining the conflict would reveal each client's confidential
position to the other (r 6(2)). An information barrier would not cure the problem. Its
adequacy depends on the nature of the work, when the barrier was set up, the size of the
firm, physical separation, and the number and seniority of tainted lawyers (*Harsha
Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at [68]). Andy is a partner
who already holds exactly the asset-protection information Vega Bank would want, and Clara
has already researched the corporate chain. Any barrier would be set up late, around people
who are already tainted. This is a lender pursuing a borrower controlled by the firm's other
client, so the firm could not act evenly for both.

In conclusion, the firm should decline Vega Bank's instructions promptly, without giving
the reason, and should not adopt Beth's split. If Andy then advises Xavier on the loan, the
firm must comply with r 21(3) or (4) to protect Vega Bank's information. That means a
documented barrier around Beth and Clara, and notifying Vega Bank.

### Q1.3 The deed signed by Xavier (10 marks)

The issue is whether Andy acted properly in obtaining Xavier's signature to a deed waiving
all claims against the firm. He did not. He breached r 22 and r 5(2)(a)–(b) PCR, and his
dishonesty makes this grossly improper conduct.

**Own interest.** A lawyer must not act where there is, or may reasonably be expected to
be, a conflict between the duty to serve the client's best interests and the lawyer's own
interests (r 22(2) PCR). Where the lawyer or the firm has an interest adverse to the
client's, the lawyer must withdraw unless they make full and frank disclosure, advise the
client to obtain independent legal advice, ensure the client does not think the lawyer is
protecting the client's interests, and obtain informed written consent (r 22(3)(a) PCR).
In *Law Society of Singapore v Govindan Balan Nair* [2020] SGHC 174, a lawyer who hid his
own negligence and kept acting preferred his own interests to the client's. A deed
releasing all claims against the firm benefits only Andy and the firm. It takes away rights
Xavier might have and is plainly adverse to him. Andy disclosed nothing, advised no
independent advice and obtained no informed consent. He also dealt through Xavier's
personal assistant, so Xavier signed without any explanation at all.

**Honesty.** A lawyer must be honest in all dealings with the client (r 5(2)(a) PCR) and
must tell the client everything that may reasonably affect the client's interests
(r 5(2)(b) PCR). Andy gave a false reason, "required routine reporting" to the firm's
insurers, which he knew did not exist, to get the deed signed. Dishonesty that violates the
trust inherent in the solicitor-client relationship will almost invariably lead to striking
off (*Law Society of Singapore v Udeh Kumar s/o Sethuraju* [2017] SGHC 141 at [25]–[30];
*Law Society of Singapore v Chia Choon Yang* [2018] SGHC 174 at [44]–[55]). Andy's motive,
avoiding Xavier's displeasure about Vega Bank's approach, is no excuse. Vega Bank's
approach is in any case confidential to Vega Bank (r 6(2) PCR), so the proper course was to
say nothing about it, not to extract a release. The deed is also unlikely to be effective.
It was procured by misrepresentation, and a law practice may not exclude its liability to a
client altogether or limit liability for fraud (PD 7.1.2).

In conclusion, Andy breached r 22(2)–(3)(a) and r 5(2)(a)–(b). He should tell Xavier the
truth and treat the deed as void. Because of the deceit and self-interest, this is likely
grossly improper conduct under s 83(2)(b) of the Legal Profession Act 1966, with a real
risk of striking off.

### Q2 Fiona and the ex parte worldwide Mareva (30 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court (r 4(a) PCR). Every advocate and solicitor is an officer of the Supreme Court
(s 82(1) Legal Profession Act 1966). Fiona breached that duty at every stage: in taking
on and running the application, in the affidavit, and in her answer to the judge.

**Failing to advise Gary.** The first issue is whether Fiona should have told Gary her
views on disclosure and on the weakness of his case. She should have. A lawyer must tell
the client all information that may reasonably affect the client's interests (r 5(2)(b)
PCR), must advise on the relevant legal issues so the client can make an informed decision
(r 17(2)(f) PCR), and must tell a client in proceedings of the client's duties to the
court, including the duty to be truthful (r 10(2) PCR). A lawyer who fails to explain
material risks breaches this duty (*Su Ah Tee v Allister Lim and Thrumurgam* [2014] SGHC
159). Fiona believed that Singapore law required disclosure of the UK and HK proceedings,
and she saw no evidence of a real risk of dissipation. She kept both points to herself
because Gary "would not like to hear this". Fear of displeasing a client never displaces the
duty to advise. Fiona breached r 5(2)(b), r 17(2)(f) and r 10(2).

**Bringing an unmeritorious application for a collateral purpose.** The second issue is
whether Fiona should have filed the application at all. She should not have. A lawyer must
assist in the administration of justice (r 9(1)(a) PCR). A lawyer must not let a personal
opinion affect their professional assessment of the facts or law or their duty to the
court (r 9(2)(e) PCR). A lawyer must not draft an affidavit containing a contention they do
not consider reasonably and properly arguable (r 9(2)(h)(ii) PCR). A lawyer must conduct
the case in a way that maintains the fairness and integrity of the proceedings (r 9(1)(e)
PCR), and must not engage in improper conduct whether or not it promotes the client's cause
(r 10(1)(c) PCR). Solicitors
owe an absolute and overriding duty to the court (*Public Trustee v By Products Traders Pte
Ltd* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 449 at [26]–[30]). They must assess the merits before starting
proceedings (*Zhou Tong v Public Prosecutor* [2010] 4 SLR 534 at [19]–[21]). They are not
mere mouthpieces of their clients (*BOI v BOJ* [2018] SGCA 61). Filing a patently
unmeritorious application may itself be misconduct (*Miya Manik v Public Prosecutor*
[2021] SGCA 90 at [82]). Gary said his sole objective was to pressure ICPL into selling, and
that he did not care if the application failed. That is a collateral purpose. Fiona saw no
evidence of dissipation, which is an essential element of a Mareva injunction, yet she
filed an affidavit asserting Gary's belief that ICPL was disposing of its assets worldwide.
Her admiration for Gary as a philanthropist "who could do no wrong" is exactly the personal
opinion r 9(2)(e) forbids. Using the court's process as leverage rather than to obtain
relief is not conduct that maintains the integrity of the proceedings. Fiona breached
r 9(2)(e) and r 9(2)(h)(ii), contrary to the principles in r 9(1)(e) and r 10(1)(c).

**Non-disclosure on a without-notice application.** The third issue is whether Fiona
breached the duty of full and frank disclosure. She did. A lawyer must disclose to the court
every fact the law requires to be disclosed (r 9(3)(b) PCR). So far as able, the lawyer
must prevent the client from suppressing evidence and must not assist in it (r 10(3)(a)
PCR). An applicant without notice must disclose all material facts. An affidavit that
discloses only part of a material matter breaches the duty of candour (*Law Society of
Singapore v de Souza Christopher James* [2023] SGHC 318 at [160]–[168], [207]). Identical
proceedings in the UK and HK, and failed freezing applications there, are plainly material
to whether a worldwide freezing order should be granted in Singapore. Fiona omitted them on
Gary's instruction, despite believing disclosure was required. A client cannot instruct a
lawyer to breach the duty of disclosure. Gary's reliance on foreign lawyers' views about
foreign law is irrelevant to a Singapore court's disclosure rules. Fiona should have
advised disclosure and, if Gary refused, declined to file. She breached r 9(3)(b) and
r 10(3).

**The answer to the judge.** The fourth issue is whether Fiona misled the court. She did. A
lawyer must not knowingly mislead or attempt to mislead the court (r 9(2)(a) PCR). Solicitors
must never mislead the court, actively or passively (*Public Trustee* at [26]–[36]). The
mental element is satisfied if the statement is made knowing it is false, without belief in
its truth, or recklessly (*Law Society of Singapore v Seah Zhen Wei Paul* [2024] SGHC 224
at [69]). A statement that implies something the lawyer knows to be untrue is dishonest
(*Loh Der Ming Andrew v Koh Tien Hua* [2022] SGHC 84 at [64]–[66]). The judge asked directly
why no remedies had been sought in the UK or HK. Fiona answered that Singapore was more
appropriate. That implied no such attempts had been made, when she knew they had been made
and had failed. A direct question from the court makes the half-truth more serious, not
less.

**What Fiona must do now.** Judgment is reserved, so the court can still be put right.
Fiona must advise Gary that the UK and HK proceedings, and the failed applications, must be
disclosed to the court and ICPL. If Gary agrees, she must disclose them and correct her
answer before judgment. If he refuses, she cannot continue without perpetuating the
falsehood. She must cease to act (r 10(4) PCR, applied by analogy; r 26(5)(f) PCR) and must
not reveal Gary's confidences beyond what the law requires (r 6 PCR). In conclusion,
Fiona breached r 5(2)(b), r 9(2)(a), (e) and (h), r 9(3)(b), r 10(2) and (3), and her
duty as an officer of the court. Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct
unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b)
and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966. The deliberate misleading of the court is likely
to be treated as grossly improper conduct.

### Q3 Terms of engagement (5 × 6 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). Each of the five terms is objectionable.

**3.1 The $4 million agreed fee.** The issue is whether a $4 million fee to defend a
$6 million goods claim, payable even on early settlement, is overcharging. It likely is. A
lawyer must not charge a fee that is overcharging, even if a fee agreement permits it
(r 17(7) PCR). Overcharging means an amount that a reasonable lawyer could not in good
faith charge, having regard to the lawyer's standing, the nature of the work, the time
required, the client's instructions and the other circumstances (r 17(8) PCR). A written
agreement for a gross sum in contentious business is permitted (s 111 Legal Profession Act
1966). However, it can be declared void if unfair or unreasonable (s 113(4) LPA), and a
"non-refundable" fee is not kept regardless of work done (PD 5.2.2). The extent and
disproportion of an overcharge go to its gravity (*Law Society of Singapore v Syn Kok Kay*
[2023] SGHC 7). The fee is two-thirds of the sum claimed, for what appears to be a
straightforward claim for the price of goods. It is payable in full even if the suit
settles early, when little work has been done. Unless exceptional complexity or seniority
justifies it, no reasonable lawyer could charge this in good faith. The term risks breaching
r 17(7). It should be tied to the work done, with staged fees.

**3.2 The $500,000 bonus.** The issue is whether the extra fee if the suit settles below
$500,000 is a prohibited contingency fee. It is. A solicitor must not enter into an agreement
for contentious proceedings that stipulates for or contemplates payment only in the event of
success (s 107(1)(b) LPA). A lawyer must also not negotiate for an interest in the subject
matter, or for remuneration proportionate to what the client recovers (r 18 PCR). The
prohibition keeps the lawyer detached enough to discharge their duties to the court and the
client (*Law Society of Singapore v Kurubalan s/o Manickam Rengaraju* [2013] 4 SLR 91 at
[42], [45]). The additional $500,000 is payable only if counsel achieves a particular result,
a settlement below $500,000. That part of the fee is therefore payable only in the event of
success, and the fixed base fee does not change that. The only lawful way to agree an extra
fee on success is a conditional fee agreement with an "uplift fee" under Part 8A of the Act
(s 107(3AA), s 115A(1) LPA). The Law Society's guidance treats exactly this structure, a base
fee plus an uplift on success, as a conditional fee agreement (GN 5.6.1). But such agreements
are allowed only in prescribed proceedings: arbitration, certain SICC proceedings and related
court and mediation proceedings (GN 5.6.1 para 8). ABC's suit is an ordinary court action, so
the exception does not apply, and in any case the regime did not exist when the 2021 paper was
set. Rule 18(b) is a less natural fit, because the client here is a defendant paying out, not
recovering; s 107(1)(b) does the work. The term breaches s 107(1)(b) and should be removed.

**3.3 Termination "for any reason" at any stage.** The issue is whether counsel may reserve
an unrestricted right to terminate with immediate effect. He may not. A lawyer must complete
the agreed work (r 26(2) PCR) and may withdraw only on the grounds in r 26(5) PCR. These
include giving reasonable notice, non-payment, or where withdrawal is needed to avoid
breaching the Rules. On withdrawal, the lawyer must take reasonable care to avoid
foreseeable harm, including giving reasonable notice and time to instruct new counsel
(r 26(6) PCR). A clause allowing termination "with immediate effect for any reason and at
any stage" would permit withdrawal on the eve of trial. That contradicts r 26(5)(a) and
r 26(6). PD 7.4.2 recommends instead reserving the right to discharge on the r 26(5)
grounds. A lawyer whose withdrawal causes a client to lose a valid defence risks a claim in
damages. The clause should be limited to r 26(5) grounds with reasonable notice.

**3.4 The $50,000 liability cap.** The issue is whether counsel may exclude or cap liability
as drafted. He may not. A law practice may limit, but not exclude, civil liability to its
clients. It may not limit liability below the minimum professional indemnity cover, and may
not limit liability for fraud or reckless disregard of professional obligations at all
(PD 7.1.2). In an agreement as to costs for contentious business, a term that the solicitor
is not liable for negligence, or is relieved of professional responsibility, is wholly void
(s 112(5) LPA). This is an engagement letter for a suit, so s 112(5) applies. The cap
purports to cover fraud and breach of professional obligations, and $50,000 is far below
the minimum indemnity cover (PD 7.1.2). The clause fails on every ground and is
unenforceable. Offering it breaches PD 7.1.2.

**3.5 Discount for shares and a favourable review.** The issue is whether a 5% discount for
promoting the firm online is permissible. It is not. A lawyer must not tout or do anything
likely to lead to a reasonable inference of touting (r 39(1) PCR). It is due cause for
discipline to give, or allow the retention of, any gratification out of fees for procuring
legal business (s 83(2)(d) LPA). Publicity must not be misleading, which includes omitting a
material fact or containing information that cannot be verified (r 44(1)(b), (2)(b)–(c)
PCR). A fee discount is consideration out of the lawyer's fees, given in return for
promoting the firm to obtain further work. A "favourable" review bought with a discount is
also misleading publicity, because readers are not told it was paid for. The term breaches
r 39 and r 44 and s 83(2)(d), and should be removed.

Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and
solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal
Profession Act 1966.

## 2022 Section B — Luna, Marci and the Settlement Agreement

Question: PYP22 p2–3 (= PS2 Q2). Marks: (a) 12, (b) 14, (c) 14. Section A was 20 MCQs set
on Examplify. Their text isn't in the PDF, so there is nothing to answer, and the 2026
paper has no MCQ section anyway. No official answer exists. Pre-writes used: 0-1, 0-9,
0-25, 0-22, 0-13, 0-23, 0-12, 0-24, 0-16, 0-4.

### (a) Can Luna and Marci act for Davion in the New Suit? (12 marks)

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). Neither Luna nor Marci can act, because both will be material
witnesses on contested issues.

**Was Ricky also a client?** It is worth ruling out the absolute bar first. If Mid Law had
prepared the Settlement Agreement on behalf of both Davion and Ricky, it could not act for
either in a dispute arising from it (r 20(8) PCR). A retainer can be implied where an
intention to form one ought fairly to be imputed to both parties (*Law Society of Singapore
v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64]–[73]). Luna told Ricky she
represented Davion, which both sides accept. The agreement recites that Ricky had the
opportunity to take independent advice. So Mid Law probably acted for Davion alone, and
r 20(8) does not apply. However, Ricky's pleaded Representations, that he did not need a
lawyer and that Luna could answer his questions, would, if proved, be legal advice to an
unrepresented person with adverse interests. That is a breach of r 8(2)(a)–(b) PCR (*Law
Society of Singapore v Ganesan Krishnan* [2003] 2 SLR(R) 251). A recital about independent
advice does not cure oral assurances.

**Lawyer as witness.** The issue is whether Luna and Marci will be required to give
evidence material to a contested issue. They will, so they must not act. Where it is known
or appears that a legal practitioner will be required to give evidence material to the
determination of a contested issue, the practitioner must not accept instructions from any
party to the case. If already acting in proceedings, the practitioner must apply to be
discharged (r 11(3)(a) PCR). A practitioner should not act where they have reason to
believe they will be a witness on a material and disputed question of fact (PD 1.7.1). The
witness's duty to tell the whole truth and the advocate's duty to advance the client's case
should not be combined in one person (r 11(1) PCR; *Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001]
SGCA 64 at [61]–[63]). Whether Luna made the Representations is the central contested
issue: Ricky pleads them and Davion denies them. Only four people were at the meeting.
Davion's case relies on Marci's typed attendance notes, and Marci signed the agreement as a
witness. Luna and Marci are the people best placed to give evidence about what was said,
and Ricky has said he will call them. This is not a bare tactical assertion by Carl,
because the issue is on the pleadings. Luna also has a personal interest in being cleared
of a r 8(2) breach, which may not match Davion's interests (r 22(2), (3)(a) PCR).

In conclusion, Luna and Marci must not act and, having filed the New Suit, must apply to be
discharged. They must withdraw in a way that avoids foreseeable harm to Davion, by giving
reasonable notice, time to instruct others, and cooperation with them (r 26(5)(f), (6)
PCR).

### (b) Can Mid Law continue through Chen? (14 marks)

The issue is whether Mid Law may continue to act through another litigation partner, Chen,
with Luna and Marci stepping aside. Rule 11(3)(b) PCR permits it in principle, but on these
facts the risk is high and Mid Law should, on balance, not continue.

Even where a lawyer will be a material witness, the law practice or a member of it may
represent the party "unless doing so would prejudice the administration of justice"
(r 11(3)(b) PCR). Separately, a law practice with an interest adverse to the client's must
withdraw unless it makes full and frank disclosure, advises independent legal advice,
ensures the client does not think the firm is protecting the client's interests, and
obtains informed written consent (r 22(2), (4)(a) PCR). In *Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim*
[2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], neither the partner who would be a witness nor the litigation
partner of the same firm should have acted, because the firm's handling of the transaction
was itself in issue. The court may also restrain a firm where a fair-minded, reasonably
informed member of the public would conclude that the proper administration of justice
requires it (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29).

In Chen's favour, he was not at the meeting, will not be a witness, and is a partner in his
own right rather than a junior. Rule 11(3)(b) also expressly contemplates a colleague taking
over, and Davion is entitled to his choice of counsel (r 26(6)(b) PCR). Against that, the
Defence attacks Mid Law's own conduct: the agreement was "drafted by the Plaintiff's
solicitors, Mid Law LLP", and its managing partner allegedly made the Representations. Chen
would have to lead evidence from his own managing partner and associate, and cross-examine
Ricky on the integrity of his own firm's conduct. If Ricky succeeds, Mid Law faces
disciplinary exposure and possibly a claim by Davion. That gives the firm an interest in
vindicating itself, which may diverge from Davion's interest in, for example, a pragmatic
settlement (r 22(4)(a)). A fair-minded observer would be uneasy about the firm whose
managing partner's honesty is in issue running the case, which invites a restraint
application by Carl.

In conclusion, Mid Law may continue through Chen only after full and frank disclosure of its
interest, advice to Davion to take independent advice, and his informed written consent
(r 22(4)(a)). Even then, Chen must be satisfied that continuing would not prejudice the
administration of justice. Given *Ho Kon Kim* and the attack on the firm's own conduct, the
prudent advice is that Davion should instruct another firm.

### (c) The original Settlement Agreement and Top Law's lien (14 marks)

The issue is whether Luna breached her obligations by sending the original document to Dire
Law. She did. She breached her undertaking to Top Law (r 7(6) PCR), and she failed to tell
Davion of the arrangement (r 5(2)(b) PCR).

A legal practitioner must honour every undertaking given to another legal practitioner.
They must not give an undertaking unless they believe it is necessary and know, when
giving it, that they can honour it (r 7(6)–(7) PCR). An undertaking is a solemn promise on
which the other lawyer relies by parting with something of value, and breach is treated
seriously (*Law Society of Singapore v Naidu Priyalatha* [2022] SGHC 224). A lawyer
claiming a lien must release documents to the client's new lawyer only if the new lawyer
undertakes to hold them subject to the lien and gives reasonable security, or offers to pay
the costs on completion and the offer is accepted (r 26(7)(a) PCR). A lawyer must keep the
client informed of everything that may reasonably affect the client's interests (r 5(2)(b)
PCR).

Top Law released the original only because Luna undertook to protect its lien for unpaid
costs. When Davion moved to Dire Law, Luna sent the original on without obtaining Dire
Law's matching undertaking or any security, and without telling Top Law. The client's
instruction does not release an undertaking already given to another lawyer. Luna also never
told Davion that the document was held subject to Top Law's lien, so he could not make an
informed decision about paying Top Law or arranging security.

What Luna should have done: when she gave the undertaking, she should have explained it to
Davion and confirmed that it was necessary and that she could honour it (r 7(7)). When
Davion moved to Dire Law, she should have released the original only against Dire Law's
written undertaking to hold it subject to Top Law's lien, or against security for Top Law's
costs (r 26(7)(a)). Failing either, she should have kept it and told Davion why. Now she
should immediately ask Dire Law either to return the original or to give Top Law the
undertaking, inform Top Law of the steps taken, and explain the position to Davion.

In conclusion, Luna breached r 7(6) and r 5(2)(b). Such a breach may amount to improper
conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for
discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

SYL p6. Cross-checked against Cyril's and Matt's PS2 notes.*

## PS1 Q2 — Seldon, Howwie and Emily

Question: PS1 (`Modules/…/3. Practice Sessions - Question Papers/ETH_Practice_Session_1_…
.pdf`), Q2. Not mark-weighted. Pre-writes used: 0-9, 0-11, 0-10, 0-12, 0-13, 0-6, 0-22.

A legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the paramount duty to the
court, and must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the standing and integrity of the legal
profession (r 4(a)–(b) PCR). In my view CT Law may continue to act for Lennard, but only
with real safeguards: a documented information barrier around Howwie, notice to Emily, and
Lennard's informed consent after full disclosure.

**Rule 20(8).** The first issue is whether the firm is barred because Howwie drafted the
shareholders' agreement. It is not. Rule 20(8) PCR bars a law practice that prepared a
document on behalf of two or more parties from acting in their dispute about it. Howwie
prepared only an initial draft, for Emily alone, at a different firm. He never met the
other shareholders, and the executed agreement is completely different. CT Law prepared
nothing. Ragesh's allegation that Howwie acted for "LRPL and all the shareholders" is
contradicted by the engagement terms and by Emily paying the invoice personally.

**Rule 21: Emily as Howwie's former client.** The second issue is whether CT Law must
decline because Howwie holds Emily's confidential information. Rule 21(2) requires a
lawyer or firm to decline if it holds a former client's confidential information, the
current client's interest is or may be adverse to the former client's, and the
information may reasonably be material (r 21(2) PCR). The duty survives the end of the
retainer (r 21(1) PCR). Emily's interest is adverse to Lennard's, because Ragesh, the
defendant, holds his shares as her nominee. Howwie learned Emily's requirements for the
agreement, which are confidential (r 6(2) PCR). Whether they are material is arguable.
The draft was preliminary, contained no names, was four years old, and differs completely
from the executed agreement. Out of prudence, CT Law should treat r 21(2) as engaged.
Emily's informed written consent (r 21(3)) is unrealistic given her threatened claim, so
CT Law should rely on r 21(4). That requires adequate safeguards to protect the
information and reasonable efforts to notify Emily of them and that the firm will act.
Adequacy turns on the nature of the earlier work, when the barrier was set up, the size of
the firm, the physical separation of the teams, and the number and seniority of tainted
lawyers (*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at [68]).
Here only one lawyer, Howwie, is tainted. He is new, is not on Lennard's matter, and the
work was limited and long ago. A barrier set up now, with written undertakings from Howwie
and the team, separate files and IT access, and monitoring, is likely to be adequate. That
is what the "usual safeguards" must mean in substance.

**The court's power to restrain.** Even if the PCR is satisfied, a former client can ask
the court to restrain a firm if it holds confidential information the former client has
not consented to disclose, the information is or may be relevant to a matter in which the
firm's client is adverse, and there is a real risk of disclosure (*Harsha*). The court
also asks whether a fair-minded, reasonably informed observer would think the
administration of justice requires restraint (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP*
[2022] SGCA 29). An early, well-documented barrier is CT Law's answer to any such
application.

**Rule 22: Howwie's threatened negligence claim.** The third issue is whether CT Law has
an interest that conflicts with Lennard's. Emily's letter says that if Lennard wins,
Howwie was negligent. A firm must not act where its own interests conflict with the
client's (r 22(2) PCR). Where the firm's interest is adverse, it must make full and frank
disclosure, advise independent advice, ensure the client does not think the firm is
protecting the client's interests, and obtain informed written consent (r 22(4)(a) PCR).
Otherwise it needs disclosure and written consent (r 22(4)(b) PCR). The conflict rules
protect the client, so it is Lennard's position that matters, not Emily's tactical
objection (*Then Khek Khoon v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2012] 2 SLR 451 at [30]–[36]).
The threatened liability attaches to Howwie's former firm, not CT Law, and Seldon has every
incentive to win. A colleague's exposure might, however, be thought to temper the firm's
zeal. CT Law should therefore disclose the threat to Lennard and get his written consent,
and keep Howwie entirely away from the matter. If Howwie may become a witness about the
draft, Seldon can still act provided this does not prejudice the administration of
justice (r 11(3)(b) PCR).

**Seldon's friendship with Lennard.** A lawyer must not appear in court where a personal
relationship with the client makes it difficult to maintain professional independence
(r 11(2)(a) PCR). Seldon should check that his close friendship does not affect his
objective advice, for example on settlement.

In conclusion, CT Law may continue to act for Lennard if it puts a documented information
barrier around Howwie at once, notifies Emily under r 21(4)(b), and makes full disclosure
to Lennard and obtains his written consent under r 22(4). Seldon must keep his professional
independence despite the friendship. Howwie must not disclose anything about Emily's
instructions (r 6(2) PCR).

## PS2 Q1 — Zapp and Barbara's undertaking

Question: PS2, Q1. Not mark-weighted. Pre-writes used: 0-21, 0-5, 0-17, 0-18, 0-20, 0-26,
0-4.

Every advocate and solicitor is an officer of the Supreme Court (s 82(1) Legal Profession
Act 1966). The duty to the client is subject to the paramount duty to the court (r 4(a)
PCR). Zapp breached that duty repeatedly.

**Advice to get round the undertaking.** The first issue is whether Zapp could advise
Barbara on the transfer. He could not. A lawyer must not give advice they know or have
reasonable grounds to believe is sought for a dishonest, fraudulent or unlawful purpose
(r 10(5) PCR). A lawyer must not knowingly assist the client to mislead the court or to do
anything dishonest (r 10(6) PCR). So far as able, the lawyer must prevent the client from
suppressing evidence (r 10(3)(a) PCR). Barbara had undertaken to the court not to dispose
of her assets anywhere. Causing her Momcorp shares to be replaced by shares issued to her
lover in the Caribbean would breach that undertaking and put assets beyond Hermes' reach.
Zapp recognised the problem and devised a "solution". A lawyer who joins a client's
improper scheme may be struck off (*Narindar Singh s/o Malagar Singh v Public Prosecutor*
[1996] 3 SLR(R) 318 at [48]–[53]). Zapp should have told Barbara that the transaction would
breach her undertaking and that she must seek the court's permission first. If she
persisted, he should have ceased to act.

**The pre-signed affidavit.** The second issue is the affidavit to be kept in the safe and
filed later. Zapp drafted an affidavit giving an LSE-rules reason for non-disclosure while
admitting he had "no idea" what those rules say. A lawyer must not fabricate or concoct
evidence or contrive facts (r 9(2)(b), (g) PCR). An affidavit must contain only what the
witness would say if giving the evidence orally (r 9(2)(h)(iv) PCR). A lawyer must have the
knowledge each matter requires and act competently (r 5(2)(c)–(d) PCR; *Law Society of
Singapore v K Jayakumar Naidu* [2012] 4 SLR 1232 at [85]–[91]). The affidavit was designed
to manufacture a record of "good faith" for later use. Its LSE explanation had no basis
Zapp had checked. He told Barbara it was fine as long as she "genuinely believes" it, and
left her to read foreign rules online. This was contrived evidence and incompetent advice.

**Attestation.** The third issue is how the affidavit was sworn. A signatory must
personally appear before the person attesting the signature, and false attestation may be
grossly improper conduct (PD 1.6.1). In *Law Society of Singapore v Thirumurthy Ayernaar
Pambayan* [2022] SGHC 79, false attestation led to a nine-month suspension. Barbara signed
and left before the Commissioner arrived. On Zapp's assurance, the Commissioner then
stamped the affidavit in front of a secretary. The jurat is false, Zapp procured it, and
he must supervise his staff (r 32 PCR). The affidavit cannot be used and would be false
evidence if filed (r 9(1)(d) PCR).

**Misleading the court.** The fourth issue is Zapp's statement at the hearing that his
"instructions are that Barbara certainly has not disposed of any assets". A lawyer must not
knowingly mislead the court (r 9(2)(a) PCR), actively or passively (*Public Trustee v By
Products Traders Pte Ltd* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 449 at [26]–[36]). A statement that implies
something the lawyer knows to be untrue is dishonest (*Loh Der Ming Andrew v Koh Tien Hua*
[2022] SGHC 84 at [64]–[66]). Recklessness is enough (*Law Society of Singapore v Seah Zhen
Wei Paul* [2024] SGHC 224 at [69]). That morning, Zapp learned that the transfer would be
executed in three days. His statement was literally true as at that date, but it was made
to persuade the judge not to grant a freezing order. It gave the false impression that the
assets were safe, when he knew a disposal was imminent. The judge relied on it. Zapp breached
r 9(2)(a) and r 10(6)(a).

**What Zapp must do now.** Where a lawyer knows the client has given false information to
the court, as Barbara's instructions relayed by Zapp did, the lawyer must cease to act or
continue without perpetuating the falsehood (r 10(4) PCR). Where the lawyer has misled the
court, the lawyer must correct the record before it is acted on further. He must also tell the client of her duties to the
court (r 10(2) PCR). Zapp must advise Barbara that the transaction would breach her
undertaking and that the court must be told the true position. If she refuses, he must
cease to act, without disclosing her confidences beyond what the law requires (r 6 PCR).

In conclusion, Zapp breached r 5(2)(c)–(d), r 9(1)(d), r 9(2)(a), (b), (g) and (h), r 10(3),
(5) and (6), r 32 and PD 1.6.1. This is likely grossly improper conduct under s 83(2)(b)
of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

## PS3 Q2 — Donnie, Melany and Mega Law

A legal practitioner's duty to the client must be fulfilled in a way that upholds the
standing and integrity of the legal profession (r 4(b) PCR). Donnie has let an unauthorised
person practise under his name and shared fees with her, which is serious misconduct.

**Qualification to practise as a sole proprietor.** A solicitor may practise on his own
account only if he has completed the legal practice management course and has been
employed in a Singapore law practice for 3 continuous years, or 3 out of 5 years, since
admission (s 75C(1) Legal Profession Act 1966). Donnie has 6 years' standing and worked in

say whether he completed the course, and he should confirm that he has.

**Supervision.** A lawyer must exercise proper supervision over the staff working under
them (r 32 PCR). Paralegals may not practise law, and the lawyer remains responsible for
all professional work (GN 3.7.1). Allowing a conveyancing clerk to deal with clients
unsupervised led to a four-year suspension in *Law Society of Singapore v Yeo Siew Chye
Troy* [2019] SGHC 115. Melany deals with clients entirely alone and prepares every
document. Donnie is merely copied on emails or signs when asked. Being copied is not
supervision. Donnie breached r 32.

**Enabling an unauthorised person to practise.** A solicitor must not do anything that
enables an unauthorised person to practise, or permit his name to be used for their
profit (s 77(1) LPA). He must not "lend his name" by signing documents prepared by an
unauthorised person (PD 3.11.2). An unauthorised person who acts as a solicitor commits an
offence (s 33 LPA). Melany runs her own contacts' matters, signs as "Mega Law" without
saying she is a paralegal, and Donnie signs or lends his name on request. In substance she
practises under his name. Donnie contravened s 77 and PD 3.11.2.

**Fee sharing.** A lawyer must not share fees with, or pay commission to, an unauthorised
person for legal work (r 19(1)–(2) PCR). Sharing about half the fees with an unqualified
person who held himself out as a solicitor was serious misconduct in *Law Society of
Singapore v Tan See Leh Jonathan* [2020] SGHC 102. The 50/50 split is precisely that. The
fact that Melany does the work makes it worse, because it shows she is practising.

**Paying the referrer.** Where a client is referred, the lawyer must not reward the
referrer by any commission or other consideration (r 39(2)(b) PCR). It is due cause for
discipline to allow the retention, out of a fee, of any gratification for procuring legal
business (s 83(2)(d) LPA). Paying referral fees was serious misconduct in *Law Society of
Singapore v Chong Wai Yen Michael* [2012] 2 SLR 113. The 5% paid out of Melany's share is
still paid out of Mega Law's fees, so it breaches r 39(2)(b) and s 83(2)(d).

**The café.** A lawyer must not accept an executive appointment in a business that
detracts from the dignity of the profession, materially interferes with their practice, is
likely to attract legal business unfairly, or shares fees with unauthorised persons
(r 34(1) PCR). "Executive appointment" excludes non-executive directorships (r 34(9) PCR).
Donnie is a director but takes no part in operations. If his role is non-executive, r 34
is not engaged. If it is executive, a small café does not detract from the profession or
feed legal work to the firm, so it is permissible, subject to the Second Schedule
conditions (r 34(4) PCR).

**Criminal work.** Donnie's limited criminal experience matters only if he takes on
criminal trials. He must then ensure he has the necessary knowledge and skills (r 5(2)(d)
PCR).

In conclusion, Donnie breached r 32, r 19 and r 39(2)(b) PCR and contravened s 77 LPA and
PD 3.11.2. The café role is likely permissible. He must take over the client relationships,
review all work, make Melany's status clear, and end the fee split and referral payments.
Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and
solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal
Profession Act 1966.

*Source note: s 33, 75C, 77, 83(2)(d) LPA; r 19, 32, 34, 39 PCR; GN 3.7.1; PD 3.11.2; Yeo Siew
Chye Troy and Tan See Leh Jonathan SYL p10, L8 p18–19; Chong Wai Yen Michael SYL p11.*

## PS3 Q3 — Brenner Law's proposed advertisements

Question: PS3, Q3. Not mark-weighted. Pre-writes used: 0-29, 0-30.

A legal practitioner must ensure that publicity about their practice complies with the
Rules, including publicity produced by others on their behalf, such as an advertising
agency (r 42(1) PCR). Joyce is therefore responsible for what DPL proposes. Under r 43(1)
PCR, a claim to expertise must be justifiable (on factors such as experience and the
significance of matters handled, r 43(2)), the publicity must not mention the firm's
success rate, and it must not compare or criticise other lawyers' fees or quality.
Publicity must not diminish public confidence in the profession, and must not be
misleading, deceptive, inaccurate, false or unbefitting the dignity of the profession
(r 44(1) PCR). It is misleading if it contains a material misrepresentation, omits a
material fact, cannot be verified, or is likely to create an unjustified expectation about
results (r 44(2) PCR).

**Statement 1.** "Banks hate her!" and "one secret technique" are clickbait, unbefitting
the profession's dignity (r 44(1)(b)). There is no secret technique, so the claim is a
material misrepresentation and unverifiable (r 44(2)(a), (c)). "Recovered millions for
plaintiffs" invites an expectation of similar results (r 44(2)(d)). "Expert litigator" can
be justified by the managing partner's 25 years in disputes practice (r 43(2)(b)). A link
to a list of matters is fine if, as stated, no confidential information is disclosed
(r 43(1)(b)(i)). Only a factual line, such as "an experienced disputes lawyer of 25 years'
standing", with the link, can be used.

**Statement 2.** The quote, placed beneath a celebrity whom BL never acted for, implies a
client testimonial that does not exist. That is a material misrepresentation (r 44(2)(a)),
and the photo licence does not cure it. "When other lawyers had failed" criticises the
quality of other lawyers' services (r 43(1)(c)). "These lawyers will change your life"
creates an unjustified expectation (r 44(2)(d)). "Cheating spouse" is undignified
(r 44(1)(b)). None of Statement 2 can be used.

**Statement 3.** "99% success rate" is prohibited outright (r 43(1)(b)(ii)). A footnote in
small print cannot cure a prohibited statement, and it also contradicts the headline.
"Learned friends don't lie" implies something about other lawyers and is unverifiable.
"Why settle just because other people tell you you're supposed to?" discourages settlement.
That is inconsistent with a lawyer's duty to evaluate alternative dispute resolution with
the client (r 17(2)(e)(ii) PCR) and is undignified (r 44(1)(b)). "Cost-effective solutions"
is general and makes no comparison, so it is probably the only part that can be used.

In conclusion, BL may use only the factual description of the managing partner's
experience (with the link) and "cost-effective solutions". Everything else breaches r 43
or r 44. Joyce must tell DPL to withdraw it (r 42(2) PCR). Such a breach may amount to
improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause
for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966.

*Source note: r 17(2)(e), 42–44 PCR (PCR p31, p63–66).*
