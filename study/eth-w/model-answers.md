## 2023 Q1 — Archie, Betty, Cheryl and the RPL facility

Question: PYP23 p2–3 (= SQ p4–5). Marks: 1.1 18, 1.2 18, 1.3 14. The official answer key is
SQ p109–113. It is written in numbered points; this is the same content in exam prose, with a
few points the key does not make (marked *beyond the key*). Pre-writes used: 0-11, 0-10,
0-6, 0-7, 0-8, 0-12.

### 1.1 Can Archie act for L? (18 marks)

Rule 4(a) PCR states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to the court, which
takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client.
Rule 4(b) PCR states that a legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client is
subject only to the legal practitioner's duty to the court, and must at all times be
fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the Singapore legal system
and the legal profession in Singapore. Here, r 21 PCR applies. In my view Archie may act for
L only with RPL's informed written consent under r 21(3) PCR. Without it, adequate safeguards
under r 21(4) PCR would be very hard to achieve, because Archie himself holds RPL's
confidential information.

**Is RPL a former or a current client?** RPL has decided not to engage Archie for the bidding
process. On that basis RPL is a former client, and the governing rule is r 21 PCR, which is how
the examiners analyse it (SQ p109). *Beyond the key:* Archie has advised RPL "for many years".
If RPL still instructs him or Tate Law (TL) on other matters, it is a current client. Acting
for a bidder against it on the sale of its main asset would then also engage the firm's duty
of loyalty under r 20(1) PCR, which makes the case for declining stronger still.

**The r 21(2) test.** Rule 21(1) PCR states the guiding principle that the duties of
loyalty and confidentiality owed by a legal practitioner, and by a law practice, to a client
continue after the termination of the retainer. Rule 21(2) PCR states that, subject to
paragraphs (3), (4) and (5), a legal practitioner or law practice must decline to represent,
or must withdraw from representing, a client (called in the rule the current client) in a
matter, if (a) the legal practitioner or the law practice holds confidential information
relating to a former client (called in the rule the former client) that is protected by
rule 6; (b) the current client has an interest that is, or may reasonably be expected to be,
adverse to an interest of the former client; and (c) that information may reasonably be
expected to be material to the representation of the current client in that matter. Limb (a)
turns on r 6(2) PCR, which states that, subject to paragraph (3), a legal practitioner must
not knowingly disclose any information which (a) is confidential to his or her client; and
(b) is acquired by the legal practitioner (whether from the client or from any other person)
in the course of the legal practitioner's engagement.

All three limbs are met. On limb (a), Archie knows RPL's minimum sale price, and he assisted
RPL in earlier confidential negotiations with interested buyers. That information is
confidential in nature (*Law Society of Singapore v Ravi s/o Madasamy* [2015] 3 SLR 1187 at
[33]) and was acquired in the course of Archie's engagement by RPL, so it is protected by
r 6(2). On limb (b), L, as a bidder, wants to pay as little as possible, and RPL, as seller,
wants as much as possible, so their interests are directly adverse. On limb (c), in a
sealed-bid process the seller's minimum price is the most material information a bidder
could have. Archie must therefore decline unless an exception applies.

**The exceptions.** There are three exceptions. Rule 21(3) PCR states that paragraph (2)
does not prevent a legal practitioner or law practice from acting, or continuing to act, for
the current client, subject to any conditions agreed between the legal practitioner or law
practice and the former client, if (a) the legal practitioner or law practice has adequately
advised the former client to obtain independent legal advice; and (b) the former client
gives the former client's informed consent in writing to the legal practitioner or law
practice acting, or continuing to act, for the current client. Rule 21(4) PCR states that
where the requirements in paragraph (3)(a) and (b) are not met despite reasonable efforts by
the lawyer to meet them, paragraph (2) does not prevent the lawyer from acting, or
continuing to act, for the current client, if (a) there are adequate safeguards in place to
protect the former client's confidential information; and (b) the lawyer has made reasonable
efforts to notify the former client (i) of those safeguards; and (ii) that the lawyer will
act, or continue to act, for the current client. Rule 21(5) PCR applies only where it would
be illegal to meet the requirements in paragraphs (3)(a) and (4)(b), which is not suggested
here.

Applying r 21(3), Archie's proper first step is to advise RPL adequately to obtain
independent legal advice (limb (a)) and to seek RPL's informed consent in writing to TL
acting for L (limb (b)), possibly on agreed conditions. Given that RPL has engaged other
lawyers, it may well agree. Applying r 21(4), if consent is not forthcoming despite
reasonable efforts, TL may act only if it puts adequate safeguards in place at once to
protect RPL's information, in practice an information barrier (limb (a)), and makes
reasonable efforts to notify RPL of those safeguards and that TL will act for L (limb (b)).

Whether the steps taken to prevent disclosure are adequate depends on factors such as (1)
the nature of the work done for the former client, (2) the timing of the creation of the
information barrier, (3) the size of the law firm, (4) the physical locations of departments
within the firm, and (5) the number and seniority of tainted lawyers (*Harsha Rajkumar
Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at [68]).

The difficulty here is fundamental. A barrier works by keeping information away from the
lawyer who acts, but Archie is both the person who holds RPL's information and the person
who would act for L. No barrier can separate a lawyer from his own memory. Professor Pinsler's
commentary (optional reading, cited in the key) says safeguards must be "iron-tight" to be
effective. Applying the *Harsha* factors, the work Archie did for RPL went to the very
price L would bid against (factor (1)), and the "tainted lawyer" is the senior partner who
would act (factor (5)). On these facts, r 21(4)(a) is very unlikely to be satisfied with
Archie as the acting lawyer. *Beyond the key:* if consent is refused, a different TL partner
with no access to RPL's file might act behind a barrier set up immediately (factor (2)).
Even then, the firm must weigh the risk that RPL applies to restrain TL from acting. The
court's supervisory jurisdiction is invoked to restrain legal practitioners from acting for a
current client whose interests are adverse to the interests of a former client if a
fair-minded, reasonably informed member of the public would conclude that the proper
administration of justice requires that the legal practitioners be restrained from acting,
in the interests of the protection of the integrity of the judicial process and the due
administration of justice, including the appearance of justice. Factors such as a long
history between the legal practitioners and the former client are relevant in deciding
whether an injunction should be granted (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29 at [65], [72]–[73]). Archie has advised RPL "for many years", which is exactly that kind of history.

In conclusion, Archie may act for L only if r 21(3) PCR is met: RPL's informed written
consent, given after RPL has been adequately advised to take independent advice. Without that
consent he should decline, because r 21(2) would otherwise be breached. If TL nevertheless
acts under r 21(4), the barrier must be in place immediately and RPL must be notified.

### 1.2 Was it acceptable for Betty to act for M? (18 marks)

The issue is whether TL may act for M through Betty while acting for L through Archie in the
same bidding process. Here, r 20 PCR applies. TL may act for M only if it complies with
r 20(3) PCR before accepting M's instructions. Betty's decision to act after only "discussing
the matter with Archie" did not comply with r 20(3).

**Rule 20 applies.** Rule 20(2) PCR states that paragraphs (3), (4) and (7) apply where (a) a
legal practitioner or law practice intends to act for 2 or more different parties (each
called a relevant party) to a matter or transaction; and (b) a diversity of interests exists,
or may reasonably be expected to exist, between those parties. Limb (a) is met: TL is one law
practice, so Archie's client L and Betty's prospective client M would both be TL's clients in
the same transaction. Limb (b) is met: L and M are rival bidders for the same facility, each
wants to win at the lowest price, and each bid is sealed and confidential. A diversity of
interests plainly exists.

**What r 20(3) requires before Betty acts.** Rule 20(3) PCR states that before accepting any
instructions from any relevant party in relation to the matter or transaction, the legal
practitioner or law practice (a) must communicate directly with each relevant party (i) to
explain to that relevant party (A) how the interests of all or any of the relevant parties
diverge or may diverge; (B) how the legal practitioner or law practice may be prevented from
disclosing to a relevant party information obtained from another relevant party, despite the
relevance of the information to the matter or transaction; and (C) how the legal practitioner
or law practice may be prevented from giving to a relevant party any advice that is
prejudicial to another relevant party; (ii) to inform that relevant party that the legal
practitioner or law practice must cease to act in the matter or transaction if, in the course
of the retainer, the legal practitioner or law practice has difficulty in advising on and
dealing with the relevant parties' divergent interests competently, evenly and consistently;
(iii) to receive and deal with any queries which that relevant party may have on the matter or
transaction, or on the risks of all or any of the relevant parties being jointly represented
in the matter or transaction by the legal practitioner or law practice; and (iv) to ascertain
precisely the intentions of that relevant party; (b) in the case of a transaction that is
ostensibly or potentially disadvantageous to a particular relevant party, must also
communicate directly with the relevant party (i) to explain the relevant party's position
before the transaction, and how the relevant party's position will or may be altered to the
relevant party's detriment by the transaction; (ii) to verify whether any instructions
purportedly given on behalf of the relevant party do in fact reflect the relevant party's
intentions; and (iii) to remove any doubt as to whether the relevant party may have been
misled by, or may be acting under the undue influence of, another person; (c) must advise each
relevant party to obtain independent legal advice; (d) if a particular relevant party does not
obtain independent legal advice, must obtain a written confirmation from the relevant party,
or maintain a written record, that the relevant party declines to do so; and (e) must obtain
each relevant party's informed consent in writing to the legal practitioner or law practice
acting for all relevant parties, despite the relevant parties' divergent interests.

Applying r 20(3), before Betty accepted M's instructions TL had to communicate directly with
both L and M under limb (a). It had to explain that, as rival bidders, their interests
diverge; that TL may be unable to pass to one what it learns from the other, such as the
other's bid; and that TL may be unable to give one advice that prejudices the other, such as
how to outbid it. It had to tell each that TL must cease to act if it cannot advise them
competently, evenly and consistently, answer their queries on the risks of joint
representation, and ascertain precisely what each intends. Limb (b) is unlikely to be
engaged, as bidding is not ostensibly disadvantageous to either bidder. TL then had to advise
each to obtain independent legal advice (limb (c)), obtain written confirmation or keep a
written record if either declined (limb (d)), and obtain each party's informed consent in
writing (limb (e)). Betty did none of this.

*The point that earns extra marks* (SQ p111): before Archie can even approach L to seek its
consent, Betty must first obtain M's consent to disclose to L that M is an interested bidder.
The fact that M has approached TL is itself information confidential to M and acquired in the
course of TL's engagement, so r 6(2) PCR (set out in 1.1 above) prevents its disclosure. It
can be revealed to L only under r 6(3)(a) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner may
disclose such information if the client authorises the disclosure. The same applies the other
way. Betty knows that Archie acts for L, and she may not tell M that without L's consent.

**Authorities.** The standard of skill and care expected of a solicitor acting for multiple
parties, as regards each client, must be at least equivalent to that of a solicitor acting
for a single party. The solicitor cannot prefer the interests of one client to the others. A
solicitor who, objectively speaking, reaches the crossroads where he has difficulty in
advising and dealing with multiple clients competently, evenly and consistently ought to
discharge himself from further involvement in the subject matter (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong
Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). Acting for multiple clients is a
fiduciary position. A solicitor may act for more than one client in a commercial deal, but
must identify and disclose any actual or potential conflicts of interest, obtain informed
consent from all clients, and remain impartial. Precautions include advising each client of
the risks and consequences of multiple representation and of the actual or potential
conflict, full disclosure and advice to seek independent legal advice, advising on the legal
ramifications of the transaction, and keeping proper documents and contemporaneous
attendance notes. A firm that fails to manage the conflict risks being disqualified from
acting for all of them (*Law Society of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram* [2009] 4 SLR(R)
674 at [34]–[53]). Consent must be genuinely informed. The courts have set out the precautions needed: advise on the risks, obtain informed consent, advise independent legal advice and record any refusal in writing (*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36 at [71], [149]–[150]; *Kuek Siang Wei v Kuek Siew Chew* [2015] 5 SLR 357). Where a solicitor acts for
multiple clients with conflicting interests, and one client enters into a transaction which
prejudices him and benefits another client, the transaction is suspect and liable to be set
aside in the absence of clear evidence that the solicitor fully advised the prejudiced client as to the terms and effect of the transaction (*Mahidon* at [80]). Preferring one client's interests over another's is misconduct (*Law Society of
Singapore v Ezekiel Peter Latimer* [2019] SGHC 92).

**Continuing duties and safeguards.** Rule 20(4) PCR states that the legal practitioner or
law practice (a) must throughout the course of the retainer for the matter or transaction
(i) continue to be vigilant of any conflict or potential conflict between the interests of
any of the relevant parties; and (ii) inform each relevant party of any conflict or potential
conflict that arises or may arise between the interests of any of the relevant parties; and
(b) must cease to act in the matter or transaction if, in the course of the retainer, the
legal practitioner or law practice has difficulty in advising on and dealing with the
relevant parties' divergent interests competently, evenly and consistently. Rule 20(6) PCR
imposes the same two duties, in the same words, where (under r 20(5)) a law practice acts for
2 or more parties and a diversity of interests arises only during the course of the retainer.
In practice TL must put effective information barriers between Archie's team and Betty's
team. These should include (a) physical and IT separation of the teams and files; (b) written
instructions to, and signed acknowledgements from, every lawyer and staff member involved;
and (c) internal monitoring of whether the barrier is working (SQ p112). *Beyond the key:* Archie and Betty are partners in the same corporate department, and Betty has
already "discussed the matter with Archie". The barrier must be set up at once, and TL should
check that nothing about L's bid or RPL's minimum price passed to Betty. If anything did, Betty
cannot act.

In conclusion, if TL obtains each client's informed written consent after the r 20(3)
explanations, secures M's consent before disclosing M's interest to L, and puts adequate
barriers in place, Archie may continue for L and Betty may act for M. Betty's decision to act
the next day without these steps did not comply with r 20(3) PCR and was not acceptable.

### 1.3 Can Cheryl act for the DSA? (14 marks)

The issue is whether TL may also act for the DSA in the same sale while Archie acts for L and
Betty for M. In my view it should not. This multiple representation is unworkable even with
safeguards.

**Rule 20 applies again.** Here, r 20 PCR applies again. Taking the r 20(2) limbs set out
in 1.2 above, TL would be acting for 2 or more different parties to one transaction (limb
(a)), because the DSA must approve the sale and is therefore a party to it. The DSA's
interests diverge, or may reasonably be expected to diverge, from L's and M's (limb (b)). So
all of the r 20(3) steps set out in 1.2 would be required before Cheryl accepted the DSA's
instructions. The decisive question is the one in r 20(3)(a)(ii) and r 20(4)(b): whether TL
could advise on and deal with all three parties' divergent interests competently, evenly
and consistently. If it cannot, it must not act, or must cease to act (r 20(4)(b), (6)(b)
PCR), and *Lie Hendri Rusli* at [48]–[50] says the same of a solicitor who reaches that
"crossroads".

**Why it is unworkable.** The DSA can veto any sale at its sole discretion. It will approve only
if it is satisfied that the purchaser can operate the facility, and only if the successful
bidder provides a $5 million on-demand performance bond. Cheryl's role would therefore be to
advise the regulator on whether to approve each bidder and on what terms. Archie and Betty would
need to advise their clients on how to satisfy, negotiate with, or even challenge the DSA's
exercise of its discretion. M imports and exports drugs and has no manufacturing experience, so
the DSA has a particular reason to doubt M's capability and to veto M's bid. L's position is
also uncertain, as we are not told the nature of its business. If the DSA takes a position
against L or M, Archie and Betty would have to advise their clients against TL's own client.
Cheryl would have to advise the DSA on how to maintain that position (SQ p112–113).
Information barriers protect confidential information. They cannot cure the inability of one
firm to advise each side on opposite positions evenly (*Lie Hendri Rusli* at [48]–[50]).

*Beyond the key:* Cheryl already knows that Archie acts for L and Betty for M. That is
information confidential to L and M, so r 6(2) PCR (set out in 1.1) bars her from telling
the DSA unless L and M authorise it under r 6(3)(a), yet the DSA's assessment of bidders is
exactly where that knowledge would matter. Acting for the regulator would also risk the
appearance that TL's bidder clients have privileged access to the decision-maker.

In conclusion, Cheryl should decline to act for the DSA. If TL had already accepted the DSA's
instructions, it would have to cease to act under r 20(4)(b) or (6)(b) PCR. Rule 20(7) PCR
states that, despite paragraphs (4)(b) and (6)(b), the legal practitioner or law practice may
continue to act for a relevant party in the matter or transaction if (a) the legal
practitioner or law practice ceases to act for all other relevant parties whose interests
diverge from the interests of that relevant party; and (b) all of those other relevant
parties give their informed consent in writing for the legal practitioner or law practice to
continue to act in the matter or transaction. So TL could keep one client only if it dropped
the others and they all consented in writing. Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act 1966
provides that due cause for discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor
(b) has been guilty of fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of his or her
professional duty, or guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the
Professional Conduct Council under section 71 (which includes the PCR) as amounts to improper
conduct or practice as an advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty of such misconduct
unbefitting an advocate and solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court or as a member of an
honourable profession. A breach of r 20 may amount to such improper conduct or misconduct.

## 2023 Q2 — Yeager, Shigan Partners and KP's website

Question: PYP23 p3–4 (handwritten corrections on the scan: 2.1 and 2.2 ask about acting for
**R**, not B). Marks: 2.1 18, 2.2 18, 2.3 14. Pre-writes used: 0-9, 0-1, 0-22, 0-13, 0-12,
0-15, 0-23, 0-29, 0-30. No official answer exists.

### 2.1 Should Y cease to act for R? (18 marks)

Rule 4(a) PCR states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to the court, which
takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client.
Rule 4(b) PCR states that a legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client is
subject only to the legal practitioner's duty to the court, and must at all times be
fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the Singapore legal system
and the legal profession in Singapore. In my view Y should cease to act for R and apply to be
discharged. Y will breach r 11(3)(a) PCR if he continues, because he is now a material
witness on contested issues, and Y's and SP's own conduct is in issue under r 22 PCR. Before
reaching that, it is necessary to rule out the more absolute bar that would apply if B had
also been Y's client.

**Was B also a client?** The first issue is whether Y or SP acted for B as well as R on the
JVA. If so, r 20(8) PCR would bar SP outright, and r 21 PCR would also be engaged. B is
unlikely to have been a client. Rule 20(8) PCR states that where a legal practitioner or law
practice has prepared a document on behalf of 2 or more parties concerning their rights and
obligations, and 2 or more of those parties become involved in a dispute arising from the
document, the legal practitioner or law practice must not represent any disputing party in
the dispute. Unlike r 20(3), it has no consent exception. A retainer need not be express. A
retainer may be implied where, on an objective consideration of all the circumstances, an
intention to enter into such a contractual relationship ought fairly and properly to be
imputed to all the parties, and the implication must be so clear that the solicitor ought to
have appreciated it (*Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64], [66]). Here Y was instructed by and advised R. The
JVA recites that both parties took independent legal advice, and B told Y that a lawyer
friend in Germany would review the draft. On Y's account he never advised B, so the JVA
was prepared on R's behalf alone and r 20(8) does not apply. However, B now pleads that Y
told him it would be "fine" to sign without a Singapore lawyer. If that is proved, it is
legal advice given to B and could support an argument that B looked to Y for protection, since express advice that is neither perfunctory nor non-committal points to an implied retainer (*Ahmad Khalis* at [67]). I therefore conclude that B was probably not a client, but the
point is itself a disputed question of fact. That leads to the decisive problem.

**Y as a material witness.** The next issue is whether Y will be required to give evidence
material to a contested issue. He will, so he must apply to be discharged. Here, r 11(3) PCR
applies, which states that in any case where it is known or it appears that a legal
practitioner will be required to give evidence which is material to the determination of any
contested issue before a court or tribunal, (a) the legal practitioner (i) must not accept
instructions from any party to that case; and (ii) must, if the legal practitioner was acting
for any party to that case, discharge himself or herself, or, where the legal practitioner
has represented that party in any proceedings relating to that case, apply to be discharged,
from acting further for that party; but (b) the law practice in which the legal practitioner
practises, or a member of that law practice, may represent or continue to represent any
party to that case, unless doing so would prejudice the administration of justice. PD 1.7.1
states that a legal practitioner shall not represent any party in any case in which he or she
has reason to believe that he or she will be a witness in respect of a material and disputed
question of fact, and if while appearing in a case it becomes apparent that he or she will
be such a witness, he or she should discharge himself or herself and, in so doing, take all
reasonable steps to ensure that he or she does not jeopardise the client's interests. It
also states that a legal practitioner shall not appear before an appellate tribunal if, in
the case under appeal, he or she has been a witness on a material and disputed question of
fact in the court below, and that nothing in the PD prevents a legal practitioner from
swearing an affidavit as to formal or undisputed facts in matters in which he or she acts or
appears. The rule rests on the principle in r 11(1) PCR that a legal practitioner's duty to
advance the interests of the client, and to present the client's best possible case, is
subject to the legal practitioner's duties as an officer of the court and to assist in the
administration of justice. The witness's duty to tell the whole
truth and the advocate's duty to present the client's best case should not be combined in
one person. In *Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], the solicitor
whose account of the transaction would be contested should not have acted. Here the
pleadings put two things directly in issue. The first is what Y said to B at the Meeting:
B says Y told him he need not appoint a Singapore lawyer and never pointed out the
Independent Advice Clause. The second is what B told Y on the telephone: R pleads that B
said a German lawyer would advise him. These matters go to whether B can resist the JVA or
the Independent Advice Clause, so they are material. Y is the only person who can give
R's side. The call was between B and Y alone. The former associate is overseas and
presumed uncontactable. Nobody knows who B's German lawyer was. The contemporaneous
attendance notes are Y's own and can only be proved through Y, who will be cross-examined
on them. None of this is formal or undisputed, so the PD 1.7.1 exception does not help.
It is also not a bare assertion by KP, because the dispute is on the pleadings, so it is
"known or it appears" that Y will be required to give evidence material to the
determination of contested issues. Y has already represented R in these proceedings, so the
second limb of r 11(3)(a)(ii) applies: he must apply to be discharged, and PD 1.7.1 requires
him to take all reasonable steps not to jeopardise R's interests in doing so.

**Conflict between Y's/SP's interests and R's.** The third issue is whether Y and SP have
their own interest in the matter that may conflict with R's. They do, and this is an
independent reason to step aside. Here, r 22 PCR applies. Rule 22(2) PCR states that, except
as otherwise permitted by the rule, a legal practitioner or law practice must not act for a
client if there is, or may reasonably be expected to be, a conflict between (a) the duty to
serve the best interests of the client; and (b) the interests of the legal practitioner or
law practice. Rule 22(3) PCR states that where a legal practitioner, any immediate family
member of the legal practitioner, or the law practice in which the legal practitioner
practises has an interest in any matter entrusted to the legal practitioner by a client of
the legal practitioner, (a) in any case where the interest is adverse to the client's
interests, the legal practitioner must withdraw from representing the client, unless (i) the
legal practitioner makes a full and frank disclosure of the adverse interest to the client;
(ii) the legal practitioner advises the client to obtain independent legal advice; (iii) if
the client does not obtain independent legal advice, the legal practitioner ensures that the
client is not under an impression that the legal practitioner is protecting the client's
interests; and (iv) despite sub-paragraphs (i) and (ii), the client gives the client's
informed consent in writing to the legal practitioner acting, or continuing to act, on the
client's behalf; or (b) in any other case, the legal practitioner must withdraw unless (i) the
legal practitioner makes a full and frank disclosure of the interest to the client; and (ii)
despite sub-paragraph (i), the client gives the client's informed consent in writing to the
legal practitioner acting, or continuing to act, on the client's behalf. Rule 22(4)(a) PCR
imposes the same four conditions, in the same words, on a law practice that has an adverse
interest in a matter entrusted to it by its client. In *Ho Kon Kim* at [61]–[63], the firm
whose handling of the transaction was itself in issue should not have acted. In *Then Khek
Khoon v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2012] 2 SLR 451 at [30]–[36], the court refused the
opponent's application to restrain the plaintiffs' solicitors, who were said to have a
personal interest in defending their own fees. Whether the firm's interest called for
independent advice was for the firm to raise with its own clients, not for the opponent, so
it is R's position that matters, not KP's complaint. Here B's Defence attacks the JVA
"drafted by Y/SP" as unfair and says Y misled him. If B's account is accepted, Y would face
findings that he breached r 8(2) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner, when dealing on
behalf of his or her client with any person who is not represented by another legal
practitioner, (a) must decline to give to the person any legal advice (other than advice to
obtain independent legal advice), if the legal practitioner knows or ought reasonably to know
that the interests of the person are adverse, or potentially adverse, to the interests of the
client; and (b) must take reasonable steps to ensure that the person is not under the impression that the person's interests are protected by the legal practitioner. B was not "represented by another legal practitioner": the PCR use that term for the Singapore-qualified and regulated or registered foreign lawyers listed in r 3, and a lawyer friend in Germany informally reviewing the draft is not one, so r 8(2) applied to Y's dealings with B. SP might also face a claim from R if the Independent Advice Clause fails. Y therefore has a personal
interest, and SP an interest of the law practice, in vindicating Y's drafting, conduct and
notes. That interest is adverse to R's, because it may not match R's best interests: for
example, on whether to settle or whether to concede points about the Meeting. So r 22(2) is
engaged, and under r 22(3)(a) and (4)(a), even apart from r 11, Y could continue only after
full and frank disclosure (condition (i)), advice to take independent legal advice
(condition (ii)), ensuring R does not think Y is protecting R's interests if R takes no such
advice (condition (iii)), and R's informed written consent (condition (iv)). Consent cannot
cure the r 11(3)(a) problem in any event.

**KP's letter.** The fourth issue is how to treat KP's demand that SP withdraw. Y should
take it seriously, although the reason to withdraw is the PCR, not KP's demand. Apart from
the PCR, the court's supervisory jurisdiction is invoked to restrain legal practitioners
from acting if a fair-minded, reasonably informed member of the public would conclude that
the proper administration of justice requires that they be restrained from acting, in the
interests of the protection of the integrity of the judicial process and the due
administration of justice, including the appearance of justice (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29 at [65]). That case concerned a former client, but the test is framed
around the administration of justice and the appearance of justice. KP's letter names no
rule, and an opponent cannot dictate a party's choice of
counsel. But a fair-minded observer would doubt that the lawyer whose own words at the
Meeting are in issue can also present R's case. If Y stays on, KP is likely to apply to
restrain SP, which would cost R time and money later in the case. Y should reply
courteously that he is applying to be discharged, without accepting that any conflict
exists between R and SP.

**How to withdraw.** Here, r 26(5)(f) PCR applies, which states that a legal practitioner
may withdraw from representing a client in a case or matter if the withdrawal is necessary to
avoid a contravention by the legal practitioner of (i) the Act; (ii) these Rules or any other
subsidiary legislation made under the Act; (iii) any practice directions, guidance notes and
rulings issued under section 71(6) of the Act; (iv) any practice directions, guidance notes
and rulings (relating to professional practice, etiquette, conduct and discipline) issued by
the Council or the Society; or (v) if the legal practitioner is duly authorised or registered
to practise law in any state or territory other than Singapore, any practice directions,
guidance notes and rulings (relating to professional practice, etiquette, conduct and
discipline) issued by (A) the foreign authority having the function conferred by law of
authorising or registering persons to practise law in that state or territory; or (B) any
relevant professional body of that state or territory. Withdrawal is necessary to avoid a
contravention of r 11(3)(a) (head (ii)) and of PD 1.7.1 (head (iv)). Rule 26(6) PCR states
that where a legal practitioner withdraws from representing a client in a case or matter,
the legal practitioner must (a) take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to the client,
including, where the circumstances permit, (i) by giving reasonable notice of the withdrawal
to the client; (ii) by giving the client a reasonable amount of time to engage another legal
practitioner to take over the case or matter; and (iii) by cooperating with the client's new
legal practitioner; and (b) abide by the client's decision on whether to appoint another
legal practitioner, and who to appoint, to take over the case or matter, if not completed.
Rule 5(2)(b) PCR also requires a legal practitioner, when advising the client, to inform the
client of all information known to the legal practitioner that may reasonably affect the
interests of the client in the matter, other than (i) any information that the legal
practitioner is precluded, by any overriding duty of confidentiality, from disclosing to the
client; and (ii) any information that the client has agreed in writing need not be disclosed
to the client. So Y should explain to R in writing why he must stop, apply to court to be
discharged, preserve the file and the attendance notes and hand them over, cooperate with
R's new solicitors, abide by R's choice of them, and make himself available as R's witness.
Withdrawing now, at the pleadings stage, causes R the least harm.

In conclusion, Y should cease to act for R and apply to be discharged. Continuing would
breach r 11(3)(a) PCR and PD 1.7.1, risks breaching r 22, and invites an application to
restrain SP. Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 provides that due cause for
discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor (b) has been guilty of
fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of his or her professional duty, or
guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the Professional Conduct
Council under section 71 (which includes the PCR) as amounts to improper conduct or practice
as an advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty of such misconduct unbefitting an
advocate and solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court or as a member of an honourable
profession. Such a breach may amount to that improper conduct or misconduct.

### 2.2 Can A take over conduct and act for R? (18 marks)

The issue is whether, with Y gone, A as SP's other partner can take over. Rule 11(3)(b) PCR
allows this in principle, but on these facts A should not. SP should advise R to instruct
another firm.

**Rule 11(3)(b): would A acting prejudice the administration of justice?** Even where one
lawyer will be a material witness, "the law practice in which the legal practitioner
practises, or a member of that law practice, may represent or continue to represent any
party to that case, unless doing so would prejudice the administration of justice"
(r 11(3)(b) PCR). The PCR therefore does not disqualify the whole firm automatically. The
question is whether A's involvement would prejudice the administration of justice. In
*Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], the Court of Appeal said that
neither the conveyancing partner, who was a material witness, nor the litigation partner
of the same firm should have acted. This was because the firm's own handling of the
transaction was in issue. The court's wider supervisory jurisdiction, under the *Lim Oon
Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29 test set out in 2.1 above, asks the same
kind of question: whether a fair-minded, reasonably informed member of the public would
conclude that the proper administration of justice, including the appearance of justice,
requires the firm to be restrained from acting.

Several facts favour A. He was not involved in the JVA at any point, has no personal
knowledge of the Meeting or the telephone call, and will not be a witness. Rule 11(3)(b)
expressly contemplates a colleague carrying on. R's choice of counsel deserves respect
(r 26(6)(b) PCR), and SP already knows the file, which saves R costs.

The facts against A are stronger. First, the firm's own conduct is attacked. B pleads that
the JVA drafted by "Y/SP" was unfair and that SP's partner misled him. A is one of only two
partners, so he shares SP's interest in defeating those allegations. That interest covers
the firm's reputation, its possible liability to R, and Y's disciplinary exposure. This is
an interest of the law practice adverse to R's, which engages r 22(2) and r 22(4)(a) PCR
(set out in 2.1 above). Second, A would have
to lead evidence-in-chief from his own senior partner, who is also his former supervisor
(Y has 18 years' standing and trained A; A has 8 years and was only recently made
partner). A would have to present Y's credibility and cross-examine B, whose whole case is
that Y misled him. In a two-partner firm, A cannot credibly be seen as independent of Y.
The risk is that A runs the case in the way that best protects Y and SP rather than R, for
example by resisting a sensible settlement or fighting over the attendance notes. Third,
information barriers are no answer. The problem is not confidential information but the
firm's own interest and A's relationship with the witness, and a two-partner firm cannot
be meaningfully separated anyway. Fourth, if the court later found that B had looked to Y
for advice, the r 20(8) and r 21 PCR objections would bind the whole law practice, A
included. Finally, a fair-minded observer would very likely see SP's continued
involvement as compromising the appearance of justice. A restraint application succeeding
mid-trial would harm R far more than a change of firm now.

In conclusion, A should not take over. If R still wishes to keep SP after SP meets the four
r 22(4)(a)(i)–(iv) PCR conditions set out in 2.1 (full and frank disclosure of the firm's
interest, advice to take independent legal advice, ensuring R does not think SP is
protecting R's interests if R takes no such advice, and R's informed written consent), A
could act only if satisfied that this would not prejudice the administration of justice
under r 11(3)(b). On these facts that is doubtful. The prudent course is for R to instruct an
independent firm, with SP cooperating in the handover as r 26(6)(a)(iii) PCR requires.

### 2.3 Are the statements on KP's website permissible? (14 marks)

The issue is whether KP's website statements comply with Part 5 of the PCR. Neither does:
the KP lawyers breached r 43 and r 44 PCR. Here, r 42, r 43 and r 44 PCR apply. Rule 42(1)
PCR states that a legal practitioner must ensure that any publicity relating to the legal
practitioner's practice complies with these Rules, whether that publicity is by the legal
practitioner or by any other person on the legal practitioner's behalf. Rule 42(2) PCR states
that where a legal practitioner becomes aware of any impropriety in any publicity relating to
the legal practitioner's practice or the practice of a law practice of which the legal
practitioner is a director or a partner, the legal practitioner must use the legal
practitioner's best endeavours to procure the rectification or withdrawal of the publicity,
and to prevent the recurrence of the impropriety. Rule 43(1) PCR states that when a legal
practitioner publicises the legal practitioner's practice or the practice of a law practice
of which the legal practitioner is a director, a partner or an employee, the legal
practitioner must ensure that (a) any claim to expertise or specialisation can be justified;
(b) the publicity does not make any direct or indirect mention of (i) any past case in
which, or any client for whom, the legal practitioner, the law practice or any member of the
law practice had acted, if the provision of any such information will result in a breach of
any duty of confidentiality owed to a client or former client of the legal practitioner or
law practice; or (ii) the success rate of
the legal practitioner, the law practice or any member of the law practice; and (c) the
publicity does not make any comparison or criticism in relation to the fees charged, or the
quality of the services provided, by any other legal practitioner or law practice. Rule 43(2)
PCR states that, for the purposes of paragraph (1)(a), the following factors may be taken
into account in justifying any claim to expertise or specialisation: (a) academic
qualifications; (b) experience; (c) proportion of working time involved; (d) level of
success achieved; (e) complexity of law and practice; (f) significance of the matters
involving the legal practitioner or the law practice; and (g) assessment by peers. Rule 44(1)
PCR states that a legal practitioner must not publicise the legal practitioner's practice,
or the practice of the law practice of which the legal practitioner is a director, a partner
or an employee, in a manner which (a) is likely to diminish public confidence in the legal
profession or to otherwise bring the legal profession into disrepute; (b) may reasonably be
regarded as being misleading, deceptive, inaccurate, false or unbefitting the dignity of the
legal profession; or (c) the Council may determine to be an undesirable manner of
publicising the practice of a legal practitioner or law practice. Rule 44(2) PCR states that
publicity is misleading, deceptive, inaccurate or false if (a) it contains a material
misrepresentation; (b) it omits to state a material fact; (c) it contains any information
which cannot be verified; or (d) it is likely to create an unjustified expectation about the
results that can be achieved by the legal practitioner or the law practice.

**Statement (i).** "We are the best of the best because unlike other lawyers, we never give
up fighting for our clients for ten years at least" is not permissible. "Best of the best"
claims superiority over every other practice. No r 43(2) factor could justify it, and it
cannot be verified (r 43(1)(a), r 44(2)(c)). "Unlike other lawyers, we never give up" is an
express comparison with other lawyers, and implies they do give up, which criticises the
quality of their services. This is the very thing r 43(1)(c) forbids. "Fighting … for ten
years at least" cannot be verified either. It also presents prolonged litigation as a
virtue, which sits badly with the principle in r 9(1)(a) PCR that a legal practitioner has a
duty to assist in the administration of justice, and must act honourably in the interests of
the administration of justice. It is likely to diminish public confidence in the profession
(r 44(1)(a)).
Statement (i) breaches r 43(1)(a), r 43(1)(c) and r 44.

**Statement (ii).** "Work injuries, employment claims? Our expert litigators will crush your
enemies and recover millions for you!" is also not permissible as written. "Expert
litigators" is a claim to expertise. It is allowed only if KP can justify it on the
r 43(2) factors, such as the experience of its litigators in work-injury and employment
claims and the proportion of their time spent on such work. We are not told that it can,
and the burden is on KP. "Crush your enemies" presents litigation as a personal vendetta.
It is aggressive language unbefitting the dignity of the profession (r 44(1)(b)) and
likely to diminish public confidence in it (r 44(1)(a)). "Recover millions for you"
promises a result that no lawyer can promise. What a claimant recovers depends on the
facts and the law, not the lawyer. The phrase is therefore likely to create an unjustified
expectation about results (r 44(2)(d)) and is unverifiable (r 44(2)(c)). That is
especially serious because the advertisement targets injured workers and employees, who
are likely to be vulnerable.

In conclusion, both statements breach r 43 and r 44 PCR. The KP lawyers responsible must
use their best endeavours to have them withdrawn or rewritten in factual, verifiable terms
(r 42(2) PCR). Rule 42(3) PCR also states that where the Council determines that any
publicity relating to the practice of a legal practitioner or law practice is contrary to
any provision of these Rules, the Council may order the legal practitioner or law practice to
alter, withdraw, remove or discontinue the publicity or cause it to be altered, withdrawn,
removed or discontinued. Such a breach may amount to improper conduct or misconduct
unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and
(h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 (set out in 2.1 above).

### Model answer

**(a) Priya's conduct in Part A (15 marks)**

Rule 4(a) PCR states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to the court, which
takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client.
Rule 4(b) PCR states that a legal practitioner's duty to the legal practitioner's client is
subject only to the legal practitioner's duty to the court, and must at all times be
fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the Singapore legal system
and the legal profession in Singapore. Priya breached r 25, r 22, r 6 and r 8(2) PCR.

*Gift to Mei.* The first issue is whether Priya could draft a will leaving $300,000 to her
own daughter. She could not: Priya breached r 25(2) PCR. Here, r 25 PCR applies. Rule 25(1)
PCR states that paragraph (2) applies (a) where a client of a legal practitioner intends to
make a significant gift, whether by will or while the client is alive, or in any other
manner, to (i) the legal practitioner; (ii) the sole proprietor, or any partner, director,
consultant or employee, of the law practice in which the legal practitioner practises;
(iii) any immediate family member of (A) the legal practitioner; or (B) the sole proprietor,
or any partner, director, consultant or employee, of the law practice in which the legal
practitioner practises; or (iv) the law practice in which the legal practitioner practises;
or (b) where a client of a law practice intends to make a significant gift, whether by will
or while the client is alive, or in any other manner, to (i) the law practice; (ii) the sole
proprietor, or any partner, director, consultant or employee, of the law practice; or
(iii) any immediate family member of the sole proprietor, or any partner, director,
consultant or employee, of the law practice. Rule 25(2) PCR states that the legal
practitioner or law practice (a) must not act for the client in relation to the gift; and
(b) must advise the client to obtain independent legal advice in relation to the gift. Under
r 2(1) PCR, "immediate family member" means a spouse, child, grandchild, sibling, sibling's
child, parent or grandparent. Whether a gift is significant is judged not just in absolute
terms but relative to the client's means and the expectations of other prospective
beneficiaries (*Law Society of Singapore v Wan Hui Hong James* [2013] 3 SLR 221 at [10]). Merely telling the client to seek independent advice is not enough (at [15]). That case sets out the steps (at [18](a)–(g)): (1) explain why independent advice is needed, and that influence may exist even if the client does not perceive it; (2) the adviser must be another advocate and solicitor; (3) ask the client to nominate the adviser, and ensure the adviser has no compromising connection; (4) advise the client to obtain written confirmation from the adviser that a full explanation was given; (5) give the adviser the information needed for a complex transaction without influencing the advice; (6) not suggest the advice is a formality; and (7) if the client declines, insist with some force, and absent such advice decline to accept the gift. Separately, a lawyer acting in respect of the gift must refuse to act (at [19]–[22]).

Applying r 25(1)(a)(iii)(A), Mei is Priya's daughter, so she is Priya's "child" and an
immediate family member. A gift of $300,000 out of an estate of about $900,000 is a third
of Mr Goh's assets, so it is significant relative to his means, and it cuts across the
expectations of his nephew Kelvin, who was the main beneficiary. Paragraph (2) therefore
applied. On limb (a), drafting the very will that makes the gift is acting "in relation to
the gift". On limb (b), telling Mr Goh that independent advice is "honestly … not necessary"
is the opposite of advising him to obtain it, and Priya took none of the *Wan Hui Hong*
steps. Priya breached both limbs of r 25(2) and should not have drafted the will.

*Priya's own interest.* The second issue is whether Priya's family interest conflicted
with her duty to Mr Goh. It did: Priya breached r 22 PCR. Rule 22(2) PCR states that, except
as otherwise permitted by the rule, a legal practitioner or law practice must not act for a
client if there is, or may reasonably be expected to be, a conflict between (a) the duty to
serve the best interests of the client; and (b) the interests of the legal practitioner or
law practice. Rule 22(3) PCR states that where a legal practitioner, any immediate family
member of the legal practitioner, or the law practice in which the legal practitioner
practises has an interest in any matter entrusted to the legal practitioner by a client of
the legal practitioner, (a) in any case where the interest is adverse to the client's
interests, the legal practitioner must withdraw from representing the client, unless (i) the
legal practitioner makes a full and frank disclosure of the adverse interest to the client;
(ii) the legal practitioner advises the client to obtain independent legal advice; (iii) if
the client does not obtain independent legal advice, the legal practitioner ensures that the
client is not under an impression that the legal practitioner is protecting the client's
interests; and (iv) despite sub-paragraphs (i) and (ii), the client gives the client's
informed consent in writing to the legal practitioner acting, or continuing to act, on the
client's behalf; or (b) in any other case, the legal practitioner must withdraw unless (i) the
legal practitioner makes a full and frank disclosure of the interest to the client; and (ii)
despite sub-paragraph (i), the client gives the client's informed consent in writing to the
legal practitioner acting, or continuing to act, on the client's behalf. In *Law Society of
Singapore v Lee Suet Fern* [2020] 5 SLR 1151, a lawyer whose husband was a significant
beneficiary took part in the preparation and execution of her father-in-law's will, and her
conduct was found to show patently divided loyalties. Mei, an immediate family member,
benefits from every dollar Mr Goh leaves her, so her interest in the will is adverse to Mr
Goh's interest in disposing of his estate free of any influence, and r 22(3)(a) applies.
Priya made no disclosure (condition (i)), gave no advice to take independent advice
(condition (ii)), did nothing to dispel any impression that she was protecting him
(condition (iii)), and obtained no informed written consent (condition (iv)). That breached
r 22. Priya may continue on the flat sale only if that matter is unaffected by the gift.

*An elderly and recently bereaved client.* Priya also had to take particular care because
Mr Goh is 81, recently widowed and emotionally reliant on Mei's visits. Those are classic
signs of a risk of undue influence. The cases call for extra caution where a client suffers
from any mental infirmity, for example when preparing or witnessing the execution of a will
(*Low Ah Cheow v Ng Hock Guan* [2009] 3 SLR(R) 1079; *Chee Mu Lin Muriel v Chee Ka Lin
Caroline* [2010] 4 SLR 373). Rule 5(2)(k) PCR also requires a legal practitioner to keep
proper contemporaneous records of all instructions received from, and all advice rendered
to, the client. Together these point to seeing Mr Goh alone, checking his capacity and
understanding, and keeping a full attendance note. Priya did none of this, which compounds
the r 25 and r 22 breaches.

*What Priya told Kelvin.* The third issue is whether Priya could reveal the effect of the
new will to Kelvin. She could not: Priya breached r 6(2) PCR, which states that, subject to
paragraph (3), a legal practitioner must not knowingly disclose any information which (a) is
confidential to his or her client; and (b) is acquired by the legal practitioner (whether
from the client or from any other person) in the course of the legal practitioner's
engagement. Rule 6(3) PCR states that a legal practitioner may disclose such information if
(a) the client authorises the disclosure; (b) the legal practitioner is permitted or is
required by law, by an order of court, or by a tribunal to make the disclosure; (c) the legal
practitioner discloses the information in confidence, for the sole purpose of obtaining
advice in connection with the legal practitioner's legal or ethical obligations; (d) the
legal practitioner discloses the information in confidence to a provider or broker of the
legal practitioner's professional indemnity insurance, in connection with any claim or
potential claim, or any complaint or potential complaint, by any person against the legal
practitioner; or (e) the legal practitioner discloses the information for the sole purpose
of responding to or defending any charge or complaint, relating to the legal practitioner's
conduct or professional behaviour, brought against the legal practitioner in court, before a
Review Committee, an Inquiry Committee or a Disciplinary Tribunal, before a complaints
committee appointed under section 36S(5) of the Act, or before any relevant professional
disciplinary body of a state or territory (other than Singapore) in which the legal
practitioner is duly authorised or registered to practise law. On limb (a), the contents of a
living client's will are confidential to him. On limb (b), Priya learned them in the course
of her engagement to draft the will. Mr Goh did not authorise disclosure, and none of the
other exceptions comes close. Telling Kelvin that "your share is only a bit smaller"
disclosed them, in breach of r 6(2).

*Kelvin as an unrepresented person.* The fourth issue is whether Priya dealt properly with
Kelvin, who had no lawyer. She did not: Priya breached r 8(2) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner, when dealing on behalf of his or her client with any person who is not
represented by another legal practitioner, (a) must decline to give to the person any legal
advice (other than advice to obtain independent legal advice), if the legal practitioner
knows or ought reasonably to know that the interests of the person are adverse, or
potentially adverse, to the interests of the client; and (b) must take reasonable steps to
ensure that the person is not under the impression that the person's interests are protected
by the legal practitioner. Where a legal practitioner acts for one party in a transaction and
other parties may reasonably believe that the practitioner is advising or protecting their
interests, the practitioner must clearly disabuse them of that impression. If their interests
are adverse or potentially adverse, the practitioner must decline to advise them and should
instead advise them to seek independent legal advice (*Law Society of Singapore v Ganesan
Krishnan* [2003] 2 SLR(R) 251; *Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006]
4 SLR(R) 308 at [74]). On limb (a), Kelvin's interest is adverse, because he was the main
beneficiary and has lost out to Mei, and Priya knew it. "The new will is fair to everyone" is
a view on the merits that he should only have been told to get independent advice about. On
limb (b), "I'm looking after the whole family's interests" creates exactly the impression
r 8(2)(b) forbids. Given that her own daughter benefits, it is also misleading. That is
contrary to the principle in r 8(1)(a) PCR that a legal practitioner who deals with any
person must be honest and courteous, and behave in a manner befitting the legal
practitioner's professional standing, and to r 8(3)(b) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner must not act towards any person in a way which is fraudulent, deceitful or
otherwise contrary to the legal practitioner's position as a member of an honourable
profession.

In conclusion, Priya breached r 25(2), r 22(3)(a), r 6(2) and r 8(2). She should stop
acting on the will and tell Mr Goh to see another firm about it. She should correct
Kelvin's impression that she acts for him, advise him to get independent advice, and
reveal nothing more about the will. Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 provides
that due cause for discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor (b) has
been guilty of fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of his or her
professional duty, or guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the
Professional Conduct Council under section 71 (which includes the PCR) as amounts to improper
conduct or practice as an advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty of such misconduct
unbefitting an advocate and solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court or as a member of an
honourable profession. Priya's breaches may amount to that improper conduct or misconduct.

**(b) Advice to Priya in Part B (15 marks)**

*(i) Bail.* The issue is whether Priya may stand surety for Arun. She may not: doing so
would breach r 14(3)(a) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner who represents an
accused person must not provide any surety or bail for the accused person. The prohibition
is absolute, with no exception. Bail is money put at risk personally by a surety to ensure
the accused's attendance in future court proceedings. Standing bail would show that Priya
has a personal interest in Arun's release beyond the scope of her representation, and would
give her a personal interest in being repaid which may conflict with her duties to him. A
remanded client is also in no position to give informed consent to that conflict. Her long
friendship with Arun's family makes no difference. If anything, it is a reason to be careful
about r 11(2)(a) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must not appear before a court
or tribunal in a matter where it would be difficult for the legal practitioner to maintain
the legal practitioner's professional independence by reason of any commercial, family,
personal or other relationship between the legal practitioner and his or her client. Priya
must withdraw the offer.

*(ii) Continuing after the confession.* The issue is whether Priya may keep acting now that
Arun has admitted taking the money. She may, within limits. Here, r 14(4) PCR applies, which
states that where a legal practitioner represents an accused person, and the accused person
confesses to any offence to the legal practitioner, the legal practitioner (a) may continue
to represent the accused person; but (b) must not adduce any evidence or make any
submission which is inconsistent with the confession by the accused person. The confession
is information confidential to Arun and acquired in the course of Priya's engagement, so
r 6(2) PCR (set out in part (a)) bars its disclosure, and none of the r 6(3) exceptions set
out there applies: Arun has not authorised disclosure, and no law or order requires it.
Applying limb (a), Priya may continue to put the prosecution to proof, for example by
testing whether its evidence establishes each element of cheating. Applying limb (b), she
cannot suggest that Arun did not take the money, by calling evidence or in submissions. This follows from r 14(2) PCR, which states that a lawyer representing an accused must pursue every reasonable defence, and raise every favourable factor, on the accused's behalf in accordance with law, read with the limit in r 14(4)(b), which applies only to *inconsistent* evidence or submissions.

*(iii) Conduct of the trial and Arun's wish to testify.* The issue is what Priya must do if
Arun insists on giving evidence that he never took the money. Here, r 14(5) PCR applies,
which states that a legal practitioner (a) must advise an accused person he or she
represents on (i) whether to plead guilty, or to claim trial, to a charge; and (ii) whether
to give evidence or to remain silent in the accused person's defence; and (b) must abide by
the decision of the accused person. Rule 10(2) PCR states that a legal practitioner whose
client is a party to any proceedings before a court or tribunal must inform the client of
the client's responsibilities to the court or tribunal, and to every other party to those
proceedings, including the client's duties (a) to be truthful towards the court or
tribunal; and (b) to comply with every legal requirement concerning the conduct and
presentation of the client's case. This creates a tension, because under r 4(a) and (b)
(set out in part (a)) her duty to the client is subject to her paramount duty to the court.
Rule 10(3) PCR states that, to the extent that a legal practitioner is able, the legal
practitioner must prevent his or her client from, must not be a party to, and must not
assist the client in, doing either or both of the following: (a) suppressing evidence; (b)
giving false evidence or false information to a court or tribunal. Rule 9(2)(a) PCR states
that when conducting any proceedings before a court or tribunal on behalf of a client, a
legal practitioner must not knowingly mislead or attempt to mislead in any way, whether by
doing anything referred to in sub-paragraph (b) (fabricating any fact or evidence) or (c)
(including in any document any statement of fact known to be false) or otherwise, (i) the
court or tribunal; (ii) any
staff of the court or tribunal; (iii) any other legal practitioner; or (iv) any witness in,
or any other person involved in or associated with, those proceedings. All solicitors, as
officers of the court, have an absolute and overriding duty to the court to ensure the
proper and efficient administration of justice, and should never mislead the court either
actively or passively (*Public Trustee v By Products Traders Pte Ltd* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 449 at
[35]). Rule 10(4) PCR states that where a legal practitioner knows that his or her client is
about to give, or has given, false evidence or false information to a court or tribunal, the
legal practitioner must (a) cease to act for the client; or (b) if the legal practitioner
continues to act for the client, conduct the client's case in a manner that does not
perpetuate the falsehood.

Applying r 14(5)(a)(ii), Priya must advise Arun on whether to give evidence, and under
r 10(2)(a) she must tell him of his duty to be truthful. She should warn him that she cannot
call him to say he never took the money. If he decides to testify, that is his decision
(r 14(5)(b)), but under r 10(3)(b) and r 9(2)(a) she cannot lead the denial or rely on it.
Because she knows from the confession that he is about to give false evidence, r 10(4)
applies. His whole defence would be the false denial, so she cannot continue under
r 10(4)(b) without perpetuating the falsehood, and she must cease to act under r 10(4)(a).

Withdrawal is justified under r 26(5)(f) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner may
withdraw from representing a client in a case or matter if the withdrawal is necessary to
avoid a contravention by the legal practitioner of (i) the Act; (ii) these Rules or any
other subsidiary legislation made under the Act; (iii) any practice directions, guidance
notes and rulings issued under section 71(6) of the Act; (iv) any practice directions,
guidance notes and rulings (relating to professional practice, etiquette, conduct and
discipline) issued by the Council or the Society; or (v) if the legal practitioner is duly
authorised or registered to practise law in any state or territory other than Singapore, any
practice directions, guidance notes and rulings (relating to professional practice,
etiquette, conduct and discipline) issued by (A) the foreign authority having the function
conferred by law of authorising or registering persons to practise law in that state or
territory; or (B) any relevant professional body of that state or territory. Withdrawal is
necessary to avoid contravening r 9(2)(a), r 10(3) and r 14(4)(b) (head (ii)). Rule 26(6)
PCR states that where a legal practitioner withdraws from representing a client in a case or
matter, the legal practitioner must (a) take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to the
client, including, where the circumstances permit, (i) by giving reasonable notice of the
withdrawal to the client; (ii) by giving the client a reasonable amount of time to engage
another legal practitioner to take over the case or matter; and (iii) by cooperating with
the client's new legal practitioner; and (b) abide by the client's decision on whether to
appoint another legal practitioner, and who to appoint, to take over the case or matter, if
not completed. So Priya must give Arun reasonable notice and time to instruct new counsel,
cooperate with them, and abide by his choice. She must apply to the court to be discharged.
When doing so she may say only that she has professional reasons, and must not reveal the
confession (r 6(2) PCR).

In conclusion, Priya must not stand bail. She may continue to defend Arun after his
confession, but only by testing the prosecution's case. If Arun insists on testifying to a
false denial, she must cease to act without disclosing why. A breach of these rules may
amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due
cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 (set out in
part (a)).

## 2021 paper — Vega Bank, Gary's Mareva, and the engagement letter

Question: PYP21 p2–5 (Q1 = PS1 Q1, Q3 = PS3 Q1). Marks: Q1 40 (12 + 18 + 10), Q2 30,
Q3 30 (5 × 6). No official answer exists. Pre-writes used: 0-1, 0-5, 0-6, 0-7, 0-8, 0-10,
0-11, 0-13, 0-14, 0-3, 0-17, 0-18, 0-19, 0-21, 0-4, 0-32, 0-33, 0-15, 0-2, 0-31.

### Q1.1 Liability for the loan two years ago (12 marks)

Here, r 4 PCR applies, which sets out the principles that guide the interpretation of the
Rules. Rule 4(a) states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to the court, which
takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client, and r 4(b) states that the
legal practitioner's duty to the client is subject only to the duty to the court, and must at
all times be fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the Singapore
legal system and the legal profession in Singapore. Neither Andy nor Beth is likely to have
breached any rule in relation to the loan.

**Beth: competence and diligence.** The first issue is whether Beth should have discovered
that Delta Tech belonged to Xavier. She was not required to. Beth did not breach r 5(2)(c)
PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must act with reasonable diligence and
competence in the provision of services to the client. Rule 5(3) PCR states that the
circumstances, nature and terms of engagement of a legal practitioner by his or her client
are to be taken into account when considering whether the legal practitioner has complied
with paragraph (2). The test is what a reasonably competent solicitor would have done,
having regard to the normal standards adopted in practice, and what is required depends on
the facts. The lawyer must do more where there is reason to suspect special risks in the
transaction or where the client is vulnerable, and the extent of the duty varies with the
client's apparent familiarity with the transaction (*Law Society of Singapore v K Jayakumar
Naidu* [2012] 4 SLR 1232 at [85]–[91]). Vega Bank expressly instructed Beth to prepare the
loan documents only and not to carry out due diligence, to save fees. Under r 5(3) that
limited scope is part of the terms of engagement. Vega Bank is a sophisticated commercial
client, and nothing warned Beth of a special risk. Xavier's 60% interest was held
indirectly through several holding companies and intermediaries, so it would have taken
real investigation to uncover. Beth also ran a firm-wide conflict check naming the client
and the counterparty and received no responses. A reasonably competent solicitor working
within that limited scope would not have done more. Beth did not breach r 5(2)(c).

**Was there a conflict at the time?** The second issue is whether the firm was acting for
two parties with diverging interests (r 20 PCR), against a former client (r 21 PCR), or
against its own interests (r 22 PCR). It was not. Rule 20(2) PCR states that the
safeguards in r 20(3), (4) and (7) apply where (a) a legal practitioner or law practice
intends to act for 2 or more different parties to a matter or transaction; and (b) a
diversity of interests exists, or may reasonably be expected to exist, between those
parties. Delta Tech had its own lawyers, and Xavier was not a party to the loan and did not
instruct the firm on it, so the firm acted for only one party and limb (a) is not met.
Rule 21(2) PCR states that, subject to paragraphs (3), (4) and (5), a legal practitioner or
law practice must decline to represent, or must withdraw from representing, a client
(called the current client) in a matter if (a) the legal practitioner or the law practice
holds confidential information relating to a former client that is protected by rule 6;
(b) the current client has an interest that is, or may reasonably be expected to be,
adverse to an interest of the former client; and (c) that information may reasonably be
expected to be material to the representation of the current client in that matter.
Rule 21 does not arise, because Xavier was a current client, not a former client, and no
former client's confidential information was in play. Rule 6(2) PCR states that, subject to
paragraph (3), a legal practitioner must not knowingly disclose any information which
(a) is confidential to his or her client; and (b) is acquired by the legal practitioner
(whether from the client or from any other person) in the course of the legal
practitioner's engagement. Andy did not know of the loan or Delta Tech's link to Xavier.
Xavier gave him information only on a need-to-know basis, so there is nothing to suggest
that any confidential information of Xavier's reached Beth or was used for Vega Bank.
Rule 22(2) PCR states that, except as otherwise permitted by the rule, a legal practitioner
or law practice must not act for a client if there is, or may reasonably be expected to be,
a conflict between (a) the duty to serve the best interests of the client; and (b) the
interests of the legal practitioner or law practice. The firm had no interest of its own in
the loan. At most, the firm had a commercial conflict, meaning a business preference not to
act against a valued client's group. That is not a PCR conflict, which needs two clients in
the same matter (r 20(2)(a)), a former client's material confidential information
(r 21(2)), or an interest of the lawyer or firm itself (r 22(2)).

In conclusion, neither Andy nor Beth is liable for the loan transaction. The episode shows
the limits of a conflict check that names only the immediate parties, which is a point of
good practice rather than a breach.

### Q1.2 Andy's and Beth's proposals (18 marks)

The issue is how the firm should deal with Vega Bank's new enquiry while acting for Xavier.
Andy's proposal to decline Vega Bank is acceptable if it is done properly, and the firm
cannot give Vega Bank the real reason. Beth's proposal to act on both sides is not
workable.

**Is Vega Bank a current client?** The firm first needs to know whether declining is a
refusal of new instructions or a withdrawal from a continuing retainer. A retainer may be
implied where, on an objective consideration of all the circumstances, an intention to
enter into a solicitor-client relationship ought fairly and properly to be imputed to all
the parties, and the implication must be so clear that the solicitor ought to have
appreciated it. Circumstances pointing to an implied retainer include a party paying the
solicitor's fees and a party giving the solicitor instructions (*Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64], [66]). The loan retainer ended two
years ago. In the five-minute call, Beth only pointed the in-house counsel to existing terms
of the loan agreement and suggested a follow-up call. No fee was agreed and no substantive
advice was given, which leans against a fresh retainer, although the suggestion of a
further call with a litigator may have led Vega Bank to think the firm was taking the matter
on. Either way, the firm should act promptly. If a retainer exists, the firm may withdraw
under r 26(5)(a) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner may withdraw from representing
a client in a case or matter if the legal practitioner gives reasonable notice of the
withdrawal to the client. Rule 26(6) PCR then states that a legal practitioner who
withdraws must (a) take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to the client, including,
where the circumstances permit, (i) by giving reasonable notice of the withdrawal to the
client; (ii) by giving the client a reasonable amount of time to engage another legal
practitioner to take over the case or matter; and (iii) by cooperating with the client's
new legal practitioner; and (b) abide by the client's decision on whether to appoint another
legal practitioner, and who to appoint, to take over the case or matter, if not completed.
If there is no retainer, the firm should tell Vega Bank at once that it cannot act, so the
bank can instruct others.

**Andy's approach.** A firm is free to decline new instructions for commercial reasons,
including to preserve a long-standing client relationship. That is a commercial choice,
not a PCR conflict. There is also a real PCR reason for the firm not to act for Vega Bank.
Andy holds Xavier's confidential information about his asset-holding structures, which
were set up partly to protect his assets from claims. If Vega Bank seeks to enforce against
Delta Tech and its controllers, that information would be directly material. Xavier is a
current client whose interests are adverse to Vega Bank's. Rule 20(1)(b) PCR states the
guiding principle that a law practice owes duties of loyalty and confidentiality to each
client of the law practice, and must act prudently to avoid any compromise of the
lawyer-client relationship by reason of a conflict, or potential conflict, between the
interests of 2 or more clients, and r 6(2) PCR, set out in Q1.1, bars disclosure of
Xavier's information. In that respect, Andy's preference coincides with what the rules point
to. One caution applies: if Andy later advises Xavier on the loan, as he does in Q1.3, Vega
Bank becomes a former client. The firm then holds Vega Bank's confidential information,
namely the loan file and its recent enforcement enquiry, and that information is material
and adverse to Xavier. All three limbs of r 21(2) PCR, set out in Q1.1, would then be met,
and the firm would have to decline unless an exception applies. Rule 21(3) PCR states that
paragraph (2) does not prevent the firm from acting, or continuing to act, for the current
client, subject to any conditions agreed between the firm and the former client, if (a) the
firm has adequately advised the former client to obtain independent legal advice; and
(b) the former client gives the former client's informed consent in writing to the firm
acting, or continuing to act, for the current client. Rule 21(4) PCR states that where the
requirements in paragraph (3)(a) and (b) are not met despite reasonable efforts by the firm
to meet them, paragraph (2) does not prevent the firm from acting, or continuing to act, if
(a) there are adequate safeguards in place to protect the former client's confidential
information; and (b) the firm has made reasonable efforts to notify the former client (i) of
those safeguards; and (ii) that the firm will act, or continue to act, for the current
client.

**Beth's need to give a reason.** Beth would breach r 6(2) PCR if she gave the reason. The
fact that Andy acts for Xavier, and anything about Xavier's interest in Delta Tech, is
confidential to Xavier and was acquired in the course of the firm's engagement. The ACRA
results are public, but linking them to the firm's client is not. Rule 6(3) PCR states that
a legal practitioner may disclose such information if (a) the client authorises the
disclosure; (b) the legal practitioner is permitted or is required by law, by an order of
court, or by a tribunal to make the disclosure; (c) the legal practitioner discloses the
information in confidence, for the sole purpose of obtaining advice in connection with the
legal practitioner's legal or ethical obligations; (d) the legal practitioner discloses the
information in confidence to a provider or broker of the legal practitioner's professional
indemnity insurance, in connection with any claim or potential claim, or any complaint or
potential complaint, against the legal practitioner; or (e) the legal practitioner
discloses the information for the sole purpose of responding to or defending any charge or
complaint relating to the legal practitioner's conduct. Only r 6(3)(a) could help, and
Xavier has not authorised disclosure. Beth may only tell Vega Bank that the firm is unable
to act. She must not explain why without Xavier's consent.

**Beth's alternative: Andy for Xavier, Beth and Clara for Vega Bank.** Here, r 20(2) PCR,
set out in Q1.1, applies, because the firm would intend to act for two parties to the same
matter between whom a diversity of interests plainly exists. Vega Bank wants to enforce
against Delta Tech, and Xavier, who controls it, wants to protect his businesses and assets.
Rule 20(3) PCR states that, before accepting any instructions from any relevant party, the
firm (a) must communicate directly with each relevant party (i) to explain to that party
(A) how the interests of all or any of the relevant parties diverge or may diverge; (B) how
the firm may be prevented from disclosing to a relevant party information obtained from
another relevant party, despite the relevance of the information to the matter; and (C) how
the firm may be prevented from giving to a relevant party any advice that is prejudicial to
another relevant party; (ii) to inform that party that the firm must cease to act if, in the
course of the retainer, it has difficulty in advising on and dealing with the relevant
parties' divergent interests competently, evenly and consistently; (iii) to receive and
deal with any queries which that party may have on the matter, or on the risks of the
parties being jointly represented by the firm; and (iv) to ascertain precisely the
intentions of that party; (b) in the case of a transaction that is ostensibly or
potentially disadvantageous to a particular party, must also communicate directly with that
party (i) to explain its position before the transaction, and how its position will or may
be altered to its detriment by the transaction; (ii) to verify whether any instructions
purportedly given on its behalf do in fact reflect its intentions; and (iii) to remove any
doubt as to whether it may have been misled by, or may be acting under the undue influence
of, another person; (c) must advise each relevant party to obtain independent legal advice;
(d) if a particular relevant party does not obtain independent legal advice, must obtain a
written confirmation from that party, or maintain a written record, that it declines to do
so; and (e) must obtain each relevant party's informed consent in writing to the firm acting
for all relevant parties, despite their divergent interests. Rule 20(4) PCR states that the
firm (a) must throughout the retainer (i) continue to be vigilant of any conflict or
potential conflict between the interests of any of the relevant parties; and (ii) inform
each relevant party of any conflict or potential conflict that arises or may arise; and
(b) must cease to act if, in the course of the retainer, it has difficulty in advising on
and dealing with the relevant parties' divergent interests competently, evenly and
consistently. Rule 20(6) PCR imposes the same duties where the diversity of interests arises
during the retainer. Consent must be genuinely informed (*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v
Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36 at [63]–[80]; *Kuek Siang Wei v Kuek Siew Chew*
[2015] 5 SLR 357, on the steps required to obtain informed consent). The standard of skill
and care owed to each of several clients must be at least equivalent to that owed to a
single client, the solicitor cannot prefer the interests of one client to the others, and a
solicitor who, objectively speaking, has difficulty advising multiple clients competently,
evenly and consistently ought to discharge himself (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong Tan & Molly
Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). A solicitor acting for more than one client must
identify and disclose any actual or potential conflict, obtain informed consent from all
clients, and remain impartial (*Law Society of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram* [2009]
4 SLR(R) 674 at [34]–[53]). If the firm then has to cease to act under r 20(4)(b) or
r 20(6)(b), r 20(7) PCR states that it may continue to act for one relevant party only if
(a) it ceases to act for all other relevant parties whose interests diverge from that
party's; and (b) all of those other relevant parties give their informed consent in writing
for the firm to continue to act. So a firm that mismanages the conflict risks losing both
clients.

Here the firm could not even meet r 20(3)(a)(i)(A), because explaining how the interests
diverge would reveal each client's confidential position to the other, contrary to r 6(2).
Without that explanation, no consent under r 20(3)(e) could be informed. An information
barrier would not cure the problem. The adequacy of the steps taken to prevent disclosure
depends on factors such as (1) the nature of the work done for the former client, (2) the
timing of the creation of the information barrier, (3) the size of the law firm, (4) the
physical locations of departments within the firm, and (5) the number and seniority of
tainted lawyers (*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at
[68]). On factor (5), Andy is a partner who already holds exactly the asset-protection
information Vega Bank would want, and Clara has already researched the corporate chain. On
factor (2), any barrier would be set up late, around people who are already tainted. On
factor (1), this is a lender pursuing a borrower controlled by the firm's other client, so
under r 20(4)(b) the firm could not act evenly for both.

In conclusion, the firm should decline Vega Bank's instructions promptly, without giving
the reason, and should not adopt Beth's split. If Andy then advises Xavier on the loan, the
firm must comply with r 21(3) or (4) to protect Vega Bank's information. That means a
documented barrier around Beth and Clara, and notifying Vega Bank.

### Q1.3 The deed signed by Xavier (10 marks)

The issue is whether Andy acted properly in obtaining Xavier's signature to a deed waiving
all claims against the firm. He did not. He breached r 22 and r 5(2)(a)–(b) PCR, and his
dishonesty makes this grossly improper conduct.

**Own interest.** Andy breached r 22(2) PCR, set out in Q1.1, because the deed put his and
the firm's interest in avoiding liability directly against Xavier's interest in keeping his
rights. Rule 22(3) PCR states that where a legal practitioner, any immediate family member
of the legal practitioner, or the law practice in which the legal practitioner practises
has an interest in any matter entrusted to the legal practitioner by a client, (a) in any
case where the interest is adverse to the client's interests, the legal practitioner must
withdraw from representing the client, unless (i) the legal practitioner makes a full and
frank disclosure of the adverse interest to the client; (ii) the legal practitioner advises
the client to obtain independent legal advice; (iii) if the client does not obtain
independent legal advice, the legal practitioner ensures that the client is not under an
impression that the legal practitioner is protecting the client's interests; and
(iv) despite sub-paragraphs (i) and (ii), the client gives the client's informed consent in
writing to the legal practitioner acting, or continuing to act, on the client's behalf; or
(b) in any other case, the legal practitioner must withdraw unless (i) the legal
practitioner makes a full and frank disclosure of the interest to the client; and
(ii) despite that disclosure, the client gives informed consent in writing. In *Law Society
of Singapore v Govindan Balan Nair* [2020] SGHC 174, a lawyer who hid his own negligence and
kept acting preferred his own interests to the client's. A deed releasing all claims against
the firm benefits only Andy and the firm. It takes away rights Xavier might have and is
plainly adverse to him, so r 22(3)(a) applies. Andy failed every limb. On limb (i), he
disclosed nothing. On limb (ii), he advised no independent advice. On limb (iii), he dealt
through Xavier's personal assistant, so Xavier signed without any explanation at all and
had no reason to think his interests were not being protected. On limb (iv), there was no
informed consent.

**Honesty.** Andy also breached r 5(2)(a) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must
be honest in all the legal practitioner's dealings with his or her client, and r 5(2)(b)
PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must, when advising the client, inform the
client of all information known to the legal practitioner that may reasonably affect the
interests of the client in the matter, other than (i) any information that the legal
practitioner is precluded, by any overriding duty of confidentiality, from disclosing to
the client; and (ii) any information that the client has agreed in writing need not be
disclosed to the client. Andy gave a false reason, "required routine reporting" to the
firm's insurers, which he knew did not exist, to get the deed signed. The true purpose and
effect of the deed plainly affected Xavier's interests, and neither exception in r 5(2)(b)
covers it. Dishonesty that violates the trust inherent in the solicitor-client relationship
will almost invariably lead to striking off (*Law Society of Singapore v Udeh Kumar s/o
Sethuraju* [2017] SGHC 141 at [25]–[30]; *Law Society of Singapore v Chia Choon Yang*
[2018] SGHC 174 at [44]–[55]). Andy's motive, avoiding Xavier's displeasure about Vega
Bank's approach, is no excuse. Vega Bank's approach is in any case confidential to Vega
Bank (r 6(2) PCR), so the proper course was to say nothing about it, not to extract a
release.

The deed is also unlikely to be effective. It was procured by misrepresentation. PD 7.1.2
states that it is not acceptable for law practices and advocates and solicitors to attempt
to exclude by contract all liability to their clients, although the Council has no
objection to their seeking to limit liability, provided the limitation is not below the
minimum level of cover required by the Legal Profession (Professional Indemnity Insurance)
Rules. It adds that liability for fraud or reckless disregard of professional obligations
cannot be limited (para (a)); that the courts will not enforce in the lawyer's favour an
unfair agreement with the client (para (b)); and that any limitation must be brought
clearly to the attention of the client and be understood and accepted by the client, with
the acceptance evidenced in or confirmed by writing. A deed waiving all claims is a total
exclusion, it was never explained to Xavier, and it was obtained by deceit.

In conclusion, Andy breached r 22(2)–(3)(a) and r 5(2)(a)–(b). He should tell Xavier the
truth and treat the deed as void. Section 83(2)(b) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states
that due cause for discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor has been
guilty of fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of his or her professional
duty, or guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the Professional
Conduct Council under section 71 or by the Council under the Act as amounts to improper
conduct or practice as an advocate and solicitor. Because of the deceit and self-interest,
Andy's conduct is likely grossly improper conduct under s 83(2)(b), with a real risk of
striking off.

### Q2 Fiona and the ex parte worldwide Mareva (30 marks)

Here, r 4(a) PCR applies, which states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to
the court, which takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client.
Section 82(1) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that the following persons are
officers of the Supreme Court: (a) a person duly admitted as a lawyer (NP) or an advocate
and solicitor; (b) a Judicial Service Officer; (c) a Legal Service Officer; and (d) a PD
Officer. Fiona breached her duty to the court at every stage: in taking on and running the
application, in the affidavit, and in her answer to the judge.

**Failing to advise Gary.** The first issue is whether Fiona should have told Gary her
views on disclosure and on the weakness of his case. She should have. Fiona breached
r 5(2)(b) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must, when advising the client,
inform the client of all information known to the legal practitioner that may reasonably
affect the interests of the client in the matter, other than (i) any information that the
legal practitioner is precluded, by any overriding duty of confidentiality, from disclosing
to the client; and (ii) any information that the client has agreed in writing need not be
disclosed to the client. She also breached r 17(2)(f) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner must advise his or her client on the relevant legal issues in a matter, to
enable the client to make an informed decision about how to act in the matter, and
r 10(2) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner whose client is a party to any
proceedings before a court or tribunal must inform the client of the client's
responsibilities to the court or tribunal, and to every other party to those proceedings,
including the client's duties (a) to be truthful towards the court or tribunal; and (b) to
comply with every legal requirement concerning the conduct and presentation of the client's
case. A lawyer who fails to explain material risks breaches this duty (*Su Ah Tee v
Allister Lim and Thrumurgam* [2014] SGHC 159). Fiona believed that Singapore law required
disclosure of the UK and HK proceedings, and she saw no evidence of a real risk of
dissipation. Both points reasonably affected Gary's interests, and neither exception in
r 5(2)(b) applies. She kept both points to herself because Gary "would not like to hear
this", so she gave no advice on the legal issues and did not tell Gary of his duty under
r 10(2)(b) to comply with the disclosure requirements. Fear of displeasing a client never
displaces the duty to advise. Fiona breached r 5(2)(b), r 17(2)(f) and r 10(2).

**Bringing an unmeritorious application for a collateral purpose.** The second issue is
whether Fiona should have filed the application at all. She should not have. Rule 9(1) PCR
sets out the guiding principles. Rule 9(1)(a) states that a legal practitioner has a duty to
assist in the administration of justice, and must act honourably in the interests of the
administration of justice, and r 9(1)(e) states that a legal practitioner must, in any
proceedings before a court or tribunal, conduct the case in a manner which maintains the
fairness, integrity and efficiency of those proceedings and which is consistent with due
process. Rule 10(1)(c) PCR states the principle that a legal practitioner must not engage in
any conduct which would be unlawful, unethical or otherwise improper, whether or not such
conduct would promote the cause of his or her client. Fiona breached r 9(2)(e) PCR, which
states that, when conducting proceedings, a legal practitioner must not allow the legal
practitioner's personal opinion to affect (i) the legal practitioner's professional
assessment of the facts or the law; or (ii) the legal practitioner's duty to the court or
tribunal. She also breached r 9(2)(h)(ii) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must
not draft any originating process, pleading, affidavit, witness statement or notice or
grounds of appeal containing any contention which the legal practitioner does not consider
to be reasonably and properly arguable. All solicitors, as officers of the court, have an
absolute and overriding duty first and foremost to the court to serve the public interest by
ensuring the proper and efficient administration of justice (*Public Trustee v By Products
Traders Pte Ltd* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 449 at [26]–[30], [35]). All solicitors have an obligation
to carefully assess the merits of their clients' cases before engaging in court
proceedings, a duty owed both to the client and to the court (*Zhou Tong v Public
Prosecutor* [2010] 4 SLR 534 at [19]). Counsel are not the mere mouthpieces of their
clients, and must counsel their clients on what is permissible and what is not (*BOI v BOJ*
[2018] SGCA 61 at [3]–[4]). Filing an unmeritorious application may amount to professional
misconduct, and much depends on how patently unmeritorious it is and whether counsel can
offer a satisfactory account for filing it (*Miya Manik v Public Prosecutor* [2021] SGCA 90
at [82]). Gary said his sole objective was to pressure ICPL into selling, and that he did
not care if the application failed. That is a collateral purpose. Fiona saw no evidence of
dissipation, which is an essential element of a Mareva injunction, yet she filed an
affidavit asserting Gary's belief that ICPL was disposing of its assets worldwide. She could
not have considered that contention reasonably and properly arguable, so r 9(2)(h)(ii) is
breached. Her admiration for Gary as a philanthropist "who could do no wrong" is exactly the
personal opinion that r 9(2)(e)(i) and (ii) forbid to affect her assessment of the facts and
her duty to the court. Using the court's process as leverage rather than to obtain relief
is not conduct that maintains the integrity of the proceedings, and Fiona can offer no
satisfactory account for filing it. Fiona breached r 9(2)(e) and r 9(2)(h)(ii), contrary to
the principles in r 9(1)(a), r 9(1)(e) and r 10(1)(c).

**Non-disclosure on a without-notice application.** The third issue is whether Fiona
breached the duty of full and frank disclosure. She did. Fiona breached r 9(3)(b) PCR, which
states that, when conducting proceedings, a legal practitioner must disclose to the court or
tribunal, and to every other person involved in or associated with those proceedings,
(i) every fact, item of evidence, item of information and other matter which the legal
practitioner is required by law to disclose in those proceedings to the court or tribunal
and to that other person, respectively; and (ii) any procedural irregularity of which the
legal practitioner is aware. She also breached r 10(3) PCR, which states that, to the extent
that a legal practitioner is able, the legal practitioner must prevent his or her client
from, must not be a party to, and must not assist the client in, doing either or both of
the following: (a) suppressing evidence; (b) giving false evidence or false information to
a court or tribunal. An applicant without notice must make full and frank disclosure of all material facts to the court; an inadvertent or negligent failure to do so breaches that duty, but r 10(3)(a) needs more (*Law Society of Singapore v de Souza Christopher James* [2023] SGHC 318 at [88]). Suppressing evidence requires (1) a wrongful
non-disclosure, which means identifying the litigant's duty to disclose, having regard to
the nature and purpose of the proceedings and the relevant law; and (2) a deliberate
non-disclosure, made with a subjective intention to prevent the court or the other party from learning of the information (*de Souza* at [163]–[165], per Ramesh JAD; and for the majority at [75]–[76], [83]). Making some disclosure does not discharge the
duty of candour if the nature and purpose of the proceedings call for more
(*Attorney-General v Shahira Banu d/o Khaja Moinudeen* [2024] SGHC 111 at [32]–[37]). On
limb (1), identical proceedings in the UK and HK, and failed freezing applications there,
are plainly material to whether a worldwide freezing order should be granted in Singapore,
so Singapore law required their disclosure under r 9(3)(b)(i). On limb (2), unlike in *de Souza*, where the lawyer had no subjective intention to suppress (at [91], [126]), Fiona omitted them
deliberately, on Gary's instruction, despite believing disclosure was required. A client
cannot instruct a lawyer to breach the duty of disclosure. Gary's reliance on foreign
lawyers' views about foreign law is irrelevant to a Singapore court's disclosure rules.
Fiona should have advised disclosure and, if Gary refused, declined to file. She breached
r 9(3)(b) and r 10(3)(a).

**The answer to the judge.** The fourth issue is whether Fiona misled the court. She did.
Fiona breached r 9(2)(a) PCR, which states that, when conducting proceedings, a legal
practitioner must not knowingly mislead or attempt to mislead in any way, whether by
fabricating facts or evidence, by including a statement of fact known to be false, or
otherwise, (i) the court or tribunal; (ii) any staff of the court or tribunal; (iii) any
other legal practitioner; or (iv) any witness in, or any other person involved in or
associated with, those proceedings. Solicitors should never mislead the court either
actively or passively, nor consciously furnish to the court erroneous or incomplete
information (*Public Trustee* at [35]). To make out a breach of the duty not to mislead the
court, it must be shown that a false statement was made to the court by a solicitor
(a) knowing it is false; (b) without belief in its truth; or (c) recklessly, without caring
whether it is true or false (*Law Society of Singapore v Seah Zhen Wei Paul* [2024] SGHC 224
at [69]). A statement that implies something the lawyer knows to be untrue is dishonest
(*Loh Der Ming Andrew v Koh Tien Hua* [2022] SGHC 84 at [64]–[66]). The judge asked
directly why no remedies had been sought in the UK or HK. Fiona answered that Singapore was
more appropriate. That implied no such attempts had been made, when she knew they had been
made and had failed, so the statement was made knowing it was false and limb (a) of *Seah
Zhen Wei Paul* is met. A direct question from the court makes the half-truth more serious,
not less.

**What Fiona must do now.** Judgment is reserved, so the court can still be put right.
Fiona must advise Gary that the UK and HK proceedings, and the failed applications, must be
disclosed to the court and ICPL. If Gary agrees, she must disclose them and correct her
answer before judgment. If he refuses, she cannot continue without perpetuating the
falsehood. Rule 10(4) PCR states that where a legal practitioner knows that his or her
client is about to give, or has given, false evidence or false information to a court or
tribunal, the legal practitioner must (a) cease to act for the client; or (b) if the legal
practitioner continues to act for the client, conduct the client's case in a manner that
does not perpetuate the falsehood. Applying r 10(4) by analogy, Fiona must cease to act.
Rule 26(5)(f) PCR permits that, as it states that a legal practitioner may withdraw if the
withdrawal is necessary to avoid a contravention by the legal practitioner of (i) the Act;
(ii) these Rules or any other subsidiary legislation made under the Act; (iii) any practice
directions, guidance notes and rulings issued under section 71(6) of the Act; (iv) any
practice directions, guidance notes and rulings (relating to professional practice,
etiquette, conduct and discipline) issued by the Council or the Society; or (v) where the
legal practitioner is also authorised to practise in another state or territory, the
practice directions, guidance notes and rulings of that jurisdiction's authorising
authority or professional body. On withdrawing she must not reveal Gary's confidences
(r 6(2) PCR), except where she is permitted or required by law, by an order of court, or by
a tribunal to make the disclosure (r 6(3)(b) PCR).

In conclusion, Fiona breached r 5(2)(b), r 9(2)(a), (e) and (h), r 9(3)(b), r 10(2) and
(3), and her duty as an officer of the court. Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act 1966
states that due cause for discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor
(b) has been guilty of fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of his or her
professional duty, or guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the
Professional Conduct Council under section 71 or by the Council under the Act as amounts to
improper conduct or practice as an advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty of such
misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court or as a
member of an honourable profession. Fiona's breaches are due cause for discipline under
s 83(2)(b) and (h). The deliberate misleading of the court is likely to be treated as
grossly improper conduct.

### Q3 Terms of engagement (5 × 6 marks)

Here, r 4 PCR applies. Rule 4(a) states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to
the court, which takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client, and
r 4(b) states that the duty to the client is subject only to the duty to the court, and must
at all times be fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the
Singapore legal system and the legal profession in Singapore. Each of the five terms is
objectionable.

**3.1 The $4 million agreed fee.** The issue is whether a $4 million fee to defend a
$6 million goods claim, payable even on early settlement, is overcharging. It likely is.
Here, r 17(7) PCR applies, which states that a legal practitioner must not charge any fee or
disbursements, or render a bill (whether or not subject to assessment) for an amount, which
constitutes overcharging, even if there is a fee agreement that permits the charging of the
fee, disbursements or amount. Rule 17(8) PCR states that there is overcharging if a
reasonable legal practitioner cannot in good faith charge the fee, disbursements or amount,
taking into account all of the following matters: (a) the legal practitioner's standing and
experience; (b) the nature of the legal work concerned; (c) the time necessary to undertake
the legal work; (d) the instructions and requirements of the client concerned; and (e) any
other relevant circumstances. Section 111(1) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that,
subject to any other written law, a solicitor may make an agreement in writing with any
client respecting the amount and manner of payment for the whole or any part of the costs
in respect of contentious business done or to be done, either by a gross sum or otherwise,
and at either the same rate as or a greater or a lesser rate than that at which the
solicitor would otherwise be entitled to be remunerated, and s 111(2) states that every such
agreement must be signed by the client and is subject to the provisions and conditions in
that Part. Section 113(4) LPA states that if the terms of the agreement are deemed by the
court or Judge to be unfair or unreasonable, the agreement may be declared void. PD 5.2.2
states that s 111 does not give solicitors a carte blanche to agree to an unreasonable fee,
that overcharging a client, whether in a bill of costs or otherwise, may amount to
professional misconduct, and that a lawyer is not in all cases entitled to keep fees
collected as a non-refundable deposit or retainer irrespective of the amount of work done,
even if the client agrees. The extent and disproportion of an overcharge go to its gravity
(*Law Society of Singapore v Syn Kok Kay* [2023] SGHC 7). On factor (b), this appears to be
a straightforward claim for the price of goods. On factor (c), the fee is payable in full
even if the suit settles early, when little work has been done. On factor (e), the fee is
two-thirds of the sum claimed. Nothing on factor (a) or (d), such as exceptional seniority or
unusual client requirements, is said to justify it. No reasonable lawyer could charge this in
good faith. The term risks breaching r 17(7), and the agreement risks being declared void
under s 113(4). It should be tied to the work done, with staged fees.

**3.2 The $500,000 bonus.** The issue is whether the extra fee if the suit settles below
$500,000 is a prohibited contingency fee. It is. Here, s 107(1)(b) of the Legal Profession
Act 1966 applies, which states that a solicitor must not enter into any agreement by which
he or she is retained or employed to prosecute any suit or action or other contentious
proceeding which stipulates for or contemplates payment only in the event of success in
that suit, action or proceeding. Rule 18 PCR states that a legal practitioner or law
practice must not enter into any negotiations with a client (a) for an interest in the
subject matter of litigation or of any other contentious proceedings; or (b) except to the
extent permitted by any applicable scale of costs, for remuneration proportionate to the
amount which may be recovered by the client in the proceedings. The prohibition exists
because one of the key elements in effectively representing a client's interest is the
lawyer's ability to maintain a sufficient sense of detachment to discharge his duty to the
court, and such arrangements would tempt the lawyer to subvert the course of justice (*Law
Society of Singapore v Kurubalan s/o Manickam Rengaraju* [2013] 4 SLR 91 at [42], [45]). The
additional $500,000 is payable only if counsel achieves a particular result, a settlement
below $500,000. That part of the fee is therefore payable only in the event of success, and
the fixed base fee does not change that.

The only lawful way to agree an extra fee on success is a conditional fee agreement with an
uplift fee under Part 8A of the Act. Section 107(3AA) LPA states that s 107 does not prevent
a solicitor from entering into a conditional fee agreement that complies with Part 8A, and
r 18A(1) PCR states that rules 17 and 18 do not prevent a legal practitioner from (a)
entering into negotiations for, or entering into, a conditional fee agreement that complies
with Part 8A of the Act; or (b) charging remuneration payable only in circumstances
specified in such an agreement. Section 115A(1) LPA defines a "conditional fee agreement" as
an agreement relating to the whole or any part of the remuneration and costs in respect of
contentious proceedings, which provides for the remuneration and costs or any part of them
to be payable only in specified circumstances, and may provide for an uplift fee. It defines
an "uplift fee" as the remuneration or costs which the agreement provides are payable in
specified circumstances which are higher than the remuneration or costs that would otherwise
be payable if there were no conditional fee agreement. GN 5.6.1 para 6 says that contingency
fee agreements, under which the lawyer takes a percentage of the sum recovered, remain
prohibited, and its second illustration treats exactly this structure, a base fee plus an
uplift on success, as a conditional fee agreement. But GN 5.6.1 para 8 states that
s 115A(1) limits the use of conditional fee agreements to specified categories of
"prescribed proceedings", which in broad terms include (a) international and domestic
arbitration proceedings; (b) certain proceedings in the Singapore International Commercial
Court; and (c) court and mediation proceedings related to the above. ABC's suit is an
ordinary court action, so the exception does not apply, and in any case the regime did not
exist when the 2021 paper was set. Rule 18(b) is a less natural fit, because the client here
is a defendant paying out, not recovering; s 107(1)(b) does the work. The term breaches
s 107(1)(b) and should be removed.

**3.3 Termination "for any reason" at any stage.** The issue is whether counsel may reserve
an unrestricted right to terminate with immediate effect. He may not. Here, r 26(2) PCR
applies, which states that, except as otherwise provided in the rule, a legal practitioner
must complete the work which he or she agreed to undertake pursuant to the client's
instructions. Rule 26(5) PCR states that a legal practitioner may withdraw from representing
a client in a case or matter only if (a) the legal practitioner gives reasonable notice of
the withdrawal to the client; (b) the withdrawal will not cause any significant harm to the
client's interests, the client is fully informed of the consequences of the withdrawal, and
the client voluntarily assents to the withdrawal; (c) the legal practitioner reasonably
believes that continued engagement is likely to have a serious adverse effect on the legal
practitioner's health; (d) the client (i) fails to pay the legal practitioner's invoice
within the stipulated period; or (ii) otherwise breaches an agreement with the legal
practitioner regarding (A) fees or expenses to be paid by the client; or (B) the client's
conduct; (e) the client makes material misrepresentations about the facts of the case or
matter to the legal practitioner; (f) the withdrawal is necessary to avoid a contravention
by the legal practitioner of the Act, the Rules or other subsidiary legislation, or the
practice directions, guidance notes and rulings issued under s 71(6) of the Act or by the
Council or the Society (or, for a lawyer authorised elsewhere, by that jurisdiction's
authorities); (g) there is a serious loss of confidence between the legal practitioner and
the client; or (h) any other good cause exists. Rule 26(6) PCR states that a legal
practitioner who withdraws must (a) take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to the
client, including, where the circumstances permit, (i) by giving reasonable notice of the
withdrawal to the client; (ii) by giving the client a reasonable amount of time to engage
another legal practitioner to take over the case or matter; and (iii) by cooperating with
the client's new legal practitioner; and (b) abide by the client's decision on whether to
appoint another legal practitioner, and who to appoint. PD 7.4.2 states that difficulty in
terminating a retainer may be averted by a reservation in the warrant to act or letter of
engagement to the effect that the legal practitioner may at any time discharge himself
based on the grounds set out in r 26(5), while observing the obligation in r 26(6) to take
reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to the client. It warns that, without a suitable
reservation, a lawyer who obtains his discharge may be exposed to a claim for damages if the
withdrawal leads to the dismissal of the client's claim or judgment against the client when
there is a valid defence. A clause allowing termination "with immediate effect for any reason
and at any stage" goes beyond every r 26(5) ground, including "any other good cause" in
r 26(5)(h), and would permit withdrawal on the eve of trial with no notice. That contradicts
r 26(5)(a) and r 26(6)(a)(i)–(ii). The clause should be limited to the r 26(5) grounds with
reasonable notice, as PD 7.4.2 recommends.

**3.4 The $50,000 liability cap.** The issue is whether counsel may exclude or cap liability
as drafted. He may not. Here, PD 7.1.2 applies, which states that it is not acceptable for
law practices and advocates and solicitors to attempt to exclude by contract all liability to
their clients, although they may seek to limit liability provided the limitation is not
below the minimum level of cover required by the Legal Profession (Professional Indemnity
Insurance) Rules. That minimum is $1 million for each and every claim for a lawyer in a law
firm or a one-director law corporation, and $2 million for a lawyer in any other law
corporation or in a limited liability law partnership. PD 7.1.2 adds that (a) liability for
fraud or reckless disregard of professional obligations cannot be limited; (b) the courts
will not enforce in the lawyer's favour an unfair agreement with the client; and (c) under
s 112(5) LPA such provisions in a contentious-business costs agreement are void; and that
any limitation must be brought clearly to the client's attention, be understood and accepted
by the client, and the acceptance be evidenced in or confirmed by writing. Section 112(5) of
the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that a provision in an agreement under s 111 that the
solicitor (a) is not liable for negligence; or (b) is relieved from any responsibility to
which the solicitor would otherwise be subject as a solicitor, is wholly void. This is an
engagement letter for a suit, so s 112(5) applies. The cap purports to cover fraud and breach
of professional obligations, contrary to PD 7.1.2 para (a), and relieves counsel of
responsibility, contrary to s 112(5)(b). At $50,000 it is far below the $1 million or
$2 million minimum cover. The clause fails on every ground and is unenforceable. Offering it
breaches PD 7.1.2.

**3.5 Discount for shares and a favourable review.** The issue is whether a 5% discount for
promoting the firm online is permissible. It is not. Counsel would breach r 39(1) PCR, which
states that a legal practitioner or law practice must not tout for business or do anything
which is likely to lead to a reasonable inference that the thing was done for the purpose of
touting. Section 83(2)(d) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that due cause for
discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor has tendered or given or
consented to retention, out of any fee payable to him or her for his or her services, of any
gratification for having procured the employment in any legal business of himself or
herself or of any other advocate and solicitor. Rule 44(1) PCR states that a legal
practitioner must not publicise the practice in a manner which (a) is likely to diminish
public confidence in the legal profession or to otherwise bring the legal profession into
disrepute; (b) may reasonably be regarded as being misleading, deceptive, inaccurate, false
or unbefitting the dignity of the legal profession; or (c) the Council may determine to be an
undesirable manner of publicising the practice. Rule 44(2) PCR states that publicity is
misleading, deceptive, inaccurate or false if (a) it contains a material misrepresentation;
(b) it omits to state a material fact; (c) it contains any information which cannot be
verified; or (d) it is likely to create an unjustified expectation about the results that
can be achieved. A fee discount is consideration out of the lawyer's fees, given in return
for promoting the firm to obtain further work, which is a gratification retained out of fees
under s 83(2)(d) and leads to a reasonable inference of touting under r 39(1). A
"favourable" review bought with a discount is also misleading publicity under r 44(1)(b),
because it omits the material fact that it was paid for (r 44(2)(b)) and its praise cannot
be verified as genuine (r 44(2)(c)). The term breaches r 39 and r 44 and s 83(2)(d), and
should be removed.

Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that due cause for discipline may be
shown by proof that an advocate and solicitor (b) has been guilty of fraudulent or grossly
improper conduct in the discharge of his or her professional duty, or guilty of such a
breach of any usage or rule of conduct made by the Professional Conduct Council under
section 71 or by the Council under the Act as amounts to improper conduct or practice as an
advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty of such misconduct unbefitting an advocate and
solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court or as a member of an honourable profession.
Offering these terms may amount to improper conduct or misconduct unbefitting an advocate and
solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under s 83(2)(b) and (h).

## 2022 Section B — Luna, Marci and the Settlement Agreement

Question: PYP22 p2–3 (= PS2 Q2). Marks: (a) 12, (b) 14, (c) 14. Section A was 20 MCQs set
on Examplify. Their text isn't in the PDF, so there is nothing to answer, and the 2026
paper has no MCQ section anyway. No official answer exists. Pre-writes used: 0-1, 0-9,
0-25, 0-22, 0-13, 0-23, 0-12, 0-24, 0-16, 0-4.

### (a) Can Luna and Marci act for Davion in the New Suit? (12 marks)

Here, r 4 PCR applies. Rule 4(a) states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to
the court, which takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client, and
r 4(b) states that the duty to the client is subject only to the duty to the court, and must
at all times be fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the
Singapore legal system and the legal profession in Singapore. Neither Luna nor Marci can
act, because both will be material witnesses on contested issues.

**Was Ricky also a client?** It is worth ruling out the absolute bar first. Rule 20(8) PCR
states that where a legal practitioner or law practice has prepared a document on behalf of
2 or more parties concerning their rights and obligations, and 2 or more of those parties
become involved in a dispute arising from the document, the legal practitioner or law
practice must not represent any disputing party in the dispute. A retainer may be implied
where, on an objective consideration of all the circumstances, an intention to enter into a
solicitor-client relationship ought fairly and properly to be imputed to all the parties,
and the implication must be so clear that the solicitor ought to have appreciated it (*Law Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64], [66]).
Luna told Ricky she represented Davion, which both sides accept. The agreement recites that
Ricky had the opportunity to take independent advice. So Mid Law probably prepared the
agreement on behalf of Davion alone, and r 20(8) does not apply. However, Ricky's pleaded
Representations, that he did not need a lawyer and that Luna could answer his questions,
would, if proved, breach r 8(2) PCR. Rule 8(2) states that a legal practitioner, when
dealing on behalf of a client with any person who is not represented by another legal
practitioner, (a) must decline to give to the person any legal advice (other than advice to
obtain independent legal advice), if the legal practitioner knows or ought reasonably to know
that the interests of the person are adverse, or potentially adverse, to the interests of the
client; and (b) must take reasonable steps to ensure that the person is not under the
impression that the person's interests are protected by the legal practitioner. Where a
lawyer acts for one party and other parties may reasonably believe the lawyer is advising or
protecting their interests, the lawyer must clearly disabuse them of that impression, and if
their interests are adverse or potentially adverse, must decline to advise them and should
advise them to seek independent legal advice (*Law Society of Singapore v Ganesan Krishnan*
[2003] 2 SLR(R) 251). Ricky's interests as the counterparty were adverse to Davion's. Telling
him he did not need a lawyer and offering to answer his questions would breach r 8(2)(a),
and would leave him thinking his interests were protected, contrary to r 8(2)(b). A recital
about independent advice does not cure oral assurances.

**Lawyer as witness.** The issue is whether Luna and Marci will be required to give
evidence material to a contested issue. They will, so they must not act. Here, r 11(3) PCR
applies, which states that in any case where it is known or it appears that a legal
practitioner will be required to give evidence which is material to the determination of
any contested issue before a court or tribunal, (a) the legal practitioner (i) must not
accept instructions from any party to that case; and (ii) must, if the legal practitioner
was acting for any party to that case, discharge himself or herself, or, where the legal
practitioner has represented that party in any proceedings relating to that case, apply to
be discharged, from acting further for that party; but (b) the law practice in which the
legal practitioner practises, or a member of that law practice, may represent or continue
to represent any party to that case, unless doing so would prejudice the administration of
justice. PD 1.7.1 states that a legal practitioner shall not represent any party in any case
in which he or she has reason to believe that he or she will be a witness in respect of a
material and disputed question of fact, and if while appearing in a case it becomes apparent
that he or she will be such a witness, he or she should discharge himself or herself, taking
all reasonable steps to ensure that he or she does not jeopardise the client's interests. It
adds that a legal practitioner shall not appear before an appellate tribunal if he or she
was a witness on a material and disputed question of fact in the court below, and that
nothing in the PD prevents a legal practitioner from swearing an affidavit as to formal or
undisputed facts. Rule 11(1) PCR states the principle that a legal practitioner's duty to
advance the client's interests, and to present the client's best possible case, is subject
to the legal practitioner's duties as an officer of the court and to assist in the
administration of justice, so the roles of witness and advocate should not be combined in
one person (*Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63]). Whether Luna made
the Representations is the central contested issue: Ricky pleads them and Davion denies
them. Only four people were at the meeting. Davion's case relies on Marci's typed attendance
notes, and Marci signed the agreement as a witness. Luna and Marci are the people best
placed to give evidence about what was said, and Ricky has said he will call them. So it is
known that they will give material evidence on a contested issue, not merely formal or
undisputed facts. This is not a bare tactical assertion by Carl, because the issue is on the
pleadings. Luna also has a personal interest in being cleared of a r 8(2) breach, which may
not match Davion's interests. Rule 22(2) PCR states that, except as otherwise permitted by the
rule, a legal practitioner or law practice must not act for a client if there is, or may
reasonably be expected to be, a conflict between (a) the duty to serve the best interests of
the client; and (b) the interests of the legal practitioner or law practice. Rule 22(3)(a)
PCR states that where the legal practitioner, an immediate family member or the law practice
has an interest in the matter that is adverse to the client's interests, the legal
practitioner must withdraw unless (i) the legal practitioner makes a full and frank
disclosure of the adverse interest to the client; (ii) the legal practitioner advises the
client to obtain independent legal advice; (iii) if the client does not obtain independent
legal advice, the legal practitioner ensures that the client is not under an impression that
the legal practitioner is protecting the client's interests; and (iv) despite (i) and (ii),
the client gives informed consent in writing to the legal practitioner acting, or continuing
to act.

In conclusion, Luna and Marci must not act and, having filed the New Suit, must apply to be
discharged under r 11(3)(a)(ii). They may withdraw under r 26(5)(f) PCR, which states that a
legal practitioner may withdraw if the withdrawal is necessary to avoid a contravention by
the legal practitioner of (i) the Act; (ii) these Rules or any other subsidiary legislation
made under the Act; (iii) any practice directions, guidance notes and rulings issued under
section 71(6) of the Act; (iv) any practice directions, guidance notes and rulings (relating
to professional practice, etiquette, conduct and discipline) issued by the Council or the
Society; or (v) for a lawyer also authorised elsewhere, those of that jurisdiction's
authorities. Rule 26(6) PCR states that on withdrawal they must (a) take reasonable care to
avoid foreseeable harm to the client, including, where the circumstances permit, (i) by
giving reasonable notice of the withdrawal to the client; (ii) by giving the client a
reasonable amount of time to engage another legal practitioner to take over the case or
matter; and (iii) by cooperating with the client's new legal practitioner; and (b) abide by
the client's decision on whether to appoint another legal practitioner, and who to appoint.

### (b) Can Mid Law continue through Chen? (14 marks)

The issue is whether Mid Law may continue to act through another litigation partner, Chen,
with Luna and Marci stepping aside. Rule 11(3)(b) PCR permits it in principle, but on these
facts the risk is high and Mid Law should, on balance, not continue.

Here, r 11(3)(b) PCR, set out in (a), applies: even where a lawyer will be a material
witness, the law practice or a member of it may represent the party "unless doing so would
prejudice the administration of justice". Separately, r 22(4)(a) PCR states that where a law
practice has an interest in any matter entrusted to it by its client and the interest is
adverse to the client's interests, the law practice must withdraw from representing the
client, unless (i) the law practice makes a full and frank disclosure of the adverse
interest to the client; (ii) the law practice advises the client to obtain independent legal
advice; (iii) if the client does not obtain independent legal advice, the law practice
ensures that the client is not under an impression that the law practice is protecting the
client's interests; and (iv) despite (i) and (ii), the client gives the client's informed
consent in writing to the law practice acting, or continuing to act, on the client's behalf.
In *Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63], the Court of Appeal said
that neither the partner who would be a witness nor the litigation partner of the same firm
should have acted, for two reasons: first, the conveyancing partner would be a material
witness; and second, both partners had an interest in the case, because it was their firm
that had acted for the client in the transaction. The court may also restrain a firm under
its supervisory jurisdiction if a fair-minded, reasonably informed member of the public would
conclude that the proper administration of justice requires it, in the interests of
protecting the integrity of the judicial process and the due administration of justice,
including the appearance of justice (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29 at [65]).

In Chen's favour, he was not at the meeting, will not be a witness, and is a partner in his
own right rather than a junior. Rule 11(3)(b) also expressly contemplates a colleague taking
over, and r 26(6)(b) PCR, set out in (a), requires the firm to abide by Davion's decision on
who represents him. Against that, the Defence attacks Mid Law's own conduct: the agreement
was "drafted by the Plaintiff's solicitors, Mid Law LLP", and its managing partner allegedly
made the Representations. That is the second reason in *Ho Kon Kim*. Chen would have to lead
evidence from his own managing partner and associate, and cross-examine Ricky on the
integrity of his own firm's conduct. If Ricky succeeds, Mid Law faces disciplinary exposure
and possibly a claim by Davion. That gives the firm an interest in vindicating itself, which
is adverse to Davion's interest in, for example, a pragmatic settlement, so r 22(4)(a) is
engaged. A fair-minded, reasonably informed observer would be uneasy about the firm whose
managing partner's honesty is in issue running the case, which invites a restraint
application by Carl under *Lim Oon Kuin*.

In conclusion, Mid Law may continue through Chen only after full and frank disclosure of its
interest, advice to Davion to take independent advice, steps to ensure Davion does not think
the firm is protecting his interests if he declines that advice, and his informed written
consent (r 22(4)(a)(i)–(iv)). Even then, Chen must be satisfied that continuing would not
prejudice the administration of justice under r 11(3)(b). Given *Ho Kon Kim* and the attack
on the firm's own conduct, the prudent advice is that Davion should instruct another firm.

### (c) The original Settlement Agreement and Top Law's lien (14 marks)

The issue is whether Luna breached her obligations by sending the original document to Dire
Law. She did. She breached her undertaking to Top Law (r 7(6) PCR), and she failed to tell
Davion of the arrangement (r 5(2)(b) PCR).

Luna breached r 7(6) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must honour every
undertaking the legal practitioner gives to another legal practitioner. Rule 7(7) PCR states
that a legal practitioner must not give an undertaking to another legal practitioner unless
(a) the legal practitioner believes that the undertaking is necessary; and (b) the legal
practitioner knows, when giving the undertaking, that he or she is able to honour it. An
undertaking is a solemn promise on which the other lawyer relies by parting with something
of value, and breach is treated seriously (*Law Society of Singapore v Naidu Priyalatha*
[2022] SGHC 224). Rule 26(7) PCR states that where a legal practitioner (A) claims to
exercise a lien for unpaid legal costs over any document of the client, (a) where another
legal practitioner (B) is acting for the client, A must release that document to B, and
offer such assistance as may be necessary to enable B to take over the matter, if (i) B
undertakes to hold that document subject to the lien, and provides reasonable security for
A's unpaid costs; or (ii) B offers to pay, or to enter into an agreement with the client to
obtain payment of, A's costs upon the completion of the matter, and A accepts B's offer; or
(b) in any other case, A must release that document to the client if A receives reasonable
security for A's unpaid costs. Luna also breached r 5(2)(b) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner must, when advising the client, inform the client of all information known to
the legal practitioner that may reasonably affect the interests of the client in the matter,
other than (i) any information that the legal practitioner is precluded, by any overriding
duty of confidentiality, from disclosing to the client; and (ii) any information that the
client has agreed in writing need not be disclosed to the client.

Top Law released the original only because Luna undertook, as B under r 26(7)(a)(i), to
hold it subject to Top Law's lien for unpaid costs. When Davion moved to Dire Law, Luna sent
the original on without obtaining Dire Law's matching undertaking or any security, and
without telling Top Law. The client's instruction does not release an undertaking already
given to another lawyer, so r 7(6) was breached. Luna also never told Davion that the
document was held subject to Top Law's lien. That plainly affected his interests, neither
exception in r 5(2)(b) applies, and so he could not make an informed decision about paying
Top Law or arranging security.

What Luna should have done: when she gave the undertaking, she should have explained it to
Davion and confirmed that it was necessary and that she could honour it (r 7(7)(a)–(b)).
When Davion moved to Dire Law, she should have released the original only against Dire Law's
written undertaking to hold it subject to Top Law's lien, or against security for Top Law's
costs, mirroring r 26(7)(a)(i). Failing either, she should have kept it and told Davion why.
Now she should immediately ask Dire Law either to return the original or to give Top Law the
undertaking, inform Top Law of the steps taken, and explain the position to Davion.

In conclusion, Luna breached r 7(6) and r 5(2)(b). Section 83(2) of the Legal Profession Act
1966 states that due cause for discipline may be shown by proof that an advocate and
solicitor (b) has been guilty of fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in the discharge of
his or her professional duty, or guilty of such a breach of any usage or rule of conduct made
by the Professional Conduct Council under section 71 or by the Council under the Act as
amounts to improper conduct or practice as an advocate and solicitor; or (h) has been guilty
of such misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor as an officer of the Supreme Court
or as a member of an honourable profession. Luna's breach may amount to improper conduct or
misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor, which is due cause for discipline under
s 83(2)(b) and (h).

## PS1 Q2 — Seldon, Howwie and Emily

Question: PS1 (`Modules/…/3. Practice Sessions - Question Papers/ETH_Practice_Session_1_…
.pdf`), Q2. Not mark-weighted. Pre-writes used: 0-9, 0-11, 0-10, 0-12, 0-13, 0-6, 0-22.

Here, r 4 PCR applies. Rule 4(a) states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to
the court, which takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client, and
r 4(b) states that the duty to the client is subject only to the duty to the court, and must
at all times be fulfilled in a manner that upholds the standing and integrity of the
Singapore legal system and the legal profession in Singapore. In my view CT Law may continue
to act for Lennard, but only with real safeguards: a documented information barrier around
Howwie, notice to Emily, and Lennard's informed consent after full disclosure.

**Rule 20(8).** The first issue is whether the firm is barred because Howwie drafted the
shareholders' agreement. It is not. Rule 20(8) PCR states that where a legal practitioner or
law practice has prepared a document on behalf of 2 or more parties concerning their rights
and obligations, and 2 or more of those parties become involved in a dispute arising from
the document, the legal practitioner or law practice must not represent any disputing party
in the dispute. Howwie prepared only an initial draft, for Emily alone, at a different firm.
He never met the other shareholders, and the executed agreement is completely different. CT
Law prepared nothing. So no document was prepared "on behalf of 2 or more parties". Ragesh's
allegation that Howwie acted for "LRPL and all the shareholders" is contradicted by the
engagement terms and by Emily paying the invoice personally.

**Rule 21: Emily as Howwie's former client.** The second issue is whether CT Law must
decline because Howwie holds Emily's confidential information. Rule 21(1) PCR states the
principles that the duties of loyalty and confidentiality owed by a legal practitioner, and
by a law practice, to a client continue after the termination of the retainer. Rule 21(2)
PCR states that, subject to paragraphs (3), (4) and (5), a legal practitioner or law
practice must decline to represent, or must withdraw from representing, a client (the
current client) in a matter if (a) the legal practitioner or the law practice holds
confidential information relating to a former client that is protected by rule 6; (b) the
current client has an interest that is, or may reasonably be expected to be, adverse to an
interest of the former client; and (c) that information may reasonably be expected to be
material to the representation of the current client in that matter. Rule 6(2) PCR, which
gives the protection in limb (a), states that, subject to paragraph (3), a legal
practitioner must not knowingly disclose any information which (a) is confidential to his
or her client; and (b) is acquired by the legal practitioner (whether from the client or
from any other person) in the course of the legal practitioner's engagement. On limb (a),
Howwie learned Emily's requirements for the agreement in the course of his engagement, and
they are confidential. On limb (b), Emily's interest is adverse to Lennard's, because Ragesh,
the defendant, holds his shares as her nominee. On limb (c), materiality is arguable. The
draft was preliminary, contained no names, was four years old, and differs completely from
the executed agreement. Out of prudence, CT Law should treat r 21(2) as engaged. Rule 21(3)
PCR states that paragraph (2) does not prevent the firm from acting, subject to any
conditions agreed with the former client, if (a) the firm has adequately advised the former
client to obtain independent legal advice; and (b) the former client gives the former
client's informed consent in writing to the firm acting, or continuing to act, for the
current client. Emily's consent is unrealistic given her threatened claim, so CT Law should
rely on r 21(4) PCR, which states that where the requirements in paragraph (3)(a) and (b)
are not met despite reasonable efforts to meet them, paragraph (2) does not prevent the firm
from acting, or continuing to act, if (a) there are adequate safeguards in place to protect
the former client's confidential information; and (b) the firm has made reasonable efforts
to notify the former client (i) of those safeguards; and (ii) that the firm will act, or
continue to act, for the current client. The adequacy of the steps taken to prevent
disclosure depends on factors such as (1) the nature of the work done for the former client,
(2) the timing of the creation of the information barrier, (3) the size of the law firm,
(4) the physical locations of departments within the firm, and (5) the number and seniority
of tainted lawyers (*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at
[68]). On factor (1), the work was limited and long ago. On factor (5), only one lawyer,
Howwie, is tainted, and he is new and not on Lennard's matter. On factors (2) and (4), a
barrier set up now, with written undertakings from Howwie and the team, separate files and
IT access, physical separation and monitoring, is likely to be adequate under r 21(4)(a).
That is what the "usual safeguards" must mean in substance. Notice to Emily of those
safeguards and that the firm will continue to act satisfies r 21(4)(b).

**The court's power to restrain.** Even if the PCR is satisfied, the court may restrain the
firm. A former prospective client who only sought advice, without retaining the firm, can
restrain a firm from acting for another client by showing that (a) the firm was in
possession of information which was confidential to him and to the disclosure of which he
had not consented; (b) the information was or might be relevant to the new matter in which
the interest of the other client was or might be adverse to his own; and (c) there was a risk
that the information would come into the possession of those now acting for the other client
(*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] SGHC 155 at [47]). Emily was an actual
former client of Howwie, so her position is at least as strong. More broadly, the court's
supervisory jurisdiction is invoked to restrain lawyers from acting where a fair-minded,
reasonably informed member of the public would conclude that the proper administration of
justice requires it, in the interests of protecting the integrity of the judicial process and
the due administration of justice, including the appearance of justice, and a long history
between the lawyers and the former client may be relevant (*Lim Oon Kuin v Rajah & Tann Singapore LLP* [2022] SGCA 29 at [65], [72]–[73]). Here there is no long history: Howwie did one limited piece of
work four years ago. An early, well-documented barrier is CT Law's answer to limb (c) of
*Harsha* and to any *Lim Oon Kuin* application.

**Rule 22: Howwie's threatened negligence claim.** The third issue is whether CT Law has
an interest that conflicts with Lennard's. Emily's letter says that if Lennard wins,
Howwie was negligent. Rule 22(2) PCR states that, except as otherwise permitted by the rule, a
legal practitioner or law practice must not act for a client if there is, or may reasonably
be expected to be, a conflict between (a) the duty to serve the best interests of the
client; and (b) the interests of the legal practitioner or law practice. Rule 22(4) PCR
states that where a law practice has an interest in any matter entrusted to it by its
client, (a) where the interest is adverse to the client's interests, the law practice must
withdraw unless (i) it makes a full and frank disclosure of the adverse interest to the
client; (ii) it advises the client to obtain independent legal advice; (iii) if the client
does not obtain independent legal advice, it ensures that the client is not under an
impression that the law practice is protecting the client's interests; and (iv) despite (i)
and (ii), the client gives informed consent in writing to the law practice acting, or
continuing to act; or (b) in any other case, the law practice must withdraw unless (i) it
makes a full and frank disclosure of the interest to the client; and (ii) despite (i), the
client gives informed consent in writing. The conflict rules protect the client, and the
client must be the aggrieved party, so it is Lennard's position that matters, not Emily's
tactical objection (*Then Khek Khoon v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2012] 2 SLR 451 at
[30]–[36]). The threatened liability attaches to Howwie's former firm, not CT Law, and Seldon
has every incentive to win, so the interest is not adverse and r 22(4)(b) is the relevant
limb. A colleague's exposure might, however, be thought to temper the firm's zeal. CT Law
should therefore disclose the threat to Lennard and get his written consent, and keep Howwie
entirely away from the matter. If Howwie may become a witness about the draft, r 11(3) PCR
applies, which states that where it is known or appears that a legal practitioner will be
required to give evidence material to the determination of any contested issue, (a) that
legal practitioner must not accept instructions from any party and must discharge himself,
or apply to be discharged, if acting; but (b) the law practice in which he practises, or a
member of it, may represent or continue to represent any party to that case, unless doing so
would prejudice the administration of justice. Howwie is not acting, so Seldon can still act
under r 11(3)(b), provided this does not prejudice the administration of justice.

**Seldon's friendship with Lennard.** Rule 11(2)(a) PCR states that a legal practitioner must
not appear before a court or tribunal in a matter where it would be difficult for the legal
practitioner to maintain the legal practitioner's professional independence by reason of any
commercial, family, personal or other relationship between the legal practitioner and his

## PS2 Q1 — Zapp and Barbara's undertaking

Section 82(1) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that the following persons are
officers of the Supreme Court: (a) a person duly admitted as a lawyer (NP) or an advocate
and solicitor; (b) a Judicial Service Officer; (c) a Legal Service Officer; and (d) a PD
Officer. Rule 4(a) PCR states that a legal practitioner has a paramount duty to the court,
which takes precedence over the legal practitioner's duty to the client. Zapp breached that
duty repeatedly.

**Advice to get round the undertaking.** The first issue is whether Zapp could advise
Barbara on the transfer. He could not. Zapp breached r 10(5) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner must not render to his or her client any advice which the legal practitioner
knows or has reasonable grounds to believe is sought by the client for any dishonest,
fraudulent or unlawful purpose. He also breached r 10(6) PCR, which states that a legal
practitioner must not knowingly assist or permit his or her client (a) to mislead a court or
tribunal; or (b) to do any other thing which the legal practitioner considers to be
dishonest. Rule 10(3) PCR states that, to the extent that a legal practitioner is able, the
legal practitioner must prevent his or her client from, must not be a party to, and must not
assist the client in, doing either or both of the following: (a) suppressing evidence;
(b) giving false evidence or false information to a court or tribunal. Barbara had undertaken
to the court not to dispose of her assets anywhere. Causing her Momcorp shares to be replaced
by shares issued to her lover in the Caribbean would breach that undertaking and put assets
beyond Hermes' reach. That is an unlawful purpose under r 10(5), and a dishonest one under
r 10(6)(b). Zapp recognised the problem and devised a "solution". A lawyer who acted with his
client to corruptly solicit a gratification was convicted (*Narindar Singh s/o Malagar Singh
v Public Prosecutor* [1996] 3 SLR(R) 318 at [48]–[53]) and struck off (*Law Society of
Singapore v Narindar Singh s/o Malagar Singh* [1997] 3 SLR(R) 334). Zapp should have told
Barbara that the transaction would breach her undertaking and that she must seek the court's
permission first. If she persisted, he should have ceased to act.

filed later. Zapp drafted an affidavit giving an LSE-rules reason for non-disclosure while
admitting he had "no idea" what those rules say. Zapp breached r 9(2)(b) PCR, which states
that a legal practitioner must not fabricate any fact or evidence in any communication with,
or representation or submission to, the court or tribunal, and r 9(2)(g) PCR, which states
that a legal practitioner must not concoct any evidence or contrive any fact, whether or not
that evidence or fact will assist in advancing the client's case. He also breached
r 9(2)(h)(iv) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must not draft, in the case of an
affidavit or a witness statement, any statement of fact other than the substance of any
evidence which the legal practitioner reasonably believes, having regard to the legal
practitioner's instructions, the witness making the affidavit or statement would give if
that evidence was given orally. Zapp further breached r 5(2)(c) PCR, which states that a
legal practitioner must act with reasonable diligence and competence in the provision of
services to the client, and r 5(2)(d) PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must
ensure that the legal practitioner has the relevant knowledge, skills and attributes
required for each matter undertaken on behalf of the client, and apply the knowledge, skills
and attributes in a manner appropriate to that matter. The test is what a reasonably
competent solicitor would have done, having regard to the normal standards adopted in
practice (*Law Society of Singapore v K Jayakumar Naidu* [2012] 4 SLR 1232 at [85]–[91]). The
affidavit was designed to manufacture a record of "good faith" for later use, which is a
contrived fact under r 9(2)(g). Its LSE explanation had no basis Zapp had checked, so he
could not reasonably believe it was the substance of Barbara's oral evidence under
r 9(2)(h)(iv). He told Barbara it was fine as long as she "genuinely believes" it, and left
her to read foreign rules online. No reasonably competent solicitor would advise on rules he
admits he does not know, so r 5(2)(c)–(d) are breached. This was contrived evidence and
incompetent advice.

**Attestation.** The third issue is how the affidavit was sworn. PD 1.6.1 states that
members are warned of the dangers of attesting the signature of documents without the
signatory having personally appeared before the legal practitioner, with Commissioners for
Oaths particularly advised to heed the warning (Part A), and that false attestation of
documents may amount to grossly improper conduct in the discharge of a legal practitioner's
professional duty and a breach of the Legal Profession Act (Part B). In *Law Society of
Singapore v Thirumurthy Ayernaar Pambayan* [2022] SGHC 79, a lawyer who falsely attested

Barbara signed and left before the Commissioner arrived. On Zapp's assurance, the
Commissioner then stamped the affidavit in front of a secretary. The jurat is false, and Zapp
procured it. Zapp also breached r 32 PCR, which states that a legal practitioner must,
regardless of the legal practitioner's designation in a law practice, exercise proper
supervision over the staff working under the legal practitioner in the law practice. The
affidavit cannot be used. Rule 9(1)(d) PCR states the principle that a legal practitioner
must not present, or permit to be presented, any evidence or information which the legal
practitioner knows to be false, and the affidavit would be exactly that if filed.

**Misleading the court.** The fourth issue is Zapp's statement at the hearing that his
"instructions are that Barbara certainly has not disposed of any assets". Zapp breached
r 9(2)(a) PCR, which states that, when conducting proceedings, a legal practitioner must not
knowingly mislead or attempt to mislead in any way, whether by fabricating facts or
evidence, by including a statement of fact known to be false, or otherwise, (i) the court or
tribunal; (ii) any staff of the court or tribunal; (iii) any other legal practitioner; or
(iv) any witness in, or any other person involved in or associated with, those proceedings.

practitioner continues to act for the client, conduct the client's case in a manner that

legal practitioner unknowingly contravenes any provision of rule 9, the legal practitioner

contravention to each person affected by it, and take reasonable steps to remedy it. Zapp's

a legal practitioner whose client is a party to proceedings must inform the client of the

requirement concerning the conduct and presentation of the client's case. Zapp must advise

In conclusion, Zapp breached r 5(2)(c)–(d), r 9(1)(d), r 9(2)(a), (b), (g) and (h), r 10(3),
(5) and (6), r 32 and PD 1.6.1. Section 83(2)(b) of the Legal Profession Act 1966 states that

practice as an advocate and solicitor. This is likely grossly improper conduct under
