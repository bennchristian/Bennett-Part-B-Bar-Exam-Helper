### eth-w-0-35 — Why ethics matters; character; due cause and sanctions (s 83 LPA)
*Keywords: character, integrity, honesty, admission, fit and proper, due cause, s 83 LPA, struck off, suspension, penalty, censure, personal misconduct, Ahmad Khalis, Rasif David, Tay Quan Li Leon, Sean Wong, Gabriel Silas Tang Rafferty*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer]'s conduct shows due cause for discipline under s 83 of
the Legal Profession Act 1966 and what sanction may follow. **C** [It does / it is likely to].
**R** All advocates and solicitors are subject to the control of the Supreme Court and on due
cause shown may be struck off, suspended for up to five years, ordered to pay a penalty of up
to $100,000, or censured (s 83(1) LPA). Due cause includes a conviction implying a defect of
character, fraudulent or grossly improper conduct in professional duty or a breach of the
rules amounting to improper conduct (s 83(2)(a)–(b) LPA), paid procurement of work
(s 83(2)(d)–(f)), misconduct unbefitting an advocate and solicitor (s 83(2)(h)), and an
incompatible business (s 83(2)(i)). **P** The standard is objective and set by the court, and there is a
paramount public interest in deterrence and in public confidence in the profession (*Law
Society of Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [2]–[5],
[81]–[82]). Honesty and integrity are required in a lawyer's personal as well as professional
life (*Law Society of Singapore v Rasif David* [2008] 2 SLR(R) 955 at [50]–[60]). Lawyers are
"ministers in the temple of justice", so admission requires good character, not just exam
passes (*Re Tay Quan Li Leon* [2022] SGHC 133 at [1], [34], [46]); cheating in the Bar
examinations shows a character deficit (*Re Sean Wong Wai Loong* [2022] SGHC 237 at [76]); and
lack of candour in the admission process does too (*Re Gabriel Silas Tang Rafferty* [2024]
SGHC 82 at [1]–[2], [38]–[39]). **A** Here [Lawyer]'s [conduct] goes to [integrity / competence]. Dishonesty almost invariably leads to
striking off. **C**
[Lawyer]'s conduct is due cause under s 83(2)[(b) / (h)], and the likely sanction is
[striking off / suspension / a penalty].

*Source note: s 83(1)–(2) LPA p217–218; Ahmad Khalis, Rasif David, Tay Quan Li Leon, Sean Wong,
Gabriel Silas Tang Rafferty pinpoints SYL p1; summaries Cyril §I.1.1–1.2 (secondary). This also
serves as the stock closing line in the preamble.*

conduct was still misconduct. Even without a retainer, a duty of care may be owed to a
third party (*AEL v Cheoh Yeoh & Associates LLC* [2014] 3 SLR 1231). **A** Here, [X]
[paid / gave instructions / was told the lawyer would "help" / received and relied on
advice about the document]. A short call that merely points to existing contract terms
leans against a fresh retainer; advice on what to do next leans towards one. **C**
Therefore [X] is [not] a client, and the conflict and confidentiality rules [do / do not]

### eth-w-0-2 — Limiting or excluding liability to the client

### eth-w-0-39 — Privilege; requests for information from authorities

### eth-w-0-7 — Acting for two or more parties: r 20 before accepting
*Keywords: r 20, multiple clients, diversity of interests, informed consent in writing, independent legal advice, written record, commercial conflict, rival bidders, consent before disclosure, Lie Hendri Rusli, Uthayasurian, Mahidon, Kuek Siang Wei, Ezekiel Peter Latimer*

**I** The issue is whether [Firm] may act for both [A] and [B] in [matter]. **C** [Firm]
may act only if it complies with r 20(3) PCR. **R** Where a firm intends to act for two or

diverge, that it may be unable to share information or give advice prejudicial to the other,
and that it must cease to act if it cannot advise competently, evenly and consistently;
advise independent legal advice and, if refused, keep a written record or confirmation; and

conflicts throughout (r 20(4) PCR). **P** The standard owed to each client is at least that
owed to a single client (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at
[48]–[50]); a firm that mishandles the conflict risks losing all its clients (*Law Society
of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram* [2009] 4 SLR(R) 674 at [34]–[53]); consent must be
genuinely informed (*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36
at [63]–[80]; *Kuek Siang Wei v Kuek Siew Chew* [2015] 5 SLR 357); and preferring one
client's interests to another's led to suspension in *Law Society of Singapore v Ezekiel
Peter Latimer* [2019] SGHC 92. **A** Here [A] and [B] are [rival bidders]; their interests
diverge because [facts]. Before approaching [A] for consent, the firm must get [B]'s consent
to reveal [B]'s interest, since that fact is itself confidential (r 6 PCR). Distinguish a
mere commercial conflict from a PCR conflict. **C** [Firm] may act for both if the r 20(3)
steps are completed and information barriers are in place.

### eth-w-0-13 — Lawyer's or firm's own interest against the client's: r 22

### eth-w-0-32 — Overcharging; fixed and non-refundable fees
*Keywords: overcharging, r 17(7), r 17(8), fixed fee, agreed fee, non-refundable deposit, disproportionate, PD 5.2.2, s 111 LPA, s 113(4) LPA, Syn Kok Kay, Andre Ravindran*

**I** The issue is whether the agreed fee of [$X] amounts to overcharging. **C** It is
[likely] overcharging. **R** A lawyer must charge fairly (r 17(1) PCR) and must not charge
a fee that is overcharging even if a fee agreement permits it (r 17(7) PCR). There is
overcharging if a reasonable lawyer could not in good faith charge the amount, given the
lawyer's standing and experience, the nature of the work, the time needed, the client's
instructions and other circumstances (r 17(8) PCR). **P** A written agreement for a gross
sum in contentious business is permitted (s 111 Legal Profession Act 1966), but it gives
no licence to charge an unreasonable fee, may be declared void if unfair or unreasonable,
and a "non-refundable" fee is not always kept regardless of work done (s 113(4) LPA; PD
5.2.2). The extent and disproportion of the overcharge are relevant to gravity (*Law
Society of Singapore v Syn Kok Kay* [2023] SGHC 7). **A** Here the fee of [$4m] is [two
thirds] of the [$6m] claim, for what appears a [straightforward claim for the price of
goods], and is payable in full even if the case settles early, when little work has been
done. Unless the complexity, seniority of counsel or client's requirements justify it,
no reasonable lawyer could charge this in good faith. **C** The term risks breaching r 17(7);
the fee should be tied to work done or reduced.

*Source note: r 17 PCR p31–33; s 111, 113(4) LPA via PD 5.2.2 p1; Syn Kok Kay SYL p12,
gravity factors Cyril §fees (secondary). Tested PYP21 Q3.1.*

### eth-w-0-33 — Contingency and success fees
*Keywords: contingency fee, success fee, bonus, uplift fee, r 18, s 107(1)(b) LPA, champerty, payment only in the event of success, conditional fee agreement, prescribed proceedings, s 107(3AA), GN 5.6.1, Kurubalan, Lau See Jin Jeffrey, impecunious client*

**I** The issue is whether the [success bonus / percentage fee] is a prohibited contingency
fee. **C** It is prohibited. **R** A solicitor must not enter an agreement for contentious
proceedings that stipulates for or contemplates payment only in the event of success
(s 107(1)(b) Legal Profession Act 1966), and must not negotiate for an interest in the subject
matter or for remuneration proportionate to the amount the client recovers (r 18 PCR). The
only exception is a conditional fee agreement complying with Part 8A, which may provide an
"uplift fee" payable in specified circumstances (s 107(3AA), s 115A(1) LPA), but only in
prescribed proceedings: arbitration, certain SICC proceedings and related court and mediation
proceedings (GN 5.6.1 para 8). **P** The prohibition keeps the lawyer detached enough to
discharge duties to the court (*Law Society of Singapore v Kurubalan s/o Manickam Rengaraju*
[2013] 4 SLR 91 at [42], [45]); a percentage of damages was a contingency fee in *Law Society
of Singapore v Lau See Jin Jeffrey* [2017] SGHC 30. GN 5.6.1 treats a discounted fee plus an
uplift on success as a conditional fee agreement, so outside prescribed proceedings it is
caught by s 107. **A** Here the extra [$500,000] is payable only if [the suit settles below
$500,000] in [an ordinary court suit], so that part of the fee is payable only in the event of
success. A fixed base fee does not save it. Contrast acting for an impecunious client knowing
fees will in practice be recovered only on success, which is permissible (*Kurubalan* at [2]).
**C** The bonus breaches s 107(1)(b) and should be removed.

*Source note: r 18, 18A PCR p34; s 107 LPA p264–265; s 115A LPA p274–275; GN 5.6.1 paras 6, 8
and illustration; Kurubalan L10 p45–47; Lau See Jin Jeffrey L10 p48. No case decides a bonus on
top of a fixed fee; the conclusion rests on s 107(3AA), s 115A and GN 5.6.1. Tested PYP21 Q3.2.*

### eth-w-0-34 — Sharing fees with unauthorised persons
*Keywords: fee sharing, r 19, unauthorised person, paralegal, 50/50 split, commission, expired practising certificate, Tan See Leh Jonathan, debt collectors, PD 5.4.1*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer]'s arrangement with [X] shares fees with an
unauthorised person. **C** It breaches r 19 PCR. **R** A lawyer or law practice must not
share fees with, or pay a commission to, any unauthorised person for legal work performed

the roll with a current practising certificate, which includes paralegals (s 32(2) Legal
Profession Act 1966; GN 3.7.1). **P** Sharing about half the fees with a paralegal whose
practising certificate had lapsed, and who held himself out as a solicitor, was serious
misconduct (*Law Society of Singapore v Tan See Leh Jonathan* [2020] SGHC 102). Using debt
collectors to recover legal fees is also prohibited (PD 5.4.1). **A** Here [Lawyer] and
[paralegal] split [50/50] the fees on [non-contentious matters], and a further [5%] goes to
the referrer. Both are payments out of legal fees to unauthorised persons for legal work.
That [the paralegal does the work] makes it worse, not better, since it shows she is
practising in substance. **C** [Lawyer] breached r 19(1), and the 5% to the referrer also breaches
r 39(2)(b).

*Source note: r 19 PCR p35; s 32(2) LPA L8 p9; GN 3.7.1 para 2 p1; Tan See Leh Jonathan SYL
p13, L10 p54; PD 5.4.1 L10 p55. Tested PS3 Q2.*

### eth-w-0-68 — Client money and the Solicitors' Accounts Rules
*Keywords: client money, client account, r 16 PCR, LPSAR, Solicitors' Accounts Rules, pay in without delay, office account, withdrawal, two signatories, records, reconciliation, Dhanwant Singh, Yong Wei Kuen Paul, Tay Choon Leng John, Lim Tean*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] dealt properly with money received for [client]. **C**
[Lawyer] breached the Solicitors' Accounts Rules and r 16 PCR. **R** A lawyer must hold money
entrusted by a client in a way that protects the client, is accountable for it, and must
promptly notify the client of money received and render accounts on request (r 16(1)–(3) PCR).
Client's money — money held for a client, other than trust money, money belonging only to the
solicitor, and conveyancing money — must be paid without delay into a client account (r 2, r 3
LPSAR). Only permitted money may go into a client account (r 6), and money may be drawn only for
payments to or for the client, reimbursement, on the client's authority, or for costs after a
bill or written notice (r 7 LPSAR; for costs, after two working days' notice, PD 3.3.1).
Withdrawals over $5,000 need two solicitors, none with under three years' practice (r 8
LPSAR). Proper records must be kept and reconciled monthly (r 11).
**P** The rules protect the public by keeping client money safe and maintain the profession's
standing (*Law Society of Singapore v Dhanwant Singh* [2019] SGHC 290; *Law Society of Singapore
v Yong Wei Kuen Paul* [2020] SGHC 66). Paying client money into the office account was not
excused by calling it an "agreed fee" without a written agreement (*Law Society of Singapore v
Tay Choon Leng John* [2012] 3 SLR 150). Ignorance of the rules is no defence (*Law Society of
Singapore v Lim Tean* [2026] SGHC 78 at [55]–[63]). **A** Here [Lawyer] [put the client's
deposit into the office account / withdrew fees without a bill]. **C** [Lawyer] breached r 3 [/ r 7] LPSAR and r 16 PCR, and must restore the
money to the client account at once.

*Source note: r 16 PCR p30; LPSAR r 2–3 p2–5, r 6–8 p7–9, r 11 p12; GN 3.3.1; PD 3.3.1 p1; Dhanwant Singh L10 p10; Yong Wei Kuen Paul and Lim Tean SYL p12; facts Cyril §VII.1
(secondary).*

### eth-w-0-69 — Anti-money laundering: customer due diligence and suspicious transactions

*Source note: AML Rules r 5–6 p9–11, r 8 p12, r 11–13 p17–20, r 16 p23–24, r 19 p28; Chan Chun
