## Client relationship

### eth-w-0-1 — Who is a client; implied retainer
*Keywords: client, implied retainer, s 2(1) LPA, non-client, duty of care, Ahmad Khalis, Anwar Patrick Adrian, Lee Suet Fern, AEL*

**I** The issue is whether [X] is [Lawyer]'s client, so that the duties of loyalty,
confidentiality and care under the PCR are owed to [X]. **C** [X] is likely [not] to be a
client under an implied retainer. **R** Under s 2(1) of the Legal Profession Act 1966, a
"client" includes any person who retains or employs, or is about to retain or employ, a
solicitor, and any person liable to pay the solicitor's costs. A retainer need not be in
writing: it may be implied where, viewed objectively, an intention to form a
solicitor-client relationship ought fairly to be imputed to both parties (*Law Society of
Singapore v Ahmad Khalis bin Abdul Ghani* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 308 at [64]–[73]). **P** Relevant
factors include who pays the fees, who gives instructions, any past relationship, and
whether substantive advice was given and relied on (*Ahmad Khalis* at [64]–[73]; *Anwar
Patrick Adrian v Ng Chong & Hue LLC* [2014] 3 SLR 761 at [49]–[61]). In *Law Society of
Singapore v Lee Suet Fern* [2020] SGHC 255 at [57]–[69] no retainer was found, but the
conduct was still misconduct. Even without a retainer, a duty of care may be owed to a
third party (*AEL v Cheoh Yeoh & Associates LLC* [2014] 3 SLR 1231). **A** Here, [X]
[paid / gave instructions / was told the lawyer would "help" / received and relied on
advice about the document]. A short call that merely points to existing contract terms
leans against a fresh retainer; advice on what to do next leans towards one. **C**
Therefore [X] is [not] a client, and the conflict and confidentiality rules [do / do not]
apply to [Lawyer]'s dealings with [X].

*Source note: s 2(1) LPA (L1 p5); Ahmad Khalis, Anwar, Lee Suet Fern pinpoints SYL p2; L1
p6–7 (AEL); factors from Cyril §1.2 (secondary). Lee Suet Fern outcome L3 p24.*

### eth-w-0-2 — Limiting or excluding liability to the client
*Keywords: limitation of liability, exclusion clause, negligence, fraud, PI insurance minimum, s 112(5) LPA, PD 7.1.2, s 113(4), engagement letter*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] may limit or exclude liability to the client as the
engagement terms propose. **C** The clause is [void in part / impermissible]. **R** A
law practice may limit, but not exclude, its civil liability to clients, and may not limit
it below the minimum cover required by the Legal Profession (Professional Indemnity
Insurance) Rules, currently $1 million per claim for a law firm (PD 7.1.2). Liability for
fraud or reckless disregard of professional obligations cannot be limited at all, and
courts will not enforce an unfair agreement in the lawyer's favour (PD 7.1.2). In an
agreement as to costs for contentious business, a term that the solicitor is not liable
for negligence, or is relieved of professional responsibility, is wholly void (s 112(5)
Legal Profession Act 1966). **P** An unfair or unreasonable costs agreement may also be
declared void (s 113(4) LPA). **A** Here the cap of [$X] [is below the PI minimum] and
[purports to cover fraud / breach of professional obligations], so it fails on [each]
ground. A cap at or above the PI minimum, covering negligence only and explained to the
client, would be acceptable. **C** The clause is unenforceable as drafted, and offering it
breaches PD 7.1.2; [Lawyer] should redraft it to a lawful cap.

*Source note: PD 7.1.2 p1; s 112(5) LPA p271; s 113(4) quoted in PD 5.2.2 p1. Tested
PYP21 Q3.4.*

### eth-w-0-3 — Honesty with the client
*Keywords: honesty, dishonesty, r 5(2)(a), false pretext, deceive client, striking off, Udeh Kumar, Chia Choon Yang, Ng Bock Hoh Dixon*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] breached the duty of honesty to the client by [act].
**C** [Lawyer] breached r 5(2)(a) PCR. **R** A legal practitioner must be honest in all
dealings with the client (r 5(1)(a), r 5(2)(a) PCR). **P** Misconduct involving dishonesty
will almost invariably lead to striking off where it reveals a character defect, such as
dishonesty that violates the trust inherent in the solicitor-client relationship (*Law

*Law Society of Singapore v Chia Choon Yang* [2018] SGHC 174 at [44]–[55]). Creating a
false document in the course of practice is dishonest even if done to protect the client's
position (*Law Society of Singapore v Ng Bock Hoh Dixon* [2011] SGHC 242 at [31]–[35]).
Inexperience is no mitigation for dishonesty (*Law Society of Singapore v Ng Chee Sing*
[2000] 1 SLR(R) 466). **A** Here [Lawyer] [gave a false reason — e.g. "required for
insurers" — to get the client to sign / misstated the position], intending the client to
act on it and for [Lawyer]'s own benefit. Note also that r 6(3)(d) only permits
disclosure *to* an insurer; it creates no reporting duty on the client. **C** This is a
deliberate breach of r 5(2)(a), likely grossly improper conduct under s 83(2)(b) LPA, and
with the self-interest involved, the sanction is likely to be severe.

*Source note: r 5 PCR p10; Udeh Kumar 2017, Chia Choon Yang, Ng Bock Hoh Dixon pinpoints

### eth-w-0-4 — Duty to inform and advise the client
*Keywords: duty to inform, r 5(2)(b), material information, advise on risks, r 17(2)(f), informed decision, client instructions, Su Ah Tee*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] failed to tell the client [information / risk]. **C**
[Lawyer] breached r 5(2)(b) PCR. **R** When advising, a lawyer must tell the client all

**A** Here [Lawyer] knew [e.g. that disclosure was legally required / there was no

client] but said nothing [because the client would not like it]. The duty is not
displaced by fear of displeasing the client; the lawyer must give the advice and, if the
client insists on an improper course, decline to act on it. **C** [Lawyer] breached

### eth-w-0-6 — Confidentiality
*Keywords: confidentiality, r 6, disclose, exceptions, consent, conflict check, reason for declining, Ravi Madasamy, privilege*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] may disclose [information] to [person]. **C**
[Lawyer] may not disclose it without [client]'s consent. **R** A lawyer must not knowingly
disclose information confidential to the client and acquired in the course of the
engagement, from the client or anyone else (r 6(2) PCR). Disclosure is allowed only if the

client's minimum price / the will's contents] is confidential and came to [Lawyer] in the
engagement, and no exception applies. In a conflict situation this means the firm may
have to decline [Y] without giving the reason, and must get [client A]'s consent before
telling [client B] that A has approached the firm. **C** [Lawyer] must not disclose [it]
and should say only that the firm is unable to act.

## Conflicts of interest

### eth-w-0-7 — Acting for two or more parties: r 20 before accepting
*Keywords: r 20, multiple clients, diversity of interests, informed consent in writing, independent legal advice, commercial conflict, Lie Hendri Rusli, Uthayasurian, Mahidon*

**I** The issue is whether [Firm] may act for both [A] and [B] in [matter]. **C** [Firm]
may act only if it complies with r 20(3) PCR. **R** Where a firm intends to act for two or
more parties to a matter and a diversity of interests exists or may reasonably be expected
to exist (r 20(2) PCR), it must first communicate directly with each party to explain how
their interests diverge and that it may be unable to share information or give advice
prejudicial to the other; tell them it must cease to act if it cannot advise competently,
evenly and consistently; advise independent legal advice, recording any refusal; and get
each party's informed consent in writing (r 20(3)(a)–(e) PCR). **P** The standard of care
owed to each of several clients is at least that owed to a single client, and one cannot
be preferred (*Lie Hendri Rusli v Wong Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]);
the lawyer must disclose conflicts, get informed consent and remain impartial, or risk
losing all clients (*Law Society of Singapore v Uthayasurian Sidambaram* [2009] 4 SLR(R)
674 at [34]–[53]); consent must be genuinely informed (*Mahidon Nichiar bte Mohd Ali v
Dawood Sultan Kamaldin* [2015] SGCA 36 at [63]–[80]). **A** Here [A] and [B] are [rival
bidders / borrower and lender]; their interests diverge because [facts]. Distinguish a
mere commercial conflict (the firm's business preference) from a PCR conflict, which
needs two clients in the same matter. **C** [Firm] may act for both if the r 20(3) steps
are completed and information barriers are in place.

*Source note: r 20 PCR p35–37; Lie Hendri Rusli L2 p9; Uthayasurian L2 p10–11, SQ p111;
Mahidon pinpoint SYL p3; commercial vs PCR conflict INTRO2 p26. Model: SQ p110–111.
Tested PYP23 Q1.2, PYP21 Q1.1–1.2.*

### eth-w-0-8 — When multiple representation is unworkable: ceasing to act
*Keywords: r 20(4), r 20(6), cease to act, competently evenly and consistently, r 20(7), regulator, veto, crossroads, Lie Hendri Rusli*

**I** The issue is whether [Firm] can continue, or begin, to act for [C] alongside [A]
and [B]. **C** [Firm] should decline [or cease] to act. **R** Throughout the retainer a
firm acting for several parties must stay vigilant for conflicts, tell each party of them,
and must cease to act if it has difficulty advising and dealing with their divergent
interests competently, evenly and consistently (r 20(4), (6) PCR). It may continue for one
party only if it stops acting for all the others whose interests diverge and they all
give informed written consent (r 20(7) PCR). **P** A lawyer who reaches the "crossroads"
where they cannot advise each client evenly must discharge themselves (*Lie Hendri Rusli
v Wong Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). **A** Here [C] [has a veto /
can impose conditions such as a performance bond / is the other side of a default] over
[A] and [B]. When [C] takes a position adverse to them, [A] and [B] will need advice on
how to resist or challenge [C], while [C] needs advice on maintaining it. Information
barriers cannot cure the lawyers' inability to advise each side evenly. **C** On balance
[Firm] should not act for [C]; if already acting, it must cease to act for [all / all
but one with written consent].

*Source note: r 20(4)–(7) PCR p37–38; Lie Hendri Rusli L2 p9; model reasoning SQ
p112–113. Tested PYP23 Q1.3, PYP21 Q1.2.*

### eth-w-0-9 — Document prepared for parties now in dispute: r 20(8)
*Keywords: r 20(8), drafted document, joint venture agreement, shareholders' agreement, settlement agreement, dispute arising from the document, absolute bar*

**I** The issue is whether [Firm] may act in a dispute about a [document] it prepared.
**C** [Firm] is barred if it prepared the document on behalf of two or more of the
parties. **R** Where a firm has prepared a document on behalf of two or more parties
concerning their rights and obligations, and two or more of them become involved in a
dispute arising from the document, it must not represent any disputing party (r 20(8)
PCR). Unlike r 20(3), this bar cannot be cured by consent. **P** The rule reflects the
duty of loyalty owed to each party for whom the document was prepared (r 20(1) PCR; *Lie
Hendri Rusli v Wong Tan & Molly Lim* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 594 at [48]–[50]). **A** The key
question is whose behalf the document was prepared on. If [Lawyer] acted for [one party
only] and the other party was unrepresented or separately advised, r 20(8) does not apply,
though r 8(2) and r 11(3) may (see eth-w-0-25, eth-w-0-22). If the other side alleges the
firm acted for "all the shareholders", test it against the engagement terms and records.
A draft for one client, later changed and signed by others the firm never met, is unlikely
to be prepared "on behalf of" them. **C** Therefore r 20(8) [does / does not] bar [Firm]
from acting.

*Source note: r 20(1), (8) PCR p35, p38; Lie Hendri Rusli L2 p9. Tested PS1 Q2, PYP23 Q2.1

### eth-w-0-10 — Information barriers (Chinese walls)
*Keywords: information barrier, Chinese wall, safeguards, tainted lawyer, physical separation, undertakings, monitoring, Harsha factors, same lawyer*

**I** The issue is whether information barriers can adequately protect [client]'s
confidential information. **C** A barrier is [likely / unlikely] to be adequate here.
**R** Where a firm acts despite holding a client's or former client's confidential
information, it must have adequate safeguards in place to protect it (r 21(4)(a) PCR),

staff member, and monitoring, all in place from the start. It is weakest where the person
holding the information is the same lawyer who would act, or the firm is small, as
here [facts]. **C** Therefore [Firm] [may rely on a barrier, provided it is set up
immediately and documented / cannot rely on a barrier and should decline].

### eth-w-0-11 — Acting against a former client: r 21
*Keywords: r 21, former client, confidential information, adverse interest, material, informed consent, independent legal advice, adequate safeguards, notify*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer] may act for [current client] given [Lawyer]'s past
work for [former client]. **C** [Lawyer] must decline unless r 21(3) or (4) PCR is met.
**R** A lawyer or firm must decline or withdraw if it holds confidential information of a
former client protected by r 6, the current client's interest is or may reasonably be
adverse to the former client's, and that information may reasonably be material to the
current matter (r 21(2) PCR). It may still act if the former client, adequately advised to
get independent legal advice, gives informed written consent (r 21(3) PCR); or, if that
fails despite reasonable efforts, if adequate safeguards protect the information and the
firm makes reasonable efforts to notify the former client of the safeguards and that it
will act (r 21(4) PCR). **P** The duties of loyalty and confidentiality continue after the
retainer ends (r 21(1) PCR). Safeguards are judged on the *Harsha* factors (*Harsha
Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti Shewakram Samtani* [2018] 5 SLR 894 at [68]). **A** Here
[Lawyer] knows [e.g. the former client's minimum price / negotiating history / the terms
of the draft], which is confidential; [current client]'s interest is adverse because
[bidder vs seller / suing the former client's nominee]; and the information is plainly
material. Consent under r 21(3) should be sought first. If refused, r 21(4) is hard to
satisfy where [Lawyer] personally holds the information. **C** [Lawyer] may act only with
[former client]'s written consent; otherwise [Lawyer] should decline.

*Source note: r 21 PCR p39–40; Harsha SQ p110; model SQ p109–110. Tested PYP23 Q1.1, PS1
Q2, PYP21 Q1.2.*

### eth-w-0-12 — Court's power to restrain a firm; former prospective clients
*Keywords: injunction to restrain, disqualify, supervisory jurisdiction, fair-minded reasonably informed member of public, Lim Oon Kuin, Harsha, prospective client, letter demanding withdrawal*

**I** The issue is whether the court could restrain [Firm] from acting for [client],
as [opponent] demands. **C** There is a [real / low] risk of an order restraining [Firm].
**R** Apart from the PCR, the court has a supervisory jurisdiction to restrain lawyers from

is a risk the information reaches those now acting (*Harsha Rajkumar Mirpuri v Shanti
Shewakram Samtani* [2018] SGHC 155). **A** Here [opponent]'s letter [does / does not]
identify a rule; [Firm] should still assess it seriously. [Lawyer]'s [past retainer /
role as drafter / likely evidence] would lead a fair-minded observer to [doubt / accept]
the firm's independence. **C** [Firm] should [withdraw / respond giving reasons and
continue with safeguards], since a later restraining order would prejudice the client.

*Source note: Lim Oon Kuin L2 p22 (no pinpoint in sources); Harsha test L2 p21; both on

### eth-w-0-13 — Lawyer's or firm's own interest against the client's: r 22
*Keywords: r 22, own interest, personal interest, firm's interest, adverse interest, full and frank disclosure, informed consent in writing, Ho Kon Kim, Then Khek Khoon, Tan Phuay Khiang, Govindan Balan Nair*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer/Firm] has a personal interest that conflicts with the
duty to serve [client]'s best interests. **C** [Lawyer/Firm] must withdraw unless
r 22(3) PCR is complied with. **R** A lawyer or firm must not act where there is, or may
reasonably be, a conflict between the duty to serve the client's best interests and its
own interests (r 22(2) PCR). If the lawyer, an immediate family member, or the firm has an
adverse interest in the matter, the lawyer must withdraw unless they make full and frank
disclosure, advise independent legal advice, ensure the client does not think the lawyer
is protecting the client's interests if no advice is taken, and obtain the client's
informed written consent (r 22(3)(a), (4)(a) PCR). **P** Where the firm's own handling of
the transaction is what caused the dispute, it should not act and should advise the client
to get independent advice (*Ho Kon Kim v Betsy Lim Gek Kim* [2001] SGCA 64 at [61]–[63]).
The rule protects the client, so it is the client, not the opponent, who must be aggrieved
(*Then Khek Khoon v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2012] 2 SLR 451 at [30]–[36]). Preferring
referrers' or the lawyer's own interests is serious misconduct (*Law Society of Singapore
v Tan Phuay Khiang* [2007] 3 SLR(R) 477). **A** Here [the firm's own drafting or advice is
in issue / a negligence claim is threatened against a firm lawyer / the firm wants to keep a
lucrative client relationship]. That gives [Firm] an interest in [defending its own work /
avoiding liability] that may diverge from [client]'s interest in the best outcome. A merely
commercial wish to keep a client is not a PCR conflict, but it must not drive advice.
**C** [Firm] should disclose the interest and get written consent after independent
advice, or withdraw.

*Source note: r 22 PCR p40–42; Ho Kon Kim and Then Khek Khoon pinpoints SYL p6 (r 11
section) and SYL p3; Tan Phuay Khiang L3 p8. Tested PYP22 (b), PYP23 Q2.2, PS1 Q2, PYP21
Q1.2.*

## Duties to the court

### eth-w-0-17 — Paramount duty to court; honest evaluation of the merits

client (r 39(2)(b), (d), (g) PCR). Due cause for discipline includes giving or allowing the
retention, out of a fee, of any gratification for procuring legal business (s 83(2)(d)
LPA) or procuring employment through a person paid for it (s 83(2)(e) LPA). **P** Paying
referral fees to property agents to procure work is serious misconduct (*Law Society of
Singapore v Chong Wai Yen Michael* [2012] 2 SLR 113). A hyperlink from a third party's
website is allowed only if there is no financial arrangement (PD 6.1.2). **A** Here
[Lawyer] [gives a 5% fee discount for sharing the website and writing a favourable
review / pays 5% to the contact who referred the matter]. A fee discount is
consideration out of the lawyer's fee, given to procure further work, and a paid review
is also unverifiable, misleading publicity (r 44). **C** The arrangement breaches r 39
and s 83(2)(d) LPA and should be removed.

*Source note: r 39 PCR p61–62; s 83(2)(d)–(e) LPA p218; PD 6.1.2 p1; Chong Wai Yen Michael
SYL p11, L9 p20. Tested PYP21 Q3.5, PS3 Q2.*

## Fees

### eth-w-0-32 — Overcharging; fixed and non-refundable fees
*Keywords: overcharging, r 17(7), r 17(8), fixed fee, agreed fee, non-refundable deposit, disproportionate, PD 5.2.2, s 111 LPA, s 113(4) LPA, Syn Kok Kay, Andre Ravindran*

**I** The issue is whether the agreed fee of [$X] amounts to overcharging. **C** It is
[likely] overcharging. **R** A lawyer must charge fairly (r 17(1) PCR) and must not charge
a fee that is overcharging even if a fee agreement permits it (r 17(7) PCR). There is
overcharging if a reasonable lawyer could not in good faith charge the amount, given the
lawyer's standing and experience, the nature of the work, the time needed, the client's
instructions and other circumstances (r 17(8) PCR). **P** A written agreement for a gross
sum in contentious business is permitted (s 111 Legal Profession Act 1966), but it gives
no licence to charge an unreasonable fee, may be declared void if unfair or unreasonable,
and a "non-refundable" fee is not always kept regardless of work done (s 113(4) LPA; PD
5.2.2). The extent and disproportion of the overcharge are relevant to gravity (*Law
Society of Singapore v Syn Kok Kay* [2023] SGHC 7). **A** Here the fee of [$4m] is [two
thirds] of the [$6m] claim, for what appears a [straightforward claim for the price of
goods], and is payable in full even if the case settles early, when little work has been
done. Unless the complexity, seniority of counsel or client's requirements justify it,
no reasonable lawyer could charge this in good faith. **C** The term risks breaching r 17(7);
the fee should be tied to work done or reduced.

*Source note: r 17 PCR p31–33; s 111, 113(4) LPA via PD 5.2.2 p1; Syn Kok Kay SYL p12,
gravity factors Cyril §fees (secondary). Tested PYP21 Q3.1.*

### eth-w-0-33 — Contingency and success fees
*Keywords: contingency fee, success fee, bonus, r 18, s 107(1)(b) LPA, champerty, payment only in the event of success, interest in subject matter, Kurubalan, PD 5.2.1*

**I** The issue is whether the [success bonus / percentage fee] is a prohibited contingency
fee. **C** It is likely prohibited. **R** A lawyer must not negotiate with a client for an
interest in the subject matter of contentious proceedings, or for remuneration
proportionate to the amount the client may recover (r 18 PCR). A solicitor must not enter
an agreement for contentious proceedings that stipulates for or contemplates payment only
in the event of success (s 107(1)(b) Legal Profession Act 1966), and remains subject to the
law of maintenance and champerty (s 107(3) LPA). **P** The prohibition keeps the lawyer
detached enough to discharge duties to the court and client (*Law Society of Singapore v
Kurubalan s/o Manickam Rengaraju* [2013] 4 SLR 91). Fee terms that make solicitor-client
costs contingent on what is recovered can breach s 107 (PD 5.2.1). **A** Here the extra
[$500,000] is payable only if [the case settles below $500,000], so part of the fee depends
on the outcome of contentious proceedings. Even though the base fee is fixed, the bonus
gives the lawyer a personal stake in a particular result, which is the mischief in
*Kurubalan*. Contrast acting for an impecunious client knowing fees will in practice only
be recovered on success, which is permissible. **C** The bonus likely breaches s 107(1)(b)
and r 18 and should be removed.

*Source note: r 18 PCR p34; s 107 LPA p265–266; Kurubalan L10 p45–47, SYL p12; PD 5.2.1
p1; rationale Cyril §fees (secondary). TODO: verify that a success bonus on top of a fixed
fee falls within s 107(1)(b) / r 18 — no model answer in the sources. Tested PYP21 Q3.2.*

### eth-w-0-34 — Sharing fees with unauthorised persons
*Keywords: fee sharing, r 19, unauthorised person, paralegal, 50/50 split, commission, expired practising certificate, Tan See Leh Jonathan, debt collectors, PD 5.4.1*

**I** The issue is whether [Lawyer]'s arrangement with [X] shares fees with an
unauthorised person. **C** It breaches r 19 PCR. **R** A lawyer or law practice must not
share fees with, or pay a commission to, any unauthorised person for legal work performed
by the lawyer or practice (r 19(1)–(2) PCR). An unauthorised person is anyone who is not on
the roll with a current practising certificate, which includes paralegals (s 32(2) Legal
Profession Act 1966; GN 3.7.1). **P** Sharing about half the fees with a paralegal whose
practising certificate had lapsed, and who held himself out as a solicitor, was serious
misconduct (*Law Society of Singapore v Tan See Leh Jonathan* [2020] SGHC 102). Using debt
collectors to recover legal fees is also prohibited (PD 5.4.1). **A** Here [Lawyer] and
[paralegal] split [50/50] the fees on [non-contentious matters], and a further [5%] goes to
the referrer. Both are payments out of legal fees to unauthorised persons for legal work.
That [the paralegal does the work] makes it worse, not better, since it shows she is
practising in substance. **C** [Lawyer] breached r 19(1), and the 5% to the referrer also breaches
r 39(2)(b).

*Source note: r 19 PCR p35; s 32(2) LPA L8 p9; GN 3.7.1 para 2 p1; Tan See Leh Jonathan SYL
p13, L10 p54; PD 5.4.1 L10 p55. Tested PS3 Q2.*
