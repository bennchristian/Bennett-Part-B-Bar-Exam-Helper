### 0.II.3 — Amicable resolution before action (Order 5)

**PYP 2023 Q1 facts.** X Pte Ltd, a Singapore company, sold food-grade plastic containers to B Pty Ltd, an Australian food and beverage company with no presence in Singapore, under a supply contract concluded at a trade fair in Singapore which X attended and B's representative attended. The contract is governed by Singapore law and has no dispute resolution clause. X sent a letter of demand for unpaid invoices of SGD 453,000 plus contractual interest. B replied denying liability, saying the containers did not meet the Australian authorities' "food grade" classification.

**Q2023-1-2** · `0.II.3` `0.II.2` · PYP 2023 Q1.2 · 3 marks · `drp-w-0-7` `drp-w-0-6`

Are there any steps that X should take before commencing court proceedings in Singapore and, if so, what are these steps?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether X must do anything before it sues B. Yes: X must make an offer of amicable resolution before commencing the action, unless it has reasonable grounds not to (O 5 r 1(2) of the Rules of Court 2021 ("ROC")), and it should confirm that its claim is in time.

Order 5 r 1(1) gives every party a duty to consider amicable resolution before commencing and during any action, and O 5 r 1(2) states that "a party is to make an offer of amicable resolution before commencing the action unless the party has reasonable grounds not to do so". The offer may be an offer to settle, or to resolve the dispute other than by litigation, in whole or in part (O 5 r 1(3)); it must be in writing and open for acceptance for a reasonable period and in any case for at least 14 days (O 5 r 2(1)–(2)); and its terms must not be made known to the Court until it has decided the merits and is dealing with costs (O 5 r 2(3)).

Here X has sent a letter of demand, but a demand for payment in full is not an offer of amicable resolution. X should therefore write to B offering, for example, to settle at a stated sum or to mediate the "food grade" dispute (for instance at the Singapore Mediation Centre), and keep the offer open for at least 14 days. Nothing in the facts gives X reasonable grounds to skip this step: there is no urgency, no limitation problem and no suggestion that B is moving assets. If X sues without making an offer, the Court may disallow or reduce its costs even if it wins, or order it to pay costs (O 21 r 4(c)), and B must not reject the offer without reasonable grounds (O 5 r 1(4)).

X should also confirm the claim is in time: an action founded on contract must be brought within 6 years from the date the cause of action accrued (s 6(1)(a) Limitation Act 1959), which here is when payment of each invoice fell due, so the claim is well within time.

Therefore X should make a written offer of amicable resolution to B, open for at least 14 days, before commencing proceedings, failing which it risks an adverse costs order under O 21 r 4(c).

### 0.II.4 — Dispute resolution clauses

**PYP 2025 Q1 facts.** X Pte Ltd, a Singapore company, supplied medical masks to A Pty Ltd, an Australian company, for use in Australia and elsewhere. The supply contract is governed by Singapore law, and the parties agreed to submit to the **non-exclusive** jurisdiction of the Singapore courts. A accepted the masks but has not paid; SGD 750,000 is owed, excluding contractual interest. X wants to sue in the Singapore courts.

**Q2025-1-1** · `0.II.4` `0.II.3` `0.I.1` · PYP 2025 Q1.1 · 4 marks · `drp-w-0-8` `drp-w-0-7` `drp-w-0-1`

X has asked whether the proceedings can be commenced immediately. Please advise X, including whether there are any steps that X should take before the commencement of proceedings and, if so, what these steps are and why they should be taken.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether X may sue A at once. X may sue in the General Division, which the non-exclusive Singapore jurisdiction clause makes available, but it should not file immediately: it must first make an offer of amicable resolution under O 5 r 1(2) ROC, unless it has reasonable grounds not to.

**Which court, and the clause.** The claim of SGD 750,000 exceeds the District Court limit of $250,000 (ss 2, 19(4) State Courts Act 1970), so X sues in the General Division of the High Court. The General Division has jurisdiction in personam over A if A is served outside Singapore in the circumstances authorised by the Rules of Court, or submits (s 16(1)(a)(ii), (b) Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1969 ("SCJA")). The clause is non-exclusive: A has agreed that Singapore is an available forum, which supports the Court's jurisdiction and is a nexus factor for service out (PD 63(3)(d)(iii)–(iv) of the Supreme Court Practice Directions 2021: the contract is governed by Singapore law and contains a term that the Court will have jurisdiction), but A may still argue that another forum, such as Australia, is more appropriate. X should therefore expect to make the full case for approval to serve out (see Q2025-1-2).

**Order 5.** O 5 r 1(2) states that "a party is to make an offer of amicable resolution before commencing the action unless the party has reasonable grounds not to do so". The offer must be in writing and open for at least 14 days (O 5 r 2(1)–(2)). X has not yet made one; nothing suggests urgency (no limitation issue, as the 6-year period under s 6(1)(a) Limitation Act runs from the payment date, and no sign of dissipation). X should therefore offer, in writing, to settle (for example payment by instalments) or to mediate, and wait at least 14 days. The reason is costs: if X sues without doing so, the Court may disallow or reduce X's costs, or order X to pay costs, even if X wins (O 21 r 4(c); PD 53(6)).

Therefore X cannot sensibly commence proceedings immediately: it should first make a written offer of amicable resolution open for at least 14 days, and then commence an originating claim in the General Division, relying on the non-exclusive clause when it applies for approval to serve A in Australia.

### 0.II.5 — Pre-action production of documents and information

**PYP 2021 facts.** Polly ("P") was one of 20 contestants in a game show produced by Doodle 1 Pty Ltd ("D1"), an Australian production company; the host, Mr Doodle Tu ("D2"), is D1's sole director and shareholder. P's contract with D1 said the winner takes home US$500,000 cash and has travel and accommodation reimbursed (cl 2), and that the contract is governed by Singapore law with both parties submitting to the **exclusive** jurisdiction of the Singapore courts (cl 3). P won; despite chasers, she has not been paid. You act for P. The paper tells candidates always to address costs.

**Q2021-1** · `0.II.5` `0.IX.6` `0.XIV.2` · PYP 2021 Q1 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-9` `drp-w-0-72`

On 1 August 2021 you advised P to sue D1 for the US$500,000 under cl 2 ("Clause 2 Claim"). P asks whether she should also sue D1 and D2 in the tort of conspiracy. She recently discovered that D2 is the majority shareholder of all the Featured Restaurants. She has no concrete evidence but is convinced the show was a scheme by D1 and D2 to publicise the restaurants and that D1 never intended to pay; D1 is a shell company with few assets. Advise P on what application(s) she may take out to assess the viability of a conspiracy claim against D1 and D2, the procedures, the prospects of success of each, and costs.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether P can obtain documents or information from D1 and D2 to decide whether a conspiracy claim is viable before she sues on it. She may apply under O 11 r 11 ROC (formerly pre-action discovery and interrogatories under ROC 2014), but on these facts the application is likely to fail, because she can already plead and pursue the Clause 2 Claim and her conspiracy case is a bare theory.

**Rule.** O 11 r 11(1) states that the Court may order the production of documents and information before the commencement of proceedings or against a non-party "to identify possible parties to any proceedings, to enable a party to trace the party's property, or for any other lawful purpose, in the interests of justice". The Court must not order production that cannot be compelled in law (r 11(2)), and a non-party is entitled to all reasonable costs of the application (r 11(3)). The applicant must show that the documents or information are relevant, and necessary for it to commence proceedings or to decide whether it has a viable claim (*ED&F Man Capital Markets Ltd v Straits (Singapore) Pte Ltd* [2020] 2 SLR 695; *Gillingham James Ian v Fearless Legends Pte Ltd* [2024] 6 SLR 697 at [14], holding that O 11 r 11 has the same rationale as its ROC 2014 predecessor). No order is made if there is no likely prospect of proceedings in Singapore (*ED&F Man* at [34]–[36]). Production may be ordered where a prospective claimant lacks the information to know whether it has a viable claim or whom to sue, but not where it can already plead its case; the Court weighs proportionality, other ways of getting the information and the intrusion (*Dorsey James Michael v World Sport Group Pte Ltd* [2014] 2 SLR 208).

**Application.** The purpose falls under "any other lawful purpose": P wants to know whether D1 and D2 combined to injure her and whether D1 ever intended to pay. Singapore proceedings are likely, since cl 3 gives the Singapore courts exclusive jurisdiction. But the application is weak. First, P has only a suspicion based on D2's shareholding in the restaurants; the Court will see a request for D1's and D2's internal communications as a fishing expedition. Second, P already has a complete cause of action on cl 2 against D1; she does not need production to sue, and once that action is on foot she can seek production within it (O 11 rr 2–3; see Q2021-5). Third, D2 is in Australia; the Court would also weigh the intrusion of compelling a foreign individual. If P applies, she should confine the request to specific, identified documents, such as D1's board resolutions approving the prize and its bank records at the time of the contract, which go to whether D1 could ever pay.

**Procedure.** Because no action on the conspiracy claim exists, the application is made by originating application in Form 15 with a supporting affidavit (O 6 r 1(3)(b), r 11), served personally (O 6 r 4); serving it on D1 and D2 in Australia needs the Court's approval under O 8 r 1. Once the Clause 2 action is pending, an application against D2 as a non-party is made by summons within that action, in the single application pending trial unless the Court approves an earlier application (O 9 r 9(4)(k), (8)).

**Costs.** No party recovers costs without a court order (O 21 r 3(1)), and costs follow the event (O 21 r 3(2)). If P fails, she will be ordered to pay D1's and D2's costs of the application. If the application succeeds against D2 as a non-party, P must still pay D2's reasonable costs of it (O 11 r 11(3)), though she may try to recover them later as part of her costs of the action.

Therefore P could apply under O 11 r 11 for specific documents bearing on D1's intention and ability to pay, but the application is unlikely to succeed on a bare theory when she can already sue on cl 2, and she would probably bear the costs. She should proceed with the Clause 2 Claim and use production within that action.

### 0.III.3 — Issue, validity and renewal of the originating process

**PYP 2023 Q1 facts.** As for Q2023-1-2 above (X Pte Ltd v B Pty Ltd, Australian buyer of food-grade containers, SGD 453,000 unpaid). X has obtained approval to serve out.

**Q2023-1-3** · `0.III.3` `0.IV.4` · PYP 2023 Q1.3 · 2 marks · `drp-w-0-12` `drp-w-0-17`

The originating claim was issued on 23 July 2023. X took some time to confirm its instructions on service. It is now 21 October 2023, and the Australian law firm you engaged needs at least another week to serve B. What would you advise X to do and why?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether X's originating claim will still be valid when it is served on B in Australia around 28 October. On the current rule it will be, so no extension is needed, but X must explain its delay in taking steps to serve.

O 6 r 3(1) ROC, as amended by S 780/2023 with effect from 1 December 2023, states that an originating claim is valid for service "(a) where it is to be served out of Singapore (i) with the court's approval under Order 8, Rule 1(2) … for 6 months beginning with the date of its issue; or (b) in any other case, for 3 months". Validity may be extended on application made before or after expiry (O 6 r 3(2)), and except in a special case only twice and by not more than 3 months each time (O 6 r 3(4)); the power is exercised only for good reason, considering all the circumstances, including difficulty in effecting service and the balance of hardship (*Lim Hong Kan v Mohd Sainudin bin Ahmad* [1992] SGCA 8 at [17]).

Here the originating claim, issued on 23 July 2023 and to be served out with approval, is valid until 22 January 2024 under the current rule, so service in late October is in time. **When the paper was set** the period was 3 months in every case, so the claim expired on 22 October 2023; the answer the paper expected was that X must apply at once, on 21 October, under O 6 r 3(2) to extend validity by up to 3 months, giving as good reason that service is being effected abroad through Australian lawyers and that B suffers no prejudice from a week's delay. Either way, X has a second problem: reasonable steps to serve out of Singapore must be made as soon as possible and in any event within 28 days after issue (O 6 r 5(7)), that is by 20 August 2023, and "taking time to confirm instructions" is not a reasonable step. If the claim is still unserved at the first case conference, the Court may dismiss the action if it is not satisfied that X took reasonable steps to serve expeditiously (O 9 r 5(1)(a)). X should be ready with an affidavit of its service steps (PD 64(3)).

Therefore, on the current law, X needs no extension (validity runs to 22 January 2024) but should serve now and document why it did not act within 28 days; on the law the paper assumed, X had to apply under O 6 r 3(2) on 21 October to extend validity before it expired.

### 0.IV.2 — Substituted service

**PYP 2022 facts.** Christopher ("C"), a Singapore investor, lent Desmond ("D") US$4 million in two tranches under Investment Agreements 1 and 2, each repayable on 3 March 2022 with a US$1 million "investment return". D did not pay. On 7 March 2022 they signed a Settlement Agreement under which D would pay US$5.5 million on 1 June 2022 in full and final settlement (cl 4). D did not pay and rejected C's offer of amicable resolution. On 1 July 2022 C issued an Originating Claim in the General Division against D for US$5.5 million. You act for C. The paper tells candidates always to address costs.

**Q2022-1** · `0.IV.2` `0.III.3` `0.IV.1` `0.IV.4` · PYP 2022 Q1 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-15` `drp-w-0-12` `drp-w-0-14` `drp-w-0-17`

Your firm tried once, on 6 July 2022, to serve the Originating Claim on D at his home in Singapore; D was not there. C then told you to hold off because his son was in settlement talks with D. It is now 26 September 2022; the talks have failed and C expects D to evade service and drag the case out. A junior colleague writes: "Don't worry. We have 6 months from the date of issue … to effect service. The Originating Claim was issued on 1 July 2022. So we have a lot more time. As for effecting service quickly, we should just go ahead and apply to the Court for an order for substituted service. Client tells us that D will likely evade service and we failed once already." Are your colleague's views accurate? Cite the ROC 2021 and the Supreme Court Practice Directions 2021.

**Answer.**

The issues are how long the Originating Claim is valid for service on D in Singapore, and whether C can apply for substituted service now. The colleague is wrong on both: the claim expires on 30 September 2022, so C must apply to extend its validity now; and C cannot yet apply for substituted service, because only one attempt at personal service has been made.

**1. Validity.** An originating claim must be served personally on each defendant (O 6 r 4), by leaving a copy with the individual (O 7 r 2(1)(a)). O 6 r 3(1)(b) states that an originating claim not to be served out of Singapore is valid for service "for 3 months beginning with the date of its issue". The 6-month period in O 6 r 3(1)(a) applies only to process served out of Singapore. D is in Singapore, so the claim, issued on 1 July 2022, is valid until 30 September 2022, four days from now. C should apply at once under O 6 r 3(2), which allows an application to extend validity before or after it expires, for an extension of up to 3 months (O 6 r 3(4)). The Court extends only for good reason, considering all the circumstances, including difficulty in effecting service and the balance of hardship (*Lim Hong Kan v Mohd Sainudin bin Ahmad* [1992] SGCA 8 at [17]). C's reason is weak: service was suspended for settlement talks, not because D could not be found. C should explain that the talks were a genuine attempt at amicable resolution, which O 5 r 1(1) encourages, that D knew of the claim throughout, and that D suffers no prejudice; C should also be candid that the delay was its own choice.

**2. Reasonable steps.** O 6 r 5(6) requires reasonable steps to serve an originating claim in Singapore "as soon as possible and, in any event, within 14 days after the originating claim is issued". C made one attempt on 6 July and then stopped for nearly three months. If the claim is unserved at the case conference, the Court may dismiss the action if it is not satisfied that C took reasonable steps to serve expeditiously, or order service or an application for substituted service within 14 days (O 9 r 5(1)); whether steps were reasonable depends on the mode, the day and time of the attempts, their number, the reasons they failed and whether substituted service has been sought (PD 64(2)).

**3. Substituted service.** O 7 r 7(1) states that "if a document is required to be served personally and it is impractical to serve it personally, a party may apply to serve it by substituted service", and the Court may order any method effective in bringing the document to the person's notice, including electronic means (O 7 r 7(2)). But PD 65(2) states that "2 reasonable attempts at personal service should be made before an application for substituted service is filed", and the applicant must show by affidavit why the attempts were reasonable. C's belief that D will evade service is not an attempt. C must first make a second, reasonable attempt, at a different time of day (for example an evening or weekend), and only if it fails apply by summons (which needs no prior approval: O 9 r 9(7)(b)) with an affidavit describing each attempt, giving evidence that D lives at the address (for example HDB, SLA or IRAS search results: PD 65(4)), and proposing a mode likely to reach him, such as posting on the door with AR registered post, or email to an address D actively uses (PD 65(3), (6)). Substituted service must then be effected within 14 days after the order (O 7 r 7(3)).

**Costs.** The costs of an application to extend time are borne by the applicant unless the Court otherwise orders (O 21 r 3(4)), so C will bear the costs of the extension. C should ask for the costs of the substituted service application to be costs in the cause, so that it recovers them if it wins (O 21 rr 2(1), 3(2)).

Therefore the colleague is wrong: C must apply now under O 6 r 3(2) to extend validity beyond 30 September 2022, make a second reasonable attempt at personal service immediately, and apply for substituted service under O 7 r 7 with a PD 65 affidavit only if that attempt fails.

**PYP 2025 Q5 facts (Written Advocacy question).** Mr Leonard Tan let his friend Mr Desmond Lim live rent-free in Mr Tan's flat at Block 89A Lemon Lane, #11-321 from 1 July to 31 December 2025. Mr Tan sold the flat (completion 9 February 2026); Mr Lim refused to leave, and Mr Tan sued in the District Court (DC/OC 2025/2025) for vacant possession. The firm's clerk tried to serve the originating claim, statement of claim and Form 10 notice at the flat on 2 December 2025 at 8.00pm (no answer after 10 minutes) and on 4 December 2025 at 7.45pm (a middle-aged man said Mr Lim was in Malaysia and he was unsure when he would be back). Mr Tan knows of no email address Mr Lim now uses (only an old one used some years ago), Mr Lim has no social media or messaging apps, and Mr Tan does not know his NRIC number (so service via SingPass is difficult). The draft summons asks for substituted service by posting at the door of the flat, with costs in the cause. The paper quotes O 7 r 7 and para 28 of the State Courts Practice Directions 2021 (the State Courts counterpart of SCPD PD 65).

**Q2025-5** · `0.IV.2` `0.IV.4` `0.IV.3` · PYP 2025 Q5 (Written Advocacy), Civil Litigation points only · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-15` `drp-w-0-17` `drp-w-0-16`

[The paper asks for the supporting affidavit. Civil Litigation version:] Can Mr Tan now apply for substituted service by posting at the door, and what must the supporting affidavit show?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether Mr Tan has met the conditions for substituted service and what his affidavit must prove. He may apply now, because two reasonable attempts at personal service have failed, but the affidavit must deal with the report that Mr Lim is in Malaysia, and he should propose AR registered post in addition to posting at the door.

O 7 r 7(1) states that if a document must be served personally "and it is impractical to serve it personally, a party may apply to serve it by substituted service"; the Court may order any method "effective in bringing the document to the notice of the person to be served" (r 7(2)), and service must be effected within 14 days after the order (r 7(3)). Para 28 of the State Courts Practice Directions requires the applicant to satisfy the Court that the proposed mode is effective (para 28(1)); 2 reasonable attempts at personal service should be made first, and the affidavit must show why the attempts were reasonable (para 28(2)); other modes such as AR registered post or email should be considered (para 28(3)); posting or AR registered post needs evidence that the person is the owner of, resident at or can be located at the address (para 28(4)); and email service needs proof that the account is the person's and is currently active (para 28(6)).

Here two attempts were made on different days, both in the evening when a working occupant is most likely to be home, which supports reasonableness (compare SCPD PD 64(2)(b), which makes the day and time of attempts a relevant factor). The affidavit must set out each attempt (date, time, who answered and what was said, with the clerk's service endorsements exhibited), and the WhatsApp messages of 17 June 2025 in which Mr Lim thanked Mr Tan for letting him stay at the flat and said he would move in on 1 July, to show he lives there. It must candidly disclose what the occupant said on 4 December, because an applicant must put all material facts before the Court; the Court must be satisfied that posting at the door will bring the papers to Mr Lim's notice although he is said to be away for an unknown period. Mr Tan should therefore propose posting at the door together with AR registered post to the flat, and explain why the other modes are not available: the only email address is years old and cannot be shown to be active (para 28(6)), Mr Lim uses no social media or messaging apps, and SingPass service is not practicable without his NRIC number. Posting on the State Courts' notice board is not an available mode (para 28(8)). If the affidavit shows that Mr Lim has left Singapore for good, the safer course is service out of Singapore with approval (O 8 r 1) or an application to dispense with personal service (O 7 r 1(2); *Madison Pacific Trust Ltd v PT Dewata Wibawa* [2024] SGHC 184 at [56]–[58]), not substituted service at a Singapore address.

Note on timing: service effected after 5pm is deemed effected the next day (O 7 r 8(2)), so if the clerk posts the papers after 5pm, every deadline (including the 21 days for Mr Lim's notice of intention to contest) runs from the following day.

Therefore Mr Tan may apply for substituted service, by summons with a supporting affidavit that sets out both attempts, proves Mr Lim's residence at the flat, discloses the Malaysia report, and asks for posting at the door plus AR registered post, with the costs of the application in the cause.

### 0.IV.5 — Service out of Singapore: the Court's approval

**PYP 2021 facts.** See Q2021-1 under 0.II.5 (P v D1, Australian game-show producer; cl 3 exclusive Singapore jurisdiction, Singapore law).

**Q2021-2** · `0.IV.5` `0.IV.6` `0.III.3` `0.IV.1` `0.IV.4` `0.II.4` · PYP 2021 Q2 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-18` `drp-w-0-19` `drp-w-0-12` `drp-w-0-14` `drp-w-0-17` `drp-w-0-8`

P decides to pursue only the Clause 2 Claim. On 1 September 2021 you issued a writ endorsed with a statement of claim against D1 in the General Division. P asks what she must do to serve it on D1 and how long she has. Advise P. [Under ROC 2021 the writ is an originating claim.]

**Answer.**

The issue is how P can validly serve the originating claim on D1, an Australian company, and by when. **Answer.**

The issue is how and when Z, served in Australia, must raise and pursue its challenge to the Singapore Court's jurisdiction. Z must file a notice of intention to contest, then a defence stating the jurisdictional ground, and then, on the Court's directions at the case conference, an application with supporting affidavit, which is heard within 14 days after affidavits close.

**1. Notice of intention.** Z must file and serve a notice of intention to contest or not contest within 21 days after service of the statement of claim (O 6 r 6(2) ROC), in Form 10 (O 6 r 6(3)). This is not a submission to jurisdiction or a waiver of any improper service (O 6 r 6(4)).

**2. Defence stating the ground.** Z must file and serve a defence within 5 weeks after service of the statement of claim (O 6 r 7(2)). Z should file its Pre-Case Conference Questionnaire about 1 week before (PD 56(2)). Unless the Court otherwise orders, the hearing will be fixed on a date no later than 14 days after all affidavits have been filed and served (O 9 r 7(2)(c)). Z should therefore put all its evidence on forum and service into its first affidavit. If the challenge fails, the Court will direct Z to file a defence on the merits (*Beltran* at [51]).

Therefore Z's timeline is: Form 10 notice within 21 days of service of the statement of claim; a defence stating the jurisdictional ground within 5 weeks; the first case conference about 12 weeks after issue; Z's application and affidavit within 14 days of that conference; A's reply affidavit 14 days later; and the hearing within 14 days after that.

The issue is how C may amend its Statement of Claim to claim 7% contractual interest under cl 17, before the SAPT. C should first ask D to agree in writing; failing that, C needs the Court's permission, which it must seek either in its SAPT or, if it wants to do so now, after obtaining the Court's approval by letter.

Without an amendment, C can at most ask the Court to award interest in its discretion; to recover 7% under cl 17 from 1 June 2022 it must plead it.

**Ground.** O 9 r 12(1) allows a defendant to apply for security if the claimant is ordinarily resident out of the jurisdiction, is a nominal or non-party-funded claimant who may not be able to pay, or has misstated or changed its address to evade the consequences of the litigation. X is a Singapore company, so r 12(1)(a) does not apply. But O 9 r 12(2) states that "if the claimant is a company, section 388 of the Companies Act also applies", and s 388(1) provides that where a corporation is claimant, the court may, "if it appears by credible testimony that there is reason to believe that the corporation will be unable to pay the costs of the defendant if successful in the defendant's defence, require sufficient security to be given for those costs and stay all proceedings until the security is given". Market rumours are not credible testimony, but X's audited financial statements filed with ACRA, showing losses for two years, are; B should exhibit them in its affidavit.

X's claim for unpaid invoices looks strong on the documents, which weighs against security, but B's "food grade" defence is a genuine dispute and two years of losses with no evidence of assets suggest a costs order may go unpaid, which weighs for it. The Court is likely to order security in an amount proportionate to B's likely costs, staying the action until it is given.

**When.** Security for costs is a SAPT matter (O 9 r 9(4)(d)) and is not on the list of applications that need no approval (O 9 r 9(7)). The SAPT is directed only after any jurisdictional challenge is dealt with and after any AEICs-before-documents order (O 9 rr 7, 8, 9(1)), and the applicant files its SAPT and affidavit within 21 days after the case conference, with a reply 21 days later (O 9 r 9(5)). Since B wants to move as soon as possible, once the proceedings are to continue in Singapore it may write to the Court for approval to apply earlier, by a Request (Form B9) setting out the essence of the application and why it is necessary at that stage (O 9 r 9(8); PD 66(6)–(8)), pointing to the costs B will incur before the SAPT. Delay counts against an applicant, so B should not wait. But while B's challenge to jurisdiction is pending, it should not apply: seeking relief in the action beyond the challenge risks being treated as a submission to the jurisdiction (s 16(1)(b) SCJA).

Therefore B can seek security for costs under O 9 r 12(2) and s 388 of the Companies Act, relying on X's audited accounts; it should raise it at the first case conference after its jurisdiction challenge is resolved and either seek the Court's approval by Form B9 to apply at once or include it in its SAPT, asking for the action to be stayed until security is given.

Summary judgment is her best route and needs no prior approval, but must be filed by 29 November 2021; particulars are the safe fallback.

**1. Prospects are good.

The bar is high: the defence must be obviously unsustainable (*Envy Asset Management Pte Ltd v Lau Lee Sheng* [2024] SGHC 38 at [17]–[18]). Under (a) no evidence is admissible (O 9 r 16(2)), and a repudiatory breach by cheating is a defence known to law, so (a) fails. Under (b), a sham defence is an abuse (*Chee Siok Chin v Minister for Home Affairs* [2006] 1 SLR(R) 582 at [34]), but P has only her own denial and the general point that contestants were watched; that is not enough to show a sham at this stage. Prospects are poor.

**3. Summary judgment.** O 9 r 17(1) allows a claimant, after the defence is filed and served, to apply for summary judgment "on the ground that the defendant has no defence to (a) a claim". The summary judgment hearing also forces D1 to put its evidence of cheating on affidavit, which serves the purpose of particulars.

**The test.** The Court considers the stage of the proceedings, whether the amendment enables the real controversy to be decided, and whether it is just to allow it, focusing on prejudice not compensable in costs and second bites at the cherry (*Wang Piao v Lee Wee Ching* [2024] 4 SLR 540 at [16]–[19]); and it may draw inferences from amended material facts (O 9 r 14(6)).

Therefore Mr X should first seek Mrs Y's written agreement; failing that, apply for permission in his SAPT, or earlier or later with the Court's approval by Form B9 Request; and in any event before the 14 days preceding trial, after which only a special case will do.

### 0.VII.7 — Amendment: when permission is granted

**PYP 2023 Q2 facts.** See Q2023-2-1 under 0.V.1. Mr A cannot recall the specifics of Mrs Y's representations and says it will take time to dig through his records.

**Q2023-2-2** · `0.VII.7` `0.VII.6` `0.V.1` · PYP 2023 Q2.2 · 4 marks · `drp-w-0-38` `drp-w-0-37` `drp-w-0-20`

**Answer.**

The issue is whether Mr A should plead an oral representation at a viewing that he is not sure was made, intending to amend later. He should not, for two reasons: first, he must certify the statement of claim as true; second, a later change to that material fact may lead the Court to draw adverse inferences, and permission to amend is not guaranteed.

**Difficulty 1: certification of truth.** A statement of claim in Form 9 ends with the claimant's certification that all statements in it are true to the best of his knowledge and belief, and the solicitor's certification that he has informed the claimant of that obligation. Mr A cannot certify as true an allegation he is not sure of; pleading a "placeholder" representation is a certification he cannot honestly give, and the solicitor cannot put forward a fact the client has said he is unsure of. The particulars of a misrepresentation are material facts which Form 9 para 2(b) requires; they must be pleaded accurately, not guessed.

**Difficulty 2: adverse inferences and the risk that permission is refused.** O 9 r 14(6) ROC states that "if material facts in the pleadings are amended, the Court may draw the appropriate inferences". If Mr A later changes "orally at a viewing" to, say, "in a WhatsApp message", Mrs Y will say his case has shifted to fit the documents, and the Court may infer that his recollection, and his account of reliance, are unreliable. That damages his credibility on the central issue in a misrepresentation claim. Further, the amendment is not as of right: without Mrs Y's written agreement (O 9 r 14(5)), Mr A needs permission (O 9 r 14(1)), and the Court considers the stage of the proceedings, whether the amendment raises the real issue, and whether it is just, including prejudice to Mrs Y not compensable in costs (*Wang Piao v Lee Wee Ching* [2024] 4 SLR 540 at [16]–[19]); if the change comes late, for example after AEICs, the Court may refuse it, and within 14 days of trial it may be allowed only in a special case (O 9 r 14(3)).

Therefore Mr A should not plead an uncertain oral representation "for now"; he should check his records first and plead only what he can certify, because a later amendment of that material fact invites adverse inferences under O 9 r 14(6) and may be refused.

### 0.VIII.1 — Striking out: when permission is needed; no reasonable cause of action

**PYP 2022 facts.** See Q2022-1 under 0.IV.2 (C v D, Settlement Agreement for US$5.5 million). The Originating Claim has been served. D's solicitors have filed a Defence pleading that Investment Agreements 1 and 2 were illegal moneylending transactions, and that D entered the Settlement Agreement under duress.

**Q2022-2** · `0.VIII.1` `0.VIII.2` `0.VIII.3` `0.VIII.4` `0.VII.1` `0.VII.2` · PYP 2022 Q2 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-40` `drp-w-0-41` `drp-w-0-42` `drp-w-0-43` `drp-w-0-32` `drp-w-0-33`

You advise C that the defences lack merit. C asks whether any application may be taken out to expedite the resolution of the case. He recalls your earlier advice that most interlocutory applications must be brought as part of the single application pending trial, and asks whether he must wait for the SAPT before filing any application to expedite matters. He also asks for the timelines and procedures. Advise C.

**Answer.**

The issues are which applications can bring C's claim to an early end, and whether C must wait for the SAPT. C should apply for summary judgment under O 9 r 17 ROC, and may also apply to strike out the whole defence under O 9 r 16; neither needs to wait for the SAPT or the Court's approval, but summary judgment must be filed within 28 days after the defence was served.

**1. No need to wait.** O 9 r 9(7) states that "no application may be taken out by any party at any time other than as directed at the case conference or with the Court's approval, except an application for … (g) summary judgment; (h) striking out of the whole of an action or defence". The SAPT (O 9 r 9(2)–(4)) is for other interlocutory matters; striking out only part of a defence would be a SAPT matter (O 9 r 9(4)(h)).

**2. Summary judgment.** After the defence is filed and served, the claimant may apply for summary judgment "on the ground that the defendant has no defence to (a) a claim" (O 9 r 17(1)). Unless the Court permits otherwise, the application may not be made more than 28 days after service of the defence (O 9 r 17(13)), so C must file within 28 days of the date D's defence was served. Procedure: a summons (Form 1) with an affidavit containing all the evidence necessary or material to the claim (O 9 r 17(2)): the Investment Agreements, the transfers, D's request for time on 3 March 2022, the Settlement Agreement signed on 7 March 2022, D's failure to pay on 1 June and his rejection of C's offer. D must file his affidavit within 14 days after service if he disputes the application (O 9 r 17(3)–(4)); C may reply within 14 days (O 9 r 17(5)); no further affidavits without approval (O 9 r 17(6)).

**The test.** C must show a prima facie case, after which D must show a triable issue or some other reason for trial; mere assertions, or assertions that are equivocal, imprecise or inherently improbable, do not raise a triable issue (*M2B World Asia Pacific Pte Ltd v Matsumura Akihiko* [2015] 1 SLR 325 at [17]–[19]); a bare point of law that is plainly misconceived does not resist summary judgment (*Horizon Capital Fund v Ollech David* [2023] SGHC 164 at [58]–[60]). C's prima facie case is the signed Settlement Agreement and non-payment. D's duress defence is a bare assertion unless D gives evidence of illegitimate pressure; the documents show that D himself asked for time on 3 March and then signed a compromise four days later; D must give evidence of illegitimate pressure, not merely assert it. The illegality defence attacks the Investment Agreements, but C sues on the Settlement Agreement, a compromise D entered into of his own accord; D must show why it is tainted. If D's affidavit gives no substance to either defence, the Court will grant judgment (O 9 r 17(7)(c)); if a defence is dubious, it may grant permission to defend on conditions, such as payment of the sum into court (O 9 r 17(7)(d)).

**3. Striking out.** The Court may strike out a pleading that "(a) discloses no reasonable cause of action or defence; (b) is an abuse of process of the Court; or (c) [where] it is in the interests of justice to do so" (O 9 r 16(1)). Under (a) no evidence is admissible (O 9 r 16(2)), and illegality and duress are defences known to law, so this limb fails if they are properly pleaded. Under (b), C's impression from the settlement talks that D is only playing for time is suspicion, not evidence of a collateral purpose. Under (c), a plainly unsustainable defence may be struck out (*Iskandar bin Rahmat v Attorney-General* [2022] 2 SLR 1018 at [19]). The bar is high: the defence must be obviously unsustainable (*Envy Asset Management Pte Ltd v Lau Lee Sheng* [2024] SGHC 38 at [17]). Striking out is a useful alternative prayer in the same summons, but summary judgment, where evidence is admissible, is the better route for a factual defence like duress.

**Costs.** Costs follow the event (O 21 r 3(2)): if C succeeds, he will be awarded the costs of the application and the action, fixed by the Court, which may take into account D's conduct in raising unmeritorious defences (O 21 r 2(2)(f)); if the Court gives D conditional permission to defend, it may decide the costs of the application then or reserve them to the end of the action (O 21 r 2(1)).

Therefore C need not wait for the SAPT: he should file a summary judgment application under O 9 r 17 within 28 days after service of D's defence, with an alternative prayer to strike out the whole defence under O 9 r 16(1)(b)–(c), and is likely to obtain judgment or at least conditional permission to defend.

### 0.VIII.3 — Summary judgment: when to apply and the affidavit timetable

The issue is how P can obtain judgment without a trial when R's only defence, an agreed variation, is disproved by the correspondence. P should apply for summary judgment under O 9 r 17 ROC; it needs no permission, but must be filed within 28 days after service of the defence, by 29 November 2023.

**Permission.** None is needed. O 9 r 9(7) lists the applications that may be taken out at any time without being directed at a case conference or approved by the Court, including "(g) summary judgment". P need not wait for the SAPT.

**Procedural steps and timelines.**
(1) Timing: "except where the Court otherwise permits, an application under this Rule may not be made after the expiry of 28 days after the date of service of the defence" (O 9 r 17(13)). The defence was served on 1 November 2023, so the period begins on 2 November and P must file by 29 November 2023 (O 3 r 3(3)).
(2) P files a summons (Form 1) with a supporting affidavit containing all the evidence necessary or material to the claim (O 9 r 17(2)): the Contract with the Delivery Clause, proof of payment of SGD 400,000, the chasers, and the correspondence showing the variation was discussed but never agreed.
(3) If R disputes the application, it must file and serve its affidavit, containing all evidence necessary or material to its defence, within 14 days after service of P's application and affidavit (O 9 r 17(3)–(4)).
(4) P may file a reply affidavit within 14 days after service of R's affidavit (O 9 r 17(5)).
(5) No further affidavits may be filed without the Court's approval (O 9 r 17(6)); the Court then hears the application.

**Prospects.** P must show a prima facie case; the burden then shifts to R to show a triable issue or some other reason for trial, and mere assertions, or assertions that are equivocal, imprecise or inherently improbable, will not suffice (*M2B World Asia Pacific Pte Ltd v Matsumura Akihiko* [2015] 1 SLR 325 at [17]–[19]). P's payment and R's non-delivery make out a prima facie case. R's variation is a bare assertion contradicted by the correspondence, which shows the variation was never agreed, so it raises no triable issue, and the Court is likely to grant judgment for repayment of SGD 400,000 (O 9 r 17(7)(c)). P may add, in the same summons, an alternative prayer to strike out the whole defence (O 9 r 16(1)(b)–(c)), which also needs no approval (O 9 r 9(7)(h)).

Therefore P should file a summary judgment application under O 9 r 17, without the Court's permission, by 29 November 2023, supported by an affidavit exhibiting the correspondence; R then has 14 days to respond and P 14 days to reply, and judgment is likely.

### 0.IX.2 — Initial production: when, scope, known adverse documents

**PYP 2023 Q3 facts.** See Q2023-3-1 under 0.VIII.3 (Company P v Company R; R's defence alleges an agreed variation making delivery "subject to goods being in stock"). P did not apply for summary judgment, and the Court has directed the parties to exchange lists of documents under O 11 r 2. P now tells you that, after the Contract was signed, a P employee emailed an R employee: "Your side's delivery is naturally subject to stock status". The email has only recently surfaced to P's management. An intern says the email "need not be included in Company P's list of documents because Company R, being the recipient of the email, would itself have a copy of the email".

**Q2023-3-2** · `0.IX.2` `0.IX.7` · PYP 2023 Q3.2 · 5 marks · `drp-w-0-46` `drp-w-0-50`

Is the intern correct?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether P may leave out of its list an email that undermines its case because R, the recipient, already has it. The intern is not correct: the email is a known adverse document that P must disclose under O 11 r 2(1)(b) ROC, and the rule on common documents does not allow P to conceal its existence.

**Known adverse document.** O 11 r 2(1) allows the Court to order the parties, within 14 days after the case conference, to "exchange a list of and a copy of all documents in their possession or control which fall within one or more of the following categories: (a) all documents that the party in question will be relying on; (b) all known adverse documents". "Known adverse documents" include "documents which a party ought reasonably to know are adverse to the party's case" (O 11 r 2(2)). P's case is that the Delivery Clause was never varied; an email from P's own employee saying delivery is "naturally subject to stock status" supports R's variation defence. P's management now knows of it, so it is a known adverse document.

**The common-documents rule.** O 11 r 2(4) states that "it is not necessary for the parties to exchange documents common to them that are in their possession or control, to avoid duplication and to save costs". This spares the parties from exchanging *copies* of documents both already hold; it is not a licence to hide that a known adverse document exists. Production is made by exchanging "a list of and a copy of" the documents, and the list fixes the scope of what has been produced (*Hu Yinan v Peh Chin Hua* [2026] SGHC 103 at [24]–[25]). Whether the list entry itself may be omitted for a common document is an **open point, with no authority**: on one view, r 2(4) speaks only of not exchanging "documents", so the list must still identify the email; on the other, a document common to both parties need not be listed at all. The rule's purpose (avoiding duplication) is served by not sending another copy, and the safe course, consistent with the duty to disclose known adverse documents, is to list the email. Further, P cannot assume R still has the email in its possession or control, or will identify it.

**Timing and consequences.** If the email surfaced only after the lists were exchanged, P must produce it within 14 days after it came into P's possession or control (O 11 r 6). If P withholds it, the Court may strike out P's claim, draw an adverse inference, punish for contempt, or bar reliance on documents within the order (O 11 r 7).

Therefore the intern is wrong: P must disclose the email as a known adverse document, at least by listing it, and, if it surfaced after the exchange, within 14 days under O 11 r 6.

The issue is how P can obtain surveillance footage held either by D1, a party, or by the Featured Restaurants, which are not parties. **Against D1: requested documents.** O 11 r 3(1) allows the Court to order a party to produce "a specific document or class of documents … in the party's possession or control, if the requesting party (a) properly identifies the requested documents; and (b) shows that the requested documents are material to the issues in the case". Recordings are documents (*Sanae Achar v Sci-Gen Ltd* [2011] 3 SLR 967 at [10]). P should also point out that if D1 relies on footage to found its allegation, it should have listed it (O 11 r 2(1)(a)); and if D1 holds footage showing P did not cheat, it is a known adverse document D1 had to list (O 11 r 2(1)(b)). **Against the restaurants: non-party production.** O 11 r 11(1) allows the Court to order production "against a non-party … for any other lawful purpose, in the interests of justice", and the Court may also, of its own accord and at any time, order any non-party to produce a document in its possession or control (O 11 r 4). P must show the footage is relevant and necessary to the issues (*Gillingham James Ian v Fearless Legends Pte Ltd* [2024] 6 SLR 697 at [14]). The difficulty is that the restaurants are in Australia: an order against them must be served out of Singapore with the Court's approval (O 8 r 1(1) applies to "other court document[s]"), and a Singapore order may be hard to enforce there, so P may in practice need the restaurants' cooperation or the assistance of the Australian courts. The restaurants are majority-owned by D2, D1's sole director, which supports an argument that D1 has practical control of the footage and should produce it under r 3.

**Preservation.** Surveillance footage is often overwritten on a cycle. P should ask the Court to order the detention, custody or preservation of property which "may give rise to issues in an action" (O 13 r 2(1)), and write at once to D1 and the restaurants demanding that the footage be preserved.

**Prospects.** Good against D1 if P identifies the footage precisely, since its materiality to the cheating allegation is obvious; uncertain against the Australian restaurants, for the enforcement reasons above.

Therefore P should request the footage from D1 under O 11 r 3 (in the SAPT or with prior approval), seek an order or cooperation for production from the Featured Restaurants under O 11 r 11 or r 4, and immediately seek preservation of the footage under O 13 r 2.

### 0.IX.7 — Continuing duty to produce; non-compliance

**PYP 2022 facts.** See Q2022-1 under 0.IV.2 and Q2022-2 under 0.VIII.1 (C v D; D pleads illegal moneylending and duress). Both stages of production are complete: initial production under O 11 r 2 following an order at a case conference, and production of requested documents under O 11 r 3. C now tells you he has discovered communications between his son and D that support D's case on duress (the "Adverse Documents"). C asks if there is any way to withhold them. You tell C your initial view is that he need not disclose them because the discovery stage has been completed.

**Q2022-4** · `0.IX.7` `0.IX.2` · PYP 2022 Q4 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-50` `drp-w-0-46`

Was your response to C correct? Explain by referring to the relevant provisions of the Rules of Court 2021.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether C must disclose documents adverse to his case that he found after production was completed. The initial response was wrong: C remains under a continuing duty to produce them within 14 days after they came into his possession or control (O 11 r 6 ROC).

O 11 r 2(1)(b) required C, under the initial production order, to exchange a list and copies of "all known adverse documents", which include documents he "ought reasonably to know are adverse" to his case (O 11 r 2(2)). O 11 r 6 provides that "where the Court makes an order under Rule 2, 3 or 4, the party required to produce the documents remains under a duty to produce any of those documents within 14 days after that document comes into the party's possession or control at any time in the course of the proceedings". The duty does not end when the production stage ends.

The communications between C's son and D support D's duress defence, so they are known adverse documents within the r 2 order. Two points need checking: whether they are in C's possession or control (if C's son holds them and C can obtain them, they likely are within his control; if only C's son has them and C cannot obtain them, D may need a non-party order against the son under O 11 r 11); and whether any of them is privileged (O 11 r 5(3)), which seems unlikely for communications between his son and D. Confidentiality is no ground to withhold (O 11 r 9). If C withholds them, the Court may, under O 11 r 7, "(a) order that the action be dismissed …; (b) draw an adverse inference …; (c) punish that party for contempt of court …; or (d) order that that party may not rely on any document within the scope of the order". Apart from the sanctions, a later discovery that C hid documents would gravely damage his credibility at trial, and his solicitors cannot be party to concealing them.

Therefore the response was not correct: C must produce the Adverse Documents within 14 days after they came into his possession or control under O 11 r 6, and there is no proper way to withhold them.

### 0.X.2 — Expert evidence

**PYP 2022 facts.** See Q2022-1 under 0.IV.2 (C v D, Settlement Agreement for US$5.5 million; D pleads illegal moneylending and duress). Two weeks before the first day of trial, D's solicitors write: "our client will be calling Mr John Tan, a leading cryptocurrency analyst … as an expert witness at the upcoming trial. As our client has an absolute right to call any witness he needs to mount his defence at the trial, we trust your client will have no objection to this." This is the first you have heard of an expert; D's solicitors never raised it at any case conference. You consider that the state of the cryptocurrency market when the Settlement Agreement was signed has no legal relevance to the issues.

**Q2022-5** · `0.X.2` `0.VII.2` `0.VI.1` · PYP 2022 Q5 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-52` `drp-w-0-33` `drp-w-0-28`

Advise C whether there are any grounds for objecting to D calling an expert witness. Refer to the relevant provisions of the Rules of Court 2021.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether D may call a cryptocurrency expert at trial without the Court's approval and without having raised it before. C has strong grounds to object: D has no right to call expert evidence at all without the Court's approval (O 12 r 2(1) ROC), the evidence does not go to any issue in the case, and D is out of time.

**No absolute right.** O 12 r 2(1) states that "no expert evidence may be used in Court unless the Court approves". D's letter is therefore wrong in law.

**The test for approval.** The parties must consider whether expert evidence "will contribute materially to the determination of any issue that relates to scientific, technical or other specialised knowledge" and whether that issue can be resolved by an agreed statement of facts or by submissions on agreed materials (O 12 r 2(2)); and "the Court must not approve expert evidence unless it will contribute materially to the determination of any issue in the case and the issue cannot be resolved" in that way (O 12 r 2(3)). The issues are whether the Settlement Agreement is enforceable, whether D signed it under duress, and whether the Investment Agreements were illegal moneylending. The state of the cryptocurrency market when D signed does not bear on whether D owes US$5.5 million under the Settlement Agreement; at most D might say market losses put him under financial pressure, but any such fact can be proved by D himself as a fact witness, without expert opinion. The evidence would not contribute materially to any issue, so approval should be refused. C can also test whether Mr Tan has the specialised knowledge and impartiality required (O 12 r 1, r 2(4)).

**D is out of time.** Parties "are to inform the Court during the case conference if they intend to rely on expert evidence" (O 9 r 21(1)), and expert evidence is a matter for the single application pending trial (O 9 r 9(4)(m)). D never raised it. Any application D now makes for approval is, two weeks before trial, within the period in which "no application may be taken out … except in a special case and with the trial Judge's approval" sought by letter explaining why there is a special case (O 9 r 9(10)–(11)). D has no special case: the expert's existence was known throughout, and admitting him now would ambush C, who has no expert of his own, and likely delay the trial, contrary to the Ideals of expeditious and fair proceedings (O 3 r 1(2)).

**Costs.** If D applies and fails, C should ask for the costs of the application (O 21 r 3(2)); if the trial is adjourned because of D's late move, C should seek the costs thrown away, citing D's conduct (O 21 r 2(2)(f)).

Therefore C should reply that D has no right to call Mr Tan, that expert evidence needs the Court's approval under O 12 r 2, which should be refused because it would not contribute materially to any issue, and that D is in any case barred from applying within 14 days of trial absent a special case and the trial Judge's approval (O 9 r 9(10)).

**PYP 2023 Q3 facts.** See Q2023-3-1 under 0.VIII.3 and Q2023-3-2 under 0.IX.2. Company R's documents include an Addendum amending the Delivery Clause to "Company R shall make delivery of the goods within 14 days of payment by Company P, subject to such goods being in stock", signed by Company P's CEO (Mr P) and R's sole director. Mr P says he has never seen it and his signature is forged. P wants to call a handwriting expert, Professor XY of the Department of Forensic Science, to prove the forgery.

**Q2023-3-3** · `0.X.2` `0.VI.1` `0.VII.1` · PYP 2023 Q3.3 · 5 marks · `drp-w-0-52` `drp-w-0-28` `drp-w-0-32`

Please advise Company P on the relevant procedural requirements for admitting Professor XY as an expert witness in the action.

**Answer.**

The issue is what P must do to adduce Professor XY's handwriting evidence. P needs the Court's approval under O 12 r 2 ROC, which it should seek by telling the Court at the case conference and dealing with it in its SAPT; approval is likely, but P should first try to agree a common expert with R.

**Approval.** "No expert evidence may be used in Court unless the Court approves" (O 12 r 2(1)). The Court "must not approve expert evidence unless it will contribute materially to the determination of any issue in the case and the issue cannot be resolved" by an agreed statement of facts or submissions on agreed materials (O 12 r 2(2)–(3)). Whether Mr P's signature on the Addendum is genuine is a central issue (it decides whether the Delivery Clause was varied), it needs specialised knowledge of handwriting comparison, and it cannot be resolved by agreed facts since R relies on the Addendum. So approval is likely.

**When and how to raise it.** P must inform the Court during the case conference that it intends to rely on expert evidence (O 9 r 21(1)); the Court then considers Order 12 (O 9 r 21(2)). Before the Registrar's case conference at which expert evidence is discussed, the parties fill in the Expert Witness Template in Form B7 (PD 56(8)). Expert evidence is one of the matters for the single application pending trial (O 9 r 9(4)(m)), filed with an affidavit within 21 days after the case conference (O 9 r 9(5)); an application outside the SAPT needs prior approval by letter (O 9 r 9(8)).

**Who and how many.** "As far as possible, parties must agree on one common expert" (O 12 r 3(1)), so P should first propose Professor XY, or another forensic document examiner, to R as a common expert. Except in a special case with approval, a party may not rely on more than one expert per issue (O 12 r 3(2)). If each side calls its own expert, the Court may have them testify as a panel (O 12 r 7). Professor XY must have the requisite specialised knowledge and be impartial, failing which the Court may reject the evidence (O 12 r 2(4); *Sanum Investments Ltd v ST Group Co Ltd* [2020] 3 SLR 225 at [31]–[33]); his overriding duty is to assist the Court, not P (O 12 r 1(2)–(3)). P should also ask R to produce the original Addendum for inspection, which R must arrange within 14 days of a request (O 11 r 12), since a handwriting examiner needs the original.

Therefore P should raise the expert evidence at the case conference, complete Form B7, propose a common expert to R, and seek the Court's approval under O 12 r 2 (in its SAPT), which is likely because the forgery issue needs specialised knowledge.

### 0.X.3 — Order to attend court or produce documents

**PYP 2025 Q3 facts.** See Q2025-3-1 under 0.VIII.4 (Company P v Restaurant M; M says P used "unheard-of bloggers" instead of "A-list influencers"). P hired Mr and Mrs V to make TikTok content after a meeting at which M's director, Mr D, said they "would fit the bill". P thinks Mr D's evidence of that meeting would help at trial, but fears he will not want to testify against M.

**Q2025-3-3** · `0.X.3` `0.X.4` `0.X.1` · PYP 2025 Q3.3 · 3 marks · `drp-w-0-53` `drp-w-0-51` `drp-w-0-54`

Advise Company P on how Mr D's testimony can be procured and presented to the Court at the relevant stage, and the steps P would need to take.

**Answer.**

The issue is how P can compel M's director to give evidence for P. P should request the Registrar to issue an order to attend court in Form 29 under O 15 r 4 ROC, serve it personally on Mr D in Singapore at least 28 days before trial, and ask the Court's permission for him to give his evidence-in-chief orally because he will not sign an AEIC for P.

**The order.** O 15 r 4(1) allows a party to "request the Registrar to issue an order to attend court or an order to produce documents in Form 29 to any witness", stating whether the witness is to give oral evidence or produce documents or both. The order "must be served by the requesting party on the witness by personal service in Singapore at least 28 days before the hearing" (O 15 r 4(3)); it cannot be served abroad. Issuing it is a ministerial act (*The Lao People's Democratic Republic v Sanum Investments Ltd* [2013] 4 SLR 947 at [17]).

**Presenting the evidence.** As a general rule a trial is decided on the witnesses' AEICs (O 15 r 16(1)), but "in a special case, the Court may allow a witness' evidence-in-chief to be given orally instead of by AEIC" (O 15 r 16(2)). A hostile non-party witness who will not cooperate in preparing an AEIC is the paradigm special case, so P should seek that permission when directions for AEICs are given. P may also seek, by the same Form 29 order, production of any notes of the meeting Mr D holds.

**Steps.** (1) Request the Form 29 order, specifying oral evidence (and documents if needed). (2) Serve it personally on Mr D in Singapore at least 28 days before trial. (3) Offer reasonable compensation for his time and expenses, which a complying witness may claim from P (O 15 r 4(9)). (4) Seek permission for oral evidence-in-chief (O 15 r 16(2)). (5) Expect M or Mr D to apply to have the order revoked (O 15 r 4(4)); an order may be set aside if it is irrelevant, oppressive or an abuse (*BVU v BVX* [2019] SGHC 69 at [103]–[107]; *Ong Jane Rebecca v Lim Lie Hoa* [2003] 1 SLR(R) 457), so P's answer is that Mr D's account of the meeting is directly relevant to whether M approved Mr and Mrs V. (6) Mr D, as a non-party witness, must remain outside the courtroom until called (O 15 r 4(13)). If Mr D is abroad at trial time, the order cannot be served out, and P would need his evidence by video link (s 62A Evidence Act) or a letter of request (O 15 r 5).

Therefore P should obtain and personally serve a Form 29 order to attend court on Mr D at least 28 days before trial and seek permission for him to give oral evidence-in-chief.

### 0.XI.2 — Procedure for an injunction; urgent and without-notice applications; full and frank disclosure

**PYP 2025 Q3 facts.** See Q2025-3-1 under 0.VIII.4 (Company P v Restaurant M for SGD 350,000). P has commenced action in the General Division. While proceedings are under way, P receives intelligence suggesting that M has been transferring large sums of money out of its bank accounts, and fears M is moving assets to evade a potential judgment.

**Q2025-3-4** · `0.XI.2` `0.XI.4` `0.XI.3` `0.VII.2` · PYP 2025 Q3.4 · 8 marks · `drp-w-0-57` `drp-w-0-59` `drp-w-0-58` `drp-w-0-33`

Advise Company P on what application it may consider taking out to protect its interests; whether the Court's permission is required to file it; and the procedural steps involved.

**Answer.**

The issue is how P can stop M from moving its assets out of reach before judgment. P should apply urgently, by summons without notice, for a Mareva (freezing) injunction over M's assets in Singapore, in Form 24, with an ancillary order for disclosure of M's assets; no prior permission is needed.

**The application.** The Court may grant an injunction by interlocutory order "in all cases in which it appears to the court to be just or convenient" (s 4(10) Civil Law Act 1909), and the General Division has power to provide for "the preservation of assets for the satisfaction of any judgment which has been or may be made" (SCJA s 18(2), First Schedule para 5(c)). A Mareva injunction requires a good arguable case on the merits and a real risk that the defendant will dissipate its assets to frustrate enforcement of an anticipated judgment (*Farooq Ahmad Mann v Xia Zheng* [2024] SGHC 182 at [21]–[23], applying *Bouvier, Yves Charles Edgar v Accent Delight International Ltd* [2015] 5 SLR 558 at [36]–[37]); the balance of convenience is not part of the test (*Farooq* at [40]–[42]). P has a good arguable case: the fee was agreed, the campaign ran and M paid the first instalment without complaint. The risk of dissipation must be shown by solid evidence: P must put the "intelligence" into admissible form (who saw what transfers, when, and how much), and link it to the dispute, for example that the transfers began after P's invoice and M's refusal to pay. A mere fear will not do.

**Permission.** None is needed: an application for an injunction is the first item on the O 9 r 9(7) list ("(a) an injunction or a search order"), so P need not wait for the SAPT or seek approval.

**Procedural steps.**
(1) File a summons without notice in the existing action, supported by an affidavit "stating the urgency and explaining why the defendant should not be informed about the application, and the merits" (O 13 r 1(3)), using the prescribed Form 24 for a local injunction prohibiting disposal of assets in Singapore (O 13 r 1(6); PD 72(2)).
(2) The affidavit must use the PD 73(1) headings: reasons for proceeding without notice (risk of dissipation), urgency and the triggering event, the factual basis, the factual basis for M's reasonable defences, any jurisdiction issues, and an undertaking to pay for losses caused by the order with evidence of the assets available to meet it (PD 73(1)(a)–(g)); skeletal arguments must be filed (PD 73(2)).
(3) Full and frank disclosure: P must disclose all material facts it knows or reasonably ought to know, "including any matter that may affect the merits of its case adversely" (O 13 r 1(5)), and draw the Court's attention to them (*The "Vasiliy Golovnin"* [2008] 4 SLR(R) 994 at [83]–[110]); here, M's complaint that P used unknown bloggers rather than A-list influencers. Non-disclosure risks discharge of the injunction with costs.
(4) Notice: even without notice, P must give M at least 2 hours' notice of the hearing unless notice would or might defeat the purpose, and must explain in the affidavit why (PD 71(2)–(3)); here notice may prompt further transfers, so P should explain why it gives none.
(5) The application is heard by a Judge if contested (PD 72(1)); P should undertake not to inform any third party of the proceedings until after the return date (PD 72(3)), and give the usual undertaking as to damages, which the Court may require to be fortified.
(6) Ask for an ancillary order requiring M to disclose its assets on affidavit, so the injunction can be policed (*Sea Trucks Offshore Ltd v Roomans* [2019] 3 SLR 836 at [44]–[55]).
(7) After the order is made, serve it on M; M may apply to discharge it at the inter partes hearing.

Therefore P should apply immediately, without prior permission, by summons without notice for a Form 24 Mareva injunction over M's Singapore assets up to SGD 350,000 plus interest and costs, with an asset disclosure order, supported by a PD 73 affidavit giving solid evidence of dissipation and full and frank disclosure, and an undertaking as to damages.

### 0.XI.4 — Mareva injunction: procedure, ancillary disclosure, principles of grant

**PYP 2021 facts.** See Q2021-1 under 0.II.5 and Q2021-5b under 0.IX.3. Lists of documents were exchanged on 1 December 2021; D1's quarterly financial statements show its financial position steadily deteriorating since early 2020. P fears that, if the case drags on, D1 will not be able to satisfy a judgment. D1's assets are predominantly abroad.

**Q2021-5a** · `0.XI.4` `0.XI.2` `0.XIV.2` · PYP 2021 Q5 (first query) · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-59` `drp-w-0-57` `drp-w-0-56` `drp-w-0-72`

P asks whether it is possible to prevent D1 from dealing with its assets, which are predominantly abroad, pending the conclusion of the action, so that they are preserved. Advise P on any application(s), the procedures, the prospects of success, and costs.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether P can freeze D1's assets, mostly outside Singapore, pending trial. The only route is a worldwide Mareva injunction in Form 25; the procedure is open to P, but on present evidence it is unlikely to be granted, because D1's declining finances show a risk of insolvency, not a real risk of dissipation.

**Power and procedure.** The Court may grant an injunction by interlocutory order where it is just or convenient (s 4(10) Civil Law Act 1909; SCJA s 18(2), First Schedule para 5(c)). A worldwide injunction "must be in Form 25" (O 13 r 1(7)). P applies by summons without notice in the action, supported by an affidavit stating the urgency, why D1 should not be told, and the merits (O 13 r 1(3)), under the PD 73(1) headings, with skeletal arguments; she must make full and frank disclosure of all material facts, including adverse ones such as D1's cheating allegation (O 13 r 1(5)); she must give at least 2 hours' notice unless that would defeat the purpose (PD 71); a contested application is heard by a Judge (PD 72(1)); and she must give an undertaking as to damages supported by evidence of her means (PD 73(1)(f)). No prior approval is needed (O 9 r 9(7)(a)).

**The test.** P must show a good arguable case on the merits and a real risk that D1 will dissipate its assets to frustrate enforcement of a judgment; for a worldwide order the threshold of necessity is more exacting (*Farooq Ahmad Mann v Xia Zheng* [2024] SGHC 182 at [21]–[23], applying *Bouvier, Yves Charles Edgar v Accent Delight International Ltd* [2015] 5 SLR 558 at [36]–[37]). The risk of dissipation must be shown by solid evidence, and dishonesty matters only where it bears on that risk.

**Application.** Good arguable case: P won the show and was not paid; that D1 defeated her summary judgment application does not stop her case being well above the "good arguable" threshold. Assets: D1's assets are mostly abroad, so only a worldwide order would bite. Real risk of dissipation: this is P's weakness. The financial statements show D1's position deteriorating since early 2020, before the dispute, which suggests trading difficulty, not that D1 is moving assets to defeat a judgment. Being a shell company with few assets is not itself dissipation. Without evidence of transfers out of the ordinary course, asset-stripping, or conduct by D1 or D2 suggesting they would put assets beyond reach, the Court is unlikely to grant a worldwide order. P should gather such evidence (for example from the financial statements: unexplained payments to D2 or related companies) before applying. If granted, P should seek an ancillary order that D1 disclose its worldwide assets (*Sea Trucks Offshore Ltd v Roomans* [2019] 3 SLR 836 at [44]–[55]).

**Costs.** P bears the risk of her undertaking as to damages if the order is later discharged, and if the application fails she will usually be ordered to pay D1's costs (O 21 r 3(2)). If the order is granted, its costs may be made costs in the cause, since it holds the ring until trial; the Court accepted this as sensible, though not an immutable rule, in *Group Lease Holdings Pte Ltd v Group Lease Public Co Ltd* [2024] SGHC 302 at [194].

Therefore P may apply without notice for a worldwide Mareva injunction in Form 25, but on the evidence of deteriorating finances alone it is unlikely to succeed; she should look for solid evidence of dissipation first, and meanwhile press on to trial.

### 0.XII.2 — Which appeal track (O 18 or O 19); when time runs; no automatic stay

**PYP 2022 Q6 facts.** See Q2022-1 under 0.IV.2 (C v D). The Originating Claim went to trial in the General Division. After trial and closing submissions, the Judge issued a written judgment on 18 October 2023 in C's favour, ending: "C is entitled to judgement for [US$5.5 million] plus post-judgement interest. D is to pay C costs. The amount of costs payable by D to C is to be assessed." C is concerned that D may appeal.

**Q2022-6b** · `0.XII.2` · PYP 2022 Q6(b) · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-63`

Will any appeal from the Judge's decision be governed by Order 18 or Order 19 of the Rules of Court 2021?

**Answer.**

The issue is which Order governs an appeal against the Judge's decision. Order 19 governs it, because the decision is a judgment given in a trial.

Order 18 applies to appeals from decisions on applications in an action (O 18 r 1(1)), and an "application in an action" excludes matters within Order 19 (O 18 r 1(2)). Order 19 applies to "an appeal against any judgment of the General Division (except a judgment of the Registrar)" (O 19 r 1(b)), and "judgment" means a judgment given by the lower Court "(a) in a trial" (O 19 r 3), "trial" meaning the hearing on the merits of an originating claim. Appeals from the General Division to the Appellate Division or Court of Appeal against such judgments are governed by Order 19 Division 3 (O 19 r 24).

The Judge's decision of 18 October 2023 was given after the trial of C's originating claim, so it is a "judgment" within O 19 r 3(a).

Therefore any appeal is governed by Order 19 (Division 3), with a notice of appeal in Form 35 due within 28 days (O 19 r 25(1)).

**Q2022-6c** · `0.XII.2` · PYP 2022 Q6(c) · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-63`

Has the time for appealing started to run following the issuance of the written judgment, or must D first wait until the amount of costs payable has been assessed?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether time to appeal runs while costs are still to be assessed. Time has already started to run from 18 October 2023; D need not wait for the assessment.

O 19 r 4(1) provides that time for an appeal "does not start to run until after the lower Court has heard and determined all matters in the trial, including costs". But under O 19 r 4(2)(a) the lower Court "is deemed to have determined costs when it has (i) decided the parties' entitlement to costs, even if the amount has not been determined; (ii) ordered that costs be assessed; (iii) ordered that costs be reserved; or (iv) decided that there is no order as to costs". (These deeming words were substituted by S 37/2024 from 1 February 2024; when the judgment was given, the Rule already deemed a direction to assess costs to be a determination, so the answer is the same.)

The Judge decided that D is to pay C's costs (entitlement) and ordered that the amount be assessed, so costs are deemed determined on 18 October 2023.

Therefore time started to run on 18 October 2023, and D's notice of appeal is due within 28 days, by 15 November 2023 (O 19 r 25(1); O 3 r 3(3)).

**PYP 2025 Q4 facts.** You act for Doctor T. In a judgment issued on 1 November 2025 after a trial, the General Division ordered Doctor T to pay Mr X SGD 500,000 in damages for medical negligence and awarded Mr X costs "to be assessed".

**Q2025-4-1** · `0.XII.2` · PYP 2025 Q4.1 · 2 marks · `drp-w-0-63`

It is now 3 November 2025. Doctor T is considering whether to appeal. Advise Doctor T on when time to appeal started (or will start) to run.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether time to appeal runs while costs are yet to be assessed. Time started to run on 1 November 2025, the date of the judgment.

Time for an appeal under Order 19 does not run until the lower Court has determined all matters, including costs (O 19 r 4(1)), but the Court "is deemed to have determined costs when it has … (ii) ordered that costs be assessed" (O 19 r 4(2)(a)(ii)). The judgment is a judgment after trial, so Order 19 applies, and the notice of appeal must be filed within 28 days after the judgment (O 19 r 25(1)).

The judgment awarded costs to Mr X "to be assessed", so costs are deemed determined on 1 November 2025, and time began then. Twenty-eight days from 1 November 2025 ends on Saturday 29 November 2025; because that is not a court day, the notice of appeal is in time if filed on Monday 1 December 2025 (O 3 r 3(3), (7)). Doctor T should not wait for the assessment, and should file well before the deadline. (If the Judge had instead called for costs submissions and not decided costs within 30 days, time would have started when the 30 days expired: O 19 r 4(1A).)

Therefore time started to run on 1 November 2025, and Doctor T must file his notice of appeal by 29 November 2025 (in time on 1 December 2025).

### 0.XII.3 — Which appellate court: Appellate Division or Court of Appeal

**PYP 2022 Q6 facts.** See Q2022-6b above (C v D; judgment after trial for US$5.5 million in a contract claim, 18 October 2023).

**Q2022-6d** · `0.XII.3` · PYP 2022 Q6(d) · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-64`

Assuming that D has a right to appeal, to which court must D file its appeal?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether the appeal goes to the Appellate Division or the Court of Appeal. It goes to the Appellate Division of the High Court.

Section 29C(1) SCJA provides that an appeal against a decision of the General Division in its original civil jurisdiction "is to be made to the Appellate Division", unless the Sixth Schedule or other written law provides for the Court of Appeal (s 29C(2)). The Sixth Schedule sends to the Court of Appeal appeals arising from cases relating to, among others, constitutional or administrative law, contempt of court, arbitration, corporate insolvency, patents, admiralty or shipping, and SICC decisions. **Q2025-3-2** · `0.XII.4` `0.XII.2` · PYP 2025 Q3.2 · 2 marks · `drp-w-0-65` `drp-w-0-63` `drp-w-0-62`

**Answer.**

The issue is whether P can appeal against a General Division Judge's dismissal of its summary judgment application, and whether it needs permission. The decision is appealable; whether permission is needed is an **open point, with no authority**, so P should apply for permission within 14 days to be safe.

Section 29 SCJA bars appeals only where written law makes a decision final or the case is in the Fourth Schedule; a dismissal of a summary judgment application is neither, so an appeal lies. Permission is required under s 29A(1) where the amount in dispute does not exceed $250,000, or in a Fifth Schedule para 3 case. The amount in dispute here, SGD 350,000, exceeds $250,000. The relevant Fifth Schedule heads are "(a) an order giving unconditional permission to defend" and the residual "(l)" head for orders at the hearing of interlocutory applications, which expressly excludes applications for summary judgment.

**The two views.** On the text, O 9 r 17(7) treats "(a) dismiss the application" and "(b) grant permission to defend … without any conditions" as different orders, and para 3(a) names only the second, so a dismissal is appealable as of right. On the other view, the scheme requires permission for orders that send the case "along the ordinary route to trial", which is the effect of both a dismissal and unconditional permission to defend, whereas an order granting summary judgment is appealable as of right (*Purwadi v MBF Northern Securities Sdn Bhd* [2025] SGHC(A) 25 at [24]–[25], discussing the predecessor provision; *Telecom Credit Inc v Midas United Group Ltd* [2019] 1 SLR 131 at [6], [18]–[19]). No case decides the point.

**Practical course.** The decision was on an application in an action, so Order 18 governs the appeal (O 18 r 1). P should apply to the Appellate Division for permission within 14 days after the decision (O 18 r 29(1)), and in any event file its notice of appeal within 14 days (O 18 r 27(1)), so that it is protected whichever view prevails. Before appealing, P may ask the Judge for further arguments before the order is extracted or by the 15th day after the decision (s 29B SCJA), which postpones the time to appeal.

Therefore the decision is appealable, but because it is unsettled whether a dismissal over $250,000 needs permission, P should seek permission within 14 days (O 18 r 29(1)).

### 0.XIII.1 — The enforcement order: modes, procedure, validity, Sheriff, objections

**PYP 2022 facts.** See Q2022-1 under 0.IV.2 and Q2022-6b under 0.XII.2 (C's judgment against D for US$5.5 million plus interest and costs). D does not appeal. C wants full payment and tells you: (a) D likely has bank accounts with DBS Bank and OCBC Bank in Singapore; the first tranche of the investment was paid into D's DBS account and the second into his OCBC account; (b) D owns a condominium unit in Singapore; (c) C has no other information on D's assets.

**Q2022-7** · `0.XIII.1` `0.XIII.2` · PYP 2022 Q7 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-67` `drp-w-0-68`

Advise C on the steps that may be taken to enforce the judgment debt.

**Answer.**

The issue is how C can recover the judgment debt from D's bank accounts and condominium, and find D's other assets. C should apply for an enforcement order under O 22 r 2 ROC for attachment of D's bank deposits and seizure and sale of the condominium, and apply under O 22 r 11 to examine D on his assets.

**1. The enforcement order.** An enforcement order may authorise the Sheriff to "(a) … seize and sell all property belonging to the enforcement respondent" and "(c) … attach a debt due to the enforcement respondent from any non-party … including a deposit with a financial institution" (O 22 r 2(2)); one order may cover several modes (O 22 r 2(1)). C must first serve the judgment on D, then apply by summons without notice in Form 38 "not earlier than 3 days after the Court order has been served" (O 22 r 2(3)), with an affidavit stating the matters in O 22 r 2(4), including the terms of the judgment and date of service, the amount due and daily interest, a description of the deposits to be attached and the property to be seized with evidence that they belong to D (r 2(4)(i), (k)): here the bank records of C's transfers of the two tranches into D's DBS and OCBC accounts, and an SLA title search showing D owns the condominium; the sequence in which the modes are to be carried out (r 2(4)(l)), sensibly the deposits first; and C's solicitors' undertaking to indemnify the Sheriff and pay its charges and fees (r 2(4)(m)).

**2. How the Sheriff carries it out.** The Sheriff attaches each deposit by serving a notice of attachment (Form 40) on the bank, and a copy goes to D within 7 days; each bank must within 14 days tell the Sheriff the amount available, must not deal with it for 21 days (or until any objection is decided), and must then pay it over within 7 days (O 22 r 6). The condominium is seized by notice to the SLA and registration within 14 days (O 22 r 6). The order is valid for 12 months and may be extended for 12 months at a time if C applies before it expires (O 22 r 2(5)–(8)).

**3. Objections.** D, or anyone else claiming the property (for example a co-owner of the condominium, or a joint account holder), may file a notice of objection with the Sheriff within 14 days after service of the notice of seizure or attachment, stating the grounds and evidence (O 22 r 10). If an account is joint, C must show strong prima facie evidence that all the money in it is D's (*Timing Ltd v Tay Toh Hin* [2020] 5 SLR 974 at [16]–[33]).

**4. Finding other assets.** Since C knows of nothing else, C should also apply for D to be examined orally in court, or to make an affidavit, or both, on the properties he owns beneficially in whole or in part or will be entitled to, and to produce documents (O 22 r 11(1)–(2)). The order must be in Form 41 and served personally on D (O 22 r 11(5)), with the PD 145 questionnaire annexed (PD 145(1)). If the examination reveals further assets, C can add them to the enforcement order. If D starts moving assets, C can seek a Mareva injunction in aid of execution (*JTrust Asia Pte Ltd v Group Lease Holdings Pte Ltd* [2021] SGCA 26 at [21]–[24]).

**Costs.** C's solicitors must undertake to pay the Sheriff's charges and fees and any deposits requested (O 22 r 2(4)(m)), so C funds these up front; C should ask that the costs of the enforcement and examination applications be paid by D (O 21 r 3(2)).

Therefore C should serve the judgment, then apply (at least 3 days later) for a Form 38 enforcement order attaching D's DBS and OCBC deposits and seizing and selling the condominium, and apply under O 22 r 11 for D to be examined on his other assets; D has 14 days after the notices to object.

### 0.XIII.3 — Mareva injunction after judgment, in aid of execution

**Q2021-9** · `0.XIII.3` `0.XIII.1` `0.XIII.2` `0.XIV.2` · PYP 2021 Q9 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-69` `drp-w-0-67` `drp-w-0-68` `drp-w-0-72`

The issue is how P can turn her judgment into money when D1 is uncooperative, its director is abroad, and P knows of only a possibly dormant bank account and a warehouse. P has four avenues: an enforcement order against the DBS account and the Kallang warehouse and equipment; an examination of D1, through D2, on its assets; a post-judgment Mareva injunction if D1 starts moving assets; and, as the appeal has been dismissed, no stay stands in her way.

**1. Enforcement order (O 22 r 2).** One enforcement order may authorise the Sheriff to "seize and sell all property belonging to the enforcement respondent" and to "attach a debt due to the enforcement respondent … including a deposit with a financial institution" (O 22 r 2(1)–(2)). P serves the judgment on D1 and, not earlier than 3 days later, applies by summons without notice in Form 38 (O 22 r 2(3)), with an affidavit under O 22 r 2(4) describing the property and the deposit and giving evidence that they belong to D1: her own transfer record for the DBS account; an SLA title search for the warehouse (if D1 owns the land, it is seized by notice to the SLA and registration within 14 days: O 22 r 6); and evidence that the cameras and lighting equipment stored there are D1's. **2. She would use Form 24 (Singapore assets) and add an asset disclosure order; the equipment, used for regional projects, is mobile, which supports urgency.

Therefore P should apply for an enforcement order in Form 38 against the DBS deposit and the Kallang warehouse and equipment, apply under O 22 r 11 to examine D1 through D2 (with permission to serve D2 in Australia), and seek a post-judgment Mareva injunction if D1 shows signs of moving its assets.

### 0.XIII.4 — Stay of enforcement, including pending appeal

**PYP 2025 Q4 facts.** See Q2025-4-1 under 0.XII.2 (Mr X's SGD 500,000 judgment against Doctor T, 1 November 2025). It is now 15 November 2025. You have advised Doctor T that any appeal is more likely than not to be dismissed. He nonetheless wants to appeal "as his assets – and any information [he] may be compelled to disclose concerning assets – can then be kept safe from enforcement in the meantime".

**Q2025-4-3** · `0.XIII.4` `0.XII.2` `0.XIII.2` · PYP 2025 Q4.3 · 7 marks · `drp-w-0-70` `drp-w-0-63` `drp-w-0-68`

Is Doctor T's understanding correct? Please explain why and/or why not.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether filing an appeal protects Doctor T's assets, and information about them, from enforcement. It does not: an appeal does not stay enforcement or an examination of his assets; he would have to apply for a stay and show special circumstances, which on these facts he is unlikely to do.

**No automatic stay.** "Except so far as the lower Court or the appellate Court directs, an appeal does not operate as a stay of enforcement or of proceedings" (O 19 r 6(1) ROC), and s 45(1) SCJA provides that an appeal to the Appellate Division does not operate as a stay unless the General Division or Appellate Division so orders. So, despite an appeal, Mr X may serve the judgment and, 3 days later, apply for an enforcement order to seize and sell Doctor T's property or attach his bank deposits (O 22 r 2(2)–(3)).

**Asset information.** Mr X may also apply for Doctor T to be examined orally or by affidavit on the properties he owns and to produce documents (O 22 r 11(1)–(2)). An examination "is deemed to be enforcement of a Court order and is within the terms of any written law or any order staying enforcement" (O 22 r 11(4)), so it is held back only if a stay of enforcement is actually ordered; the appeal alone does not do so.

**A stay must be applied for.** Doctor T may apply to stay enforcement pending appeal (s 45(1) SCJA), or where there is "a special case making it inappropriate to enforce the Court order immediately" (O 22 r 13(1)); the application needs no prior approval (O 9 r 9(7)(j)). A successful party should not be deprived of the fruits of its judgment; the applicant must show special circumstances, usually a real risk that a successful appeal would be rendered nugatory, for example because the judgment sum could not be recovered from the judgment creditor; a mere offer to pay the sum into court is not enough, and the merits of the appeal are generally irrelevant (*Axis Megalink Sdn Bhd v Far East Mining Pte Ltd* [2024] SGHC 47 at [7]–[11]; *Lee Kuan Yew v Jeyaretnam Joshua Benjamin* [1990] 1 SLR(R) 772 at [6]). Nothing suggests Mr X could not repay SGD 500,000 if Doctor T won. Doctor T's wish to keep his assets out of reach is not a special circumstance; it is the reverse. If a stay were granted, it would probably be on conditions, such as payment of the judgment sum into court or to Mr X's solicitors as stakeholders (*Axis Megalink* at [12]–[13]), which would not keep his assets "safe" from Mr X in any event.

**Further risks.** If Doctor T moves assets to avoid the judgment, Mr X may obtain a Mareva injunction in aid of execution (*JTrust Asia Pte Ltd v Group Lease Holdings Pte Ltd* [2021] SGCA 26 at [21]–[24]). Interest continues to run for any delay caused by the appeal (O 19 r 6(3)). And an appeal Doctor T has been told will probably fail, pursued only to delay enforcement, exposes him to further costs, including $20,000 security for Mr X's costs of the appeal (O 19 r 27(5)).

Therefore Doctor T's understanding is incorrect: an appeal does not stay enforcement or an examination of his assets (O 19 r 6(1); s 45(1) SCJA; O 22 r 11(4)); he would need to apply for a stay and show special circumstances, which he cannot on these facts.

### 0.XIV.6 — Offers to settle and their costs consequences

**PYP 2021 facts.** See Q2021-1 under 0.II.5. After trial, the Judge found D1 in breach of contract but awarded P less than she claimed: judgment for US$350,000. Three months before trial, D1 had served on P an offer to settle in Form 33 [ROC 2014] offering to pay US$400,000; the offer had not expired when judgment was entered.

**Q2021-7** · `0.XIV.6` `0.XIV.2` `0.XIV.3` · PYP 2021 Q7 · not mark-weighted · `drp-w-0-75` `drp-w-0-72` `drp-w-0-73`

Advise P on the costs implications of the offer to settle.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether P, who won but recovered less than D1's unaccepted offer, will be penalised in costs. Under ROC 2021 there is no fixed offer-to-settle costs regime; D1's offer is an offer of amicable resolution that the Court weighs when deciding costs, and because P did worse at trial than the offer, the Court is likely to reduce her costs, or order her to pay D1's costs, for the period after the offer unless she had reasonable grounds to reject it.

**Rule.** An offer of amicable resolution includes an offer to settle the action (O 5 r 1(3)); it must be in writing and open for at least 14 days, and its terms must not be revealed to the Court "until after the Court has determined the merits … and is dealing with costs" (O 5 r 2(1)–(3)). A party "must not reject an offer of amicable resolution unless the party has reasonable grounds to do so" (O 5 r 1(4)). In fixing costs, the Court must have regard to "(a) efforts made by the parties at amicable resolution" (O 21 r 2(2)), and it may disallow or reduce a successful party's costs, or order it to pay costs, if it "(b) has done or omitted to do anything unreasonably" or "(c) has not discharged [its] duty to consider amicable resolution" (O 21 r 4). The ROC 2021 contain no rule fixing costs consequences for failing to beat an offer; the old Form 33 regime in ROC 2014 O 22A, under which this paper was set, has no counterpart.

**Application.** P is the successful party, so the starting point is that she gets her costs (O 21 r 3(2)). But D1 offered US$400,000, US$50,000 more than the judgment, three months before trial. At the costs stage D1 will disclose the offer and argue that P rejected it without reasonable grounds (O 5 r 1(4)), so that her costs after the offer should be disallowed or she should pay D1's post-offer costs (O 21 r 4(b)–(c)). P's answers depend on the facts: whether the offer covered interest and costs (if it excluded costs already incurred, its true value may have been lower than the judgment plus costs); whether, when it was made, she reasonably expected to recover her US$22,000 expenses claim and the full prize; and whether D1's cheating allegation, still live then, made the offer look like a tactical discount. Departures from the usual order are exceptional (*Tullio Planeta v Maoro Andrew G* [1994] 2 SLR(R) 501 at [21]–[24]), but rejecting an offer better than the eventual result is the kind of conduct O 21 r 4 targets. The Court has a discretion, not a formula.

Therefore P will probably receive her costs up to the date of the offer, but the Court is likely to reduce or disallow her costs after it, or order her to pay some of D1's costs from then, unless she shows reasonable grounds for rejecting it; she should gather the facts that made the rejection reasonable before the costs hearing.

### 0.I.2 — Which court to sue in: State Courts limits and Small Claims Tribunals

**Qdrill-1** · `0.I.2` `0.I.1` · drill-1 · `drp-w-0-1`

Kopi Pte Ltd has three unpaid customers: (a) Alpha Pte Ltd owes $180,000 for coffee machines supplied last year; (b) Beta Pte Ltd owes $262,000 under a distribution agreement; (c) Gamma, a café owner, owes $14,000 for beans supplied 18 months ago. Kopi asks in which court or tribunal it should sue each, and whether it can bring the Beta claim in the District Court.

**Answer.**

The issue is which forum has jurisdiction over each claim. Kopi should sue Alpha in the District Court, Beta in the General Division (or in the District Court only by abandoning the excess or by agreement), and Gamma in the Small Claims Tribunals if the claim is of a kind they hear, or otherwise in the Magistrate's Court.

A District Court has no jurisdiction where "(a) the amount claimed exceeds the District Court limit" of $250,000 (ss 2, 19(4) State Courts Act 1970), and a Magistrate's Court none where the amount claimed exceeds the Magistrate's Court limit of $60,000 (s 52(1A)). The test is whether the claim "exceeds" the limit, so a claim of exactly $250,000 stays in the District Court. A claimant may abandon the excess, in which case it cannot recover more than the limit and the judgment fully discharges the cause of action (s 22), or the parties may agree by a signed memorandum that the District Court has jurisdiction despite the excess (s 23). The Small Claims Tribunals hear specified claims not exceeding $20,000, or up to $30,000 if the parties agree by signed memorandum, brought within 2 years of accrual (Small Claims Tribunals Act 1984 ss 2, 5). Above these limits, the General Division has jurisdiction in personam once the defendant is served or submits (s 16(1) SCJA).

(a) $180,000 is within the District Court limit and above the Magistrate's Court limit, so Kopi sues Alpha in the District Court. Suing in the General Division is possible but disproportionate, contrary to the Ideal of cost-effective work proportionate to the value of the claim (O 3 r 1(2)(c)), and risks costs consequences. (b) $262,000 exceeds the District Court limit. Kopi may sue in the General Division; or sue in the District Court by abandoning the $12,000 excess (s 22), which forfeits that sum for good; or ask Beta to sign a memorandum agreeing to the District Court's jurisdiction (s 23). (c) $14,000 is within the Tribunals' $20,000 limit and the claim accrued 18 months ago, within 2 years; a claim for the price of goods supplied is the kind of contract claim the Tribunals hear, so Kopi may file there (confirm against the prescribed claims under s 5); otherwise the Magistrate's Court.

Therefore Kopi should sue Alpha in the District Court, Beta in the General Division unless it chooses to abandon $12,000 or Beta agrees to the District Court, and Gamma in the Small Claims Tribunals (or the Magistrate's Court).

### 0.I.3 — Powers of the General Division (SCJA s 18, First Schedule) and O 3 r 2

**Qdrill-2** · `0.I.3` · drill-2 · `drp-w-0-2`

In Kopi's General Division action against Beta, Kopi's solicitors obtained an order without notice extending the time for Kopi's reply affidavit in an application. Beta's solicitors learned of the order 10 days later. Separately, Beta has missed the Court's deadline to file a list of documents by 5 days, and Kopi asks what the Court can do about that. Advise both sides.

**Answer.**

The issues are whether Beta can undo the order made without notice, and what the Court may do about Beta's late list. Beta may apply to set aside the order under O 3 r 2(8)(a) ROC, and must do so within 14 days after it learned of it; the Court may deal with Beta's default under O 3 r 2(4), most likely by waiving it on terms as to costs.

**Setting aside.** O 3 r 2(8) provides that the Court may, on its own accord or on application, if in the interests of justice, revoke any judgment or order obtained "(a) without notice to, or in the absence of, the party affected". The application must be taken out within 14 days after the applicant knows or should have known of the ground (O 3 r 2(9)–(10)). Beta learned of the order 10 days ago, so it has 4 days left. It should explain why the extension was unjustified and why it should have been heard.

**Non-compliance.** Where there is non-compliance with the Rules or the Court's orders, the Court may "(a) … waive the non-compliance; (b) disallow or reject the filing or use of any document; (c) refuse to hear any matter or dismiss it without a hearing; (d) dismiss, stay or set aside any proceedings … if the non-compliance is inconsistent with any of the Ideals in a material way; (e) impose a late filing fee of $50 for each day a document remains unfiled after the deadline, excluding non-court days; (f) make costs orders" (O 3 r 2(4)). A 5-day delay in a list of documents is not inconsistent with the Ideals in a material way, so dismissal under (d) is unlikely; the realistic outcomes are waiver with a late filing fee and costs. Where no rule covers a step, the Court may do whatever is necessary to ensure justice or prevent abuse, consistent with the Ideals (O 3 r 2(2)); and the General Division's powers also include the First Schedule powers, such as preserving property, evidence and assets (s 18 SCJA, First Schedule para 5).

Therefore Beta should apply within the next 4 days to set aside the without-notice order under O 3 r 2(8)(a), and Kopi can expect the Court to waive Beta's late list on terms (a late filing fee and costs) rather than strike out its defence.

### 0.I.4 — Sources of civil procedure; when ROC 2021 applies; the Ideals

**Qdrill-3** · `0.I.4` · drill-3 · `drp-w-0-3`

Delta commenced an action by writ on 15 March 2022; Echo commenced an action by originating claim on 5 April 2022. Judgment in Delta's action was given in 2025, and the defendant filed its notice of appeal in 2025. A trainee asks which Rules govern each case, and why counsel keep citing "the Ideals" in argument.

**Answer.**

The issue is which procedural rules apply. Echo's action is governed by the Rules of Court 2021; Delta's action, begun before 1 April 2022, is a legacy case under the ROC 2014, but the ROC 2021 apply with necessary modifications to its appeal filed after that date.

O 1 r 2(3) states that the ROC 2021 "(a) apply to and in relation to all civil proceedings in the Supreme Court or the State Courts which are commenced on or after 1 April 2022, including appeals arising from those proceedings; and (b) apply with the necessary modifications to every appeal to the Court of Appeal or the Appellate Division … filed on or after 1 April 2022, relating to a decision of a lower court made in proceedings to which the revoked Rules of Court apply". The saving and transitional provisions in the First Schedule keep the revoked ROC 2014 for legacy cases (O 1 r 2(2)). The Rules "are to be given a purposive interpretation" (O 3 r 1(1)) and seek to achieve the Ideals: fair access to justice, expeditious proceedings, cost-effective work proportionate to the nature and importance of the action, its complexity and its value, efficient use of court resources, and fair and practical results suited to the parties' needs (O 3 r 1(2)); the Court must seek to achieve them in all its orders, and the parties must assist it (O 3 r 1(3)–(4)). Cases decided under the ROC 2014 remain relevant where the principle has not changed (*Iskandar bin Rahmat v Attorney-General* [2022] 2 SLR 1018 at [17]–[19]).

Echo's action began on 5 April 2022, so the ROC 2021 apply under r 2(3)(a). Delta's began on 15 March 2022, so the ROC 2014 govern the proceedings in the lower court; its 2025 appeal is governed by the ROC 2021 with necessary modifications under r 2(3)(b). Counsel cite the Ideals because every discretionary power is exercised in their light: for example, a late application runs against expeditious proceedings, and a disproportionate production request against cost-effective work.

Therefore Echo's action is under the ROC 2021, Delta's action under the ROC 2014 with its appeal under the ROC 2021 as modified, and the Ideals (O 3 r 1) are the touchstone for every discretionary argument.

### 0.I.5 — Calculating, extending and shortening time

**Qdrill-4** · `0.I.5` `0.V.2` `0.V.4` · drill-4 · `drp-w-0-4` `drp-w-0-21` `drp-w-0-22`

Foxtrot Pte Ltd's process server personally served an originating claim and statement of claim on Golf in Singapore at 6.15pm on Monday 5 October 2026. Golf's solicitors ask for the deadlines for Golf's notice of intention to contest and defence, and whether they can get more time to file the defence.

**Answer.**

The issue is when Golf's deadlines fall and how they can be extended. Service is deemed effected on Tuesday 6 October 2026; the notice of intention to contest is due by Tuesday 20 October and the defence by Tuesday 27 October 2026; the defence deadline may be extended once by up to 14 days with Foxtrot's written consent, or by the Court.

"Service effected after 5pm on a day is deemed effected on the following day" (O 7 r 8(2)). Time is calculated under O 3 r 3, not the Interpretation Act (O 3 r 3(1)): "where an act must be done within a specified period after or from a specified date, the period begins immediately after that date" (O 3 r 3(3)); periods of 6 days or less exclude non-court days (O 3 r 3(6)); and if time expires on a non-court day, the act is in time on the next court day (O 3 r 3(7)). A defendant served in Singapore must file and serve a notice of intention to contest or not contest within 14 days after service of the statement of claim (O 6 r 6(1)), and a defence within 21 days (O 6 r 7(1)). Service at 6.15pm on 5 October is deemed made on 6 October. The 14 days begin on 7 October and end on Tuesday 20 October 2026; the 21 days end on Tuesday 27 October 2026, both court days. ### 0.III.1 — Choosing the mode: originating claim or originating application

**Qdrill-6** · `0.III.1` · drill-6 · `drp-w-0-10`

### 0.III.4 — Originating application: form and supporting affidavit

**Qdrill-8** · `0.III.4` `0.V.8` · drill-8 · `drp-w-0-13` `drp-w-0-27`

### 0.V.7 — Further pleadings

**Qdrill-9** · `0.V.7` `0.II.2` · drill-9 · `drp-w-0-26` `drp-w-0-6`

Mike sues November Pte Ltd for losses from a 2017 investment scheme. November's defence pleads that the claim is time-barred and denies all allegations. Mike says he discovered the fraud only in 2024 because November concealed it. His junior wants to file a reply "to deny everything in the defence and plead the concealment". Advise.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether Mike may file a reply. Mike needs no reply merely to deny the defence, but he should seek the Court's approval at the case conference to file a reply pleading concealment, because that is a material matter in answer to the limitation defence.

"Parties must seek the approval of the Court at the case conference to file any further pleadings beyond the defence or defence to counterclaim, in cases where it is necessary for certain matters to be pleaded" (O 6 r 10(1)), and "no further pleadings may be filed unless the Court otherwise orders" (O 6 r 10(2)). The Court "must not order further pleadings to be filed if they merely deny or repeat assertions in earlier pleadings without adding anything material" (O 9 r 15(2)). A claimant "need not file a reply to the defence if the claimant merely wishes to deny assertions without adding anything material" (O 6 r 9(1)).

The plan to "deny everything" adds nothing material and will not be allowed. But November's limitation plea raises a new issue to which Mike has a positive answer: that the right of action was concealed by fraud, so time did not run until he discovered it or could with reasonable diligence have discovered it (s 29(1)(b) Limitation Act 1959; *Chua Teck Chew Robert v Goh Eng Wah* [2009] 4 SLR(R) 716 at [25]–[33]). That is a material matter November must know before trial. Mike should raise it at the case conference (filing further pleadings is also a SAPT matter: O 9 r 9(4)(g)), explaining the matter to be pleaded.

Therefore Mike should not file a bare denial, but should ask the Court at the case conference for approval to file a reply pleading concealment under s 29(1)(b), with particulars of when and how he discovered the fraud.

**Qdrill-10** · `0.V.8` `0.III.4` · drill-10 · `drp-w-0-27`

Oscar Pte Ltd was served in Singapore on 1 October 2026 with Papa Pte Ltd's originating application (and affidavit) seeking a declaration on the construction of a shareholders' agreement. Oscar wants to resist and to counterclaim for a declaration on a related clause. After Oscar files, Papa wants to file an affidavit "to strengthen its evidence". Advise both sides.

**Answer.**

The issues are Oscar's deadline and what its affidavit must contain, and whether Papa may file a further affidavit. Oscar must file and serve one affidavit containing all its evidence, including its counterclaim, within 21 days after service; Papa may file a further affidavit only in a special case, which a wish to strengthen its evidence is not.

A defendant served in Singapore "must file and serve the defendant's affidavit within 21 days after being served with the claimant's originating application and affidavit, if the defendant wishes to introduce evidence" (O 6 r 12(1)). The affidavit must contain all the evidence necessary or material to the defence (O 6 r 13), and a defendant who intends to counterclaim "must include it in the defendant's affidavit together with all the evidence necessary for the counterclaim" (O 6 r 14). "Except in a special case, no further affidavits may be filed after the defendant files the defendant's affidavit on the merits" (O 6 r 12(6)). The parallel rule for applications intends "only one round of affidavits from each party, except in a 'special case'"; new issues in an opponent's affidavit may be a special case if they could not reasonably have been within the applicant's contemplation, but a wait-and-see approach is not (*CZD v CZE* [2023] 5 SLR 806 at [19]–[21]).

Oscar's 21 days begin on 2 October and end on 22 October 2026 (O 3 r 3(3)). Its affidavit must set out its whole case on the clause and its counterclaim on the related clause, with all supporting evidence. Papa's wish "to strengthen its evidence" is not a special case; Papa could respond only if Oscar's affidavit raised matters Papa could not reasonably have foreseen, such as a new allegation about the parties' negotiations, and even then only on those matters.

Therefore Oscar must file one affidavit with all its evidence and its counterclaim by 22 October 2026, and Papa may not file a further affidavit unless it shows a special case under O 6 r 12(6).

### 0.VI.2 — Absence of parties at a case conference; no defence by the first case conference

**Qdrill-11** · `0.VI.2` `0.VI.1` · drill-11 · `drp-w-0-29` `drp-w-0-28`

(a) Quebec's solicitor missed the first case conference in Quebec's originating claim against Romeo because of a diary error; the Court dismissed the action. (b) In Sierra's action against Tango, both attended the first case conference, but Tango had filed no defence and its time had expired. What happens in each case?

**Answer.**

The issues are the consequences of a claimant's absence, and of a defendant's failure to file a defence, by the first case conference. (a) Quebec should apply promptly to set aside the dismissal, showing a valid reason for its absence. (b) The Court may enter judgment in default of defence against Tango, though it may in a special case extend Tango's time.

"If no party attends the case conference or if the claimant is absent, the Court may dismiss the action" (O 9 r 4(1)); "if the claimant attends but the defendant is absent, the Court may give judgment for the claimant upon proof of service" (O 9 r 4(2)); and the Court "may set aside or vary the dismissal or default judgment on proof that there were valid reasons for the absence" (O 9 r 4(3)). If both parties attend and no defence has been served by the first case conference although the time has expired, "the Court may enter judgment against the defendant in default of a defence" (O 9 r 6(1)); it may set aside or vary that judgment (O 9 r 6(2)) and may, in a special case, extend time for the defence, failing which the defendant is deemed not to have served one (O 9 r 6(3)). A case conference is held 8 weeks after issue where the defendant is served in Singapore (O 9 r 1(1)(a)).

(a) A diary error is a genuine, if unimpressive, reason; Quebec should apply at once under O 9 r 4(3), with an affidavit from the solicitor explaining the error, and offer to bear the costs of the application and the wasted conference. (b) Sierra should ask for judgment under O 9 r 6(1). Tango's only hope is to show a special case for an extension under O 9 r 6(3), for example a defence ready for filing that raises a real issue, with a good explanation for the delay.

Therefore Quebec should apply to set aside the dismissal for valid reasons under O 9 r 4(3), and Sierra may obtain default judgment against Tango under O 9 r 6(1) unless Tango shows a special case.

### 0.VI.4 — AEICs before documents (AB4D)

**Qdrill-12** · `0.VI.4` `0.IX.3` · drill-12 · `drp-w-0-31`

Uniform Pte Ltd sues Victor Pte Ltd, alleging an oral agreement made at two meetings to extend a distribution term. Victor's internal board papers about the meetings are held only by Victor. At the case conference the Registrar proposes that the parties exchange witness lists and AEICs before any exchange of documents. Uniform asks whether to object.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether the Court should order AEICs before documents, and what that means for Uniform. The Court has power to make the order and treats it as the default, but Uniform has a recognised ground to resist it: highly relevant documents it needs to prepare its witnesses' evidence are held only by Victor.

O 9 r 8(1) provides that, after pleadings and before any exchange of documents, "the Court may, in any particular case, order the parties to file and serve their lists of witnesses and the affidavits of evidence-in-chief of all or some of the witnesses simultaneously or in any sequence". The Courts' Digest 4 (para 12) states that "an order for AB4D will be the default and is not confined only to exceptional circumstances", but that it may not be appropriate where highly relevant documents necessary for a party to prepare its evidence are in the possession or control of the other party only. Once AB4D is ordered, production of requested documents may not be ordered before the parties comply with it, except in a special case (O 11 r 3(3)). Parties state their position in the Pre-Case Conference Questionnaire (Form B6, question 5a; PD 56(10)–(11)).

AB4D suits a dispute about what was said at meetings, because witnesses' independent recollections are captured before documents can shape them. But Uniform's witnesses need Victor's board papers, which record Victor's own account of the meetings and are available only from Victor; without them, Uniform's AEICs would be incomplete, which is the Digest's exception. Uniform should object on that ground, or ask that, if AB4D is ordered, Victor first produce the board papers as a specific category, since after the order it will need a special case to get them.

Therefore Uniform should object to AB4D, or at least seek production of Victor's board papers first, because O 11 r 3(3) will otherwise bar requested-document production until AEICs are exchanged.

### 0.VII.8 — Amendment after the limitation period has expired

**Qdrill-13** · `0.VII.8` `0.VII.7` · drill-13 · `drp-w-0-39` `drp-w-0-38`

In 2025 Whiskey sued "Xray Pte Ltd" for breach of warranty over a machine sold in May 2019. It is now November 2026 and the 6-year limitation period has expired. Whiskey wants to amend to: (a) correct the defendant's name to "Xray Holdings Pte Ltd", the company that actually sold the machine and has defended throughout; (b) add a claim in misrepresentation based on the same sales statements; (c) add a claim based on statements made at a different meeting in 2020 about a second machine. Advise.

**Answer.**

The issue is which amendments the Court may allow after the limitation period has expired. Amendments (a) and (b) fall within O 9 r 14(4) ROC and may be allowed; (c) relies on new facts and should be refused.

O 9 r 14(4) provides that where permission to amend is sought after the relevant limitation period has expired, the Court may allow "(a) an amendment to correct the name of a party even if its alleged effect will be to substitute a new party, if it was a genuine mistake and was not misleading as to the identity of the party in question"; "(b) an amendment to alter the capacity in which a party sues …"; and "(c) an amendment to add or substitute a new cause of action, if the new cause of action arises out of the same or substantially the same facts as an existing cause of action for which relief has already been claimed in the same action". A new cause of action relying on new factual material is not allowed (*Management Corporation Strata Title Plan No 3322 v Mer Vue Developments Pte Ltd* [2016] 4 SLR 351 at [48]; *Multistar Holdings Ltd v Geocon Piling & Engineering Pte Ltd* [2016] 2 SLR 1). Even within r 14(4), the general discretion applies (*Wang Piao v Lee Wee Ching* [2024] 4 SLR 540 at [16]–[19]).

(a) The mistake was genuine and misled no one: Xray Holdings sold the machine and has defended throughout, so it knew it was the intended defendant. (b) The misrepresentation claim relies on the very sales statements already pleaded as warranties, so it arises out of substantially the same facts. (c) Statements at a different 2020 meeting about a different machine are new factual material; the claim is time-barred and r 14(4)(c) does not save it. Whiskey will also bear the costs occasioned by the amendments.

Therefore the Court may allow amendments (a) under O 9 r 14(4)(a) and (b) under r 14(4)(c), but should refuse (c).

### 0.VIII.5 — Determining a question of law or construction without a trial

**Qdrill-14** · `0.VIII.5` `0.VII.1` · drill-14 · `drp-w-0-44` `drp-w-0-32`

Yankee Pte Ltd sues Zulu Logistics Pte Ltd for $900,000 for goods damaged in storage. Zulu relies on cl 14 of the storage contract, which excludes liability "for any loss howsoever caused"; Yankee says cl 14 does not cover Zulu's own negligence. The facts of the storage and the damage are agreed. Can the cl 14 issue be decided before trial?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether the Court can decide the construction of cl 14 without a trial. Yes: it may do so under O 9 r 19 ROC, because the question is one of construction on agreed facts, and deciding it would dispose of the claim or a major issue.

O 9 r 19(1) provides that, "upon a party's application or on the Court's own accord, the Court may decide any question of law or the construction of any document arising in any action without a trial or hearing on the facts, whether or not such decision will fully determine the action"; where it fully determines the matter or a claim or issue, the Court may give judgment or dismiss the action (O 9 r 19(2)). Two conjunctive requirements apply: the issue must be suitable for determination without a full trial, and resolving it must fully determine the whole matter or at least one claim or issue (*Hyflux Ltd v SM Investments Pte Ltd* [2020] 4 SLR 1265 at [34]). A suitable question is one based on facts that are agreed or undisputed and duly pleaded (*United Overseas Bank Ltd v Lippo Marina Collection Pte Ltd* [2016] 2 SLR 597 at [15]), and the Court will decline where the meaning turns on disputed facts (*Babbobox Pte Ltd v Epiq Singapore Pte Ltd* [2026] SGHC 66 at [50]–[52]).

The meaning of cl 14 is a question of construction; the material facts are agreed; and if cl 14 excludes liability for negligence, Yankee's claim fails, while if it does not, the exclusion defence falls away and only quantum may remain. Either way the determination saves the cost of a trial on the clause. If Zulu relied on disputed background (for example, negotiations about insurance), the question would become unsuitable. Procedure: determination of questions of law or construction is a SAPT matter (O 9 r 9(4)(j)), or the Court may raise it of its own accord at a case conference.

Therefore either party may apply in its SAPT for the Court to construe cl 14 under O 9 r 19, and if the answer disposes of the claim the Court may give judgment or dismiss the action under r 19(2).

### 0.IX.1 — What a document is; Order 11's principles; broader discovery

**Qdrill-15** · `0.IX.1` `0.IX.3` · drill-15 · `drp-w-0-45`

Alpha Pte Ltd sues its former CTO for breach of confidence. It wants (a) the CTO's WhatsApp messages with a competitor and (b) "all of the CTO's personal emails from 2023 to 2025, in case they show something". The CTO says chat messages are not "documents" and that Alpha is fishing. Advise.

**Answer.**

The issues are whether chat messages are documents for production, and whether Alpha can obtain a wide trawl of personal emails. The messages are documents; the trawl is unlikely to be ordered unless the CTO agrees or the Court allows broader discovery, which Alpha would have to justify as aiding a fair disposal.

Electronically stored information is a "document" for production (*Sanae Achar v Sci-Gen Ltd* [2011] 3 SLR 967 at [10]; Evidence Act 1893 s 3(1)). In exercising its powers under Order 11 the Court bears in mind the Ideals and "(a) that a claimant is to sue and proceed on the strength of the claimant's case and not on the weakness of the defendant's case; (b) that a party who sues or is sued in court does not thereby give up the party's right to privacy and confidentiality" (O 11 r 1(2)). Order 11 does not affect an agreement to broaden production, or the Court's power to allow broader discovery in the interests of justice (O 11 r 1(1), (3)), which exists "where broader discovery could aid in disposing fairly of the proceedings" (O 11 r 1(4)); on such an application r 1(2) does not apply (O 11 r 1(5)).

(a) The WhatsApp messages are documents. If they are material to Alpha's pleaded case (that confidential information passed to the competitor), Alpha can request them under O 11 r 3 by identifying them properly (for example, messages with named persons at the competitor in a stated period). (b) A request for all personal emails "in case they show something" offends r 1(2)(a) and (b): Alpha must prove its case on its own strength and the CTO keeps his privacy. Unless the CTO agrees, Alpha must apply for broader discovery and show that it could aid a fair disposal, which a speculative trawl cannot; private correspondence is in any case protected unless it is a known adverse document or a special case (O 11 r 5(2)).

Therefore the WhatsApp messages are documents that may be ordered produced if properly identified and material, but the request for all personal emails will be refused unless Alpha narrows it or justifies broader discovery under O 11 r 1(4).

### 0.IX.5 — Limits on production: train of inquiry, internal correspondence, privilege, confidentiality

**Qdrill-16** · `0.IX.5` `0.IX.2` · drill-16 · `drp-w-0-49`

In Bravo Pte Ltd's claim against Charlie Pte Ltd over a price-adjustment clause, Charlie refuses to produce: (a) internal emails between its sales staff about the clause; (b) its in-house counsel's advice on the clause; (c) its confidential pricing spreadsheets, which Bravo says are material; and (d) "any documents that might lead to evidence of how the clause was negotiated". Bravo also received one of Charlie's privileged emails by mistake. Advise Bravo.

**Answer.**

The issue is which categories Charlie may withhold. Charlie may withhold the privileged advice and need not produce documents that merely lead to a train of inquiry; it need not produce internal emails unless they are known adverse documents or there is a special case, but it must itself say whether any are adverse; and it cannot withhold the spreadsheets for confidentiality. Bravo cannot rely on the misdirected privileged email.

Except in a special case, the Court must not order production of a document "that merely leads a party on a train of inquiry to other documents" (O 11 r 5(1)), or of a party's "private or internal correspondence … (including email …) unless (a) it is a special case; or (b) such correspondence are known adverse documents" (O 11 r 5(2)); and it must not order production of privileged documents or where production would be contrary to the public interest (O 11 r 5(3)). A party "may not withhold or object to production on the ground that a document is confidential" (O 11 r 9(1)). A privileged document "must not be relied on unless the party entitled to the privilege consents or the Court approves", and does not lose its privilege if disclosed inadvertently (O 11 r 8). The requested party must deny the existence of known adverse documents (including internal ones), assert privilege, or disclose them; it is not for the requesting party to prove they exist (*Cachet Multi Strategy Fund SPC v Feng Shi* [2024] SGHC 327 at [24]).

(a) Internal sales emails are protected by r 5(2), but Charlie must say whether any of them are known adverse documents and produce those (*Cachet*). (b) Legal advice is privileged (r 5(3)). (c) Material pricing spreadsheets must be produced; confidentiality can be managed by redaction of irrelevant parts or a confidentiality order, but is no ground to refuse (r 9). (d) A request for anything "that might lead to evidence" is a train of inquiry and fails absent a special case (r 5(1)). As to the misdirected email, Bravo must not rely on it without Charlie's consent or the Court's approval (r 8), and should tell Charlie's solicitors.

Therefore Bravo can obtain the pricing spreadsheets and any internal emails that are known adverse documents, but not the privileged advice, the train-of-inquiry category or the misdirected privileged email.

### 0.X Witnesses and evidence at trial — drills

### 0.X.1 — Fact witnesses: evidence by AEIC; oral evidence only in a special case

**Qdrill-17** · `0.X.1` `0.X.3` · drill-17 · `drp-w-0-51` `drp-w-0-53`

Delta Pte Ltd wants two witnesses to give oral evidence-in-chief at trial instead of filing AEICs: (a) its own operations manager, who "finds affidavits tedious"; (b) a former employee of the defendant who saw the key events but refuses to cooperate, and whom Delta will compel by an order to attend court. Delta's opponent says it will object to both at trial. Advise.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether either witness may give evidence-in-chief orally. Only in a special case, with the Court's permission: the cooperative manager must file an AEIC, but the hostile former employee is a proper special case; and the opponent must object when Delta applies, not at trial.

"As a general rule, the trial in an originating claim … must be decided on the basis of the witnesses' AEICs, cross-examination, re-examination and oral or written submissions" (O 15 r 16(1)), and "in a special case, the Court may allow a witness' evidence-in-chief to be given orally instead of by AEIC" (O 15 r 16(2)). An AEIC must contain all material facts, which may not be departed from or supplemented in oral evidence unless they occurred after it was made (O 15 r 16(4)). An objection to a party's application to call a witness without an AEIC must be taken at that application, not collaterally at trial (*Basil Anthony Herman v Premier Security Cooperative Ltd* [2010] 3 SLR 110 at [78]).

(a) Finding affidavits tedious is mere convenience; the AEIC system exists to save costs and prevent surprise, so the manager must file an AEIC with all material facts. (b) A non-party who refuses to cooperate cannot be made to sign an AEIC; Delta will serve an order to attend court (O 15 r 4) and should apply for permission for his evidence-in-chief to be given orally, which is a special case. The opponent must oppose that application when Delta makes it; if it stays silent, it cannot raise the point at trial (*Basil Anthony Herman*).

Therefore the manager must give evidence by AEIC, while Delta should apply under O 15 r 16(2) for the former employee to give oral evidence-in-chief, and the opponent must object then or not at all.

### 0.X.5 — Absence of a party at trial

**Qdrill-18** · `0.X.5` `0.I.3` · drill-18 · `drp-w-0-55` `drp-w-0-2`

On the first day of trial of Echo's claim against Foxtrot, neither Foxtrot nor its solicitors appear. The Judge proceeds and gives judgment for Echo. Foxtrot's director says the firm had "forgotten the date". Advise Echo on what the Court could do, and Foxtrot on how to respond.

**Answer.**

The issue is the consequence of a party's absence at the start of trial, and how the absent party may undo it. The Court may proceed with the trial and give judgment against Foxtrot; Foxtrot may apply to set the judgment aside within 14 days of learning of it, but must explain its absence and show a real defence.

"If one of the parties does not attend when the trial begins, the Court may proceed with the trial or give judgment against or dismiss the claim of the absent party or make any other appropriate order" (O 15 r 8(4)); if none attends, the Court may dismiss the claim and any counterclaim (O 15 r 8(3)). Parties must attend in person, if unrepresented, or by a solicitor (O 15 r 3(1)); a party represented by counsel who attends is not absent (*Lam Hwa Engineering & Trading Pte Ltd v Yang Qiang* [2013] 2 SLR 524 at [19]). A judgment obtained in the absence of the party affected may be revoked or set aside if in the interests of justice, on an application made within 14 days after the party knows or should have known of the ground (O 3 r 2(8)(a), (9)–(10)).

Echo was entitled to ask the Judge to proceed on its evidence, and the judgment stands unless set aside. Foxtrot must apply immediately, within 14 days of learning of the judgment, with an affidavit explaining the absence and showing that it has a real defence. A solicitor's diary error is an explanation the Court may accept if promptly raised and if Foxtrot shows a defence worth trying; Foxtrot will almost certainly be ordered to pay the costs thrown away, and its solicitors risk a personal costs order for costs incurred unreasonably (O 21 r 6).

Therefore the Court may give judgment against the absent Foxtrot under O 15 r 8(4), and Foxtrot's only remedy is a prompt application under O 3 r 2(8)(a), on terms as to costs.

### 0.XI.1 — Source of the power; interim and interlocutory injunctions; freestanding injunctions

**Qdrill-19** · `0.XI.1` `0.XI.2` `0.XI.4` · drill-19 · `drp-w-0-56` `drp-w-0-57` `drp-w-0-59`

Golf Pty Ltd has sued Hotel Ltd in the Federal Court of Australia for US$2 million. Hotel has no assets in Australia but holds money in a Singapore bank account, and Golf has evidence that Hotel is moving it. Golf has no claim to bring in Singapore. Can the Singapore court help, and how?

**Answer.**

The issue is whether a Singapore court can grant interim relief in aid of foreign proceedings where no Singapore action exists. Yes: the court may grant interim relief, including a Mareva injunction, in aid of proceedings commenced outside Singapore if it appears just or convenient (s 4(10A) Civil Law Act 1909), provided the Australian judgment would be enforceable here.

A court may grant an injunction by interlocutory order "in all cases in which it appears to the court to be just or convenient" (s 4(10) CLA). Under s 4(10A), it "has power to grant interim relief in aid of proceedings in any civil or commercial matter … which have been or are to be commenced outside Singapore, if it appears just or convenient"; it may refuse if (a) its lack of jurisdiction apart from s 4(10A) makes the order inappropriate, or (b) the foreign proceedings cannot give rise to a judgment enforceable in Singapore (s 4(10C)); "interim relief" excludes arrest of property and obtaining evidence (s 4(10D)). Interim and interlocutory injunctions are the same thing, an injunction made pending final disposal (*Tay Long Kee Impex Pte Ltd v Tan Beng Huwah* [2000] SGCA 22). The Court has also recognised freestanding injunctions not tied to a substantive claim before it (*Sulzer Pumps Spain, SA v Hyflux Membrane Manufacturing (S) Pte Ltd* [2020] 5 SLR 634 at [75]–[81]); for Mareva relief in aid of foreign proceedings see *Bi Xiaoqiong v China Medical Technologies, Inc* [2019] 2 SLR 595 at [27]–[126], now read with s 4(10A).

Golf's claim is a civil or commercial matter commenced in Australia. Golf must show that an Australian judgment would be enforceable in Singapore (s 4(10C)(b)), with Australian law evidence on the route to enforcement here. It then needs the Mareva requirements: a good arguable case in the Australian action, assets in Singapore, and a real risk of dissipation shown by solid evidence (*Farooq Ahmad Mann v Xia Zheng* [2024] SGHC 182 at [21]–[23]). Procedure: because no Singapore action exists, Golf applies by originating application without notice, supported by an affidavit stating the urgency, why Hotel should not be told and the merits (O 13 r 1(3)), using Form 24 (O 13 r 1(6)), with full and frank disclosure (O 13 r 1(5)) and an undertaking as to damages; if Hotel is outside Singapore, Golf needs the Court's approval to serve the OA and the order on it there (O 8 r 1).

Therefore Golf may apply by originating application without notice for a Mareva injunction over Hotel's Singapore account under s 4(10A) CLA, showing that the Australian judgment would be enforceable here and a real risk of dissipation.

### 0.XI.5 — Search orders

**Qdrill-20** · `0.XI.5` `0.XI.2` · drill-20 · `drp-w-0-60` `drp-w-0-57`

India Pte Ltd owns the registered mark "ZESTO" for sauces. Test purchases show Juliet Pte Ltd selling identical-looking "ZESTO" bottles from an unmarked warehouse; a former Juliet employee says Juliet's director keeps the supply records on one laptop and has twice before "cleared everything" when complaints arrived. India wants to seize the stock and records before Juliet is warned. Advise.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether India can obtain a search order. It can apply without notice to a Judge for a search order in Form 26, and on these facts has a good chance, if it frames the order narrowly and uses an independent supervising solicitor.

A search order is an exceptional remedy, granted only if "(a) there is an extremely strong prima facie case; (b) the damage the claimant would suffer would be very serious; (c) there is a real possibility the defendant would destroy relevant documents; and (d) the effect of the order is proportionate to its legitimate object" (*Asian Corporate Services (SEA) Pte Ltd v Eastwest Management Ltd (Singapore Branch)* [2006] 1 SLR(R) 901 at [14], applied in *Arfat Pannir Selvam v Sharad Selvam Ramachandra* [2026] SGHC 56 at [34]). The risk of destruction may be inferred from the defendant's conduct and propensity ([41], [47]–[50] of *Arfat*); where goods are sold openly, there may be no real risk of destruction (*Bengawan Solo Pte Ltd v Season Confectionary Co Pte Ltd* [1994] 1 SLR(R) 448). The order must be in Form 26 (O 13 r 1(8)), is heard by a Judge (PD 72(1)), and must be served and carried out under an experienced independent supervising solicitor, not from the applicant's firm, identified in the affidavit (PD 72(5)). The application is made without notice with a PD 73 affidavit, full and frank disclosure (O 13 r 1(5)) and an undertaking as to damages, and needs no prior approval (O 9 r 9(7)(a)).

(a) The test purchases of identical bottles bearing India's registered mark make a very strong prima facie case. (b) Counterfeit sauces threaten lost sales and harm to India's reputation, potentially to health. (c) The former employee's evidence that the director has twice "cleared everything" when warned shows propensity, and the records are on a single portable laptop; unlike *Bengawan Solo*, the stock is held in an unmarked warehouse, not sold openly from a shop. (d) To be proportionate, the order should cover only the warehouse and the laptop's supply records, not Juliet's whole business; India should consider whether a preservation order or Mareva would suffice and explain why not.

Therefore India should apply without notice to a Judge for a narrowly framed Form 26 search order, supported by a PD 73 affidavit with full disclosure, an undertaking as to damages and an identified independent supervising solicitor.

### 0.XII.1 — Further arguments

**Qdrill-21** · `0.XII.1` `0.XII.4` · drill-21 · `drp-w-0-62` `drp-w-0-65`

On Monday 2 November 2026 a General Division Judge, in chambers, dismissed Kilo Pte Ltd's application to strike out Lima's claim. Kilo's counsel has since found a Court of Appeal decision the Judge was not referred to, and wants to put it to the Judge before deciding whether to appeal. The order has not been extracted. Advise.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether Kilo can ask the Judge to reconsider before appealing, and how that affects the time to appeal. Kilo may request further arguments under s 29B SCJA, by letter to the Registrar, before the order is extracted and by Tuesday 17 November 2026; no appeal or permission application may be filed until the Judge decides, and time runs from then.

Section 29B applies to a decision of a General Division Judge "after any hearing other than a trial of an action" (s 29B(1)). Before any notice of appeal is filed, the Judge may hear further arguments if a party requests them before the earlier of "(a) the time the judgment or order is extracted" and "(b) the 15th day after the date of the decision" (s 29B(2)); the Judge may then affirm, vary or set aside the decision (s 29B(3)); no notice of appeal may be filed until the Judge does so or certifies that no further arguments are required, and time for appealing begins on that date (s 29B(4)). The request is a letter to the Registrar, served on all parties, setting out the arguments briefly with copies of authorities; if the Registrar does not respond within 14 days, the Judge is deemed not to require further arguments (O 18 r 28; PD 112). Where permission to appeal is needed, the request must be made before the permission application (O 18 r 29(2)). Further arguments are not available after a trial (*TG Master Pte Ltd v Tung Kee Development (Singapore) Pte Ltd* [2023] SGHC 64 at [21]–[50]), and further evidence may support new arguments but not strengthen old ones (*ARW v Comptroller of Income Tax* [2019] 1 SLR 499 at [85]–[92]).

The decision was made in chambers on an application, not after a trial, so s 29B applies. The 15th day after 2 November 2026 is 17 November 2026; Kilo must file before then, and before the order is extracted. The new Court of Appeal authority is a proper new argument. Kilo should also note that an appeal against "an order refusing to strike out an action, pleading or part" needs permission (Fifth Schedule para 3(e) SCJA), which is why the further-arguments request must come first (O 18 r 29(2)).

Therefore Kilo should file a Request for further arguments with the new authority before extraction and by 17 November 2026; its time to seek permission to appeal will run from the Judge's response (or 14 days of silence).

### 0.XIV.1 — Costs of contentious business; solicitor-and-client and party-and-party costs

**Qdrill-22** · `0.XIV.1` `0.XIV.2` · drill-22 · `drp-w-0-71` `drp-w-0-72`

Mike has agreed to pay his solicitors $120,000 to take his contract claim to trial, including pre-action letters and a mediation. He asks: "If I win, will the other side pay all of that, including the pre-action work?"

**Answer.**

The issue is what costs Mike can recover from the other side if he wins. He can recover only party-and-party costs fixed by the Court under a costs order, which usually cover part, not all, of his own solicitor-and-client costs, and never more than he owes his solicitors; his pre-action work counts as contentious business and can be included.

Order 21 applies to costs of or incidental to "contentious business" (O 21 r 1(1)), meaning "business done in or for the purposes of proceedings begun before a court of justice or before an arbitrator" (s 2(1) Legal Profession Act 1966). "No party is entitled to recover any costs from another except under an order of the Court" (O 21 r 3(1)), and the Court must order costs in favour of a successful party unless some other order should be made (O 21 r 3(2)). A client "is not entitled to recover from any other person, under any order for payment of costs which are the subject of a costs agreement, more than the amount payable by the client to [its] own solicitor" (s 112(2) LPA). Contentious business includes work done before the writ, such as drafting and pre-action negotiations, and mediation after the writ (*Legis Point LLC v Tay Choon Ai* [2018] 3 SLR 1269 at [79]–[84]); full recovery of one's own costs is the exception (*Maryani Sadeli v Arjun Permanand Samtani* [2015] 1 SLR 496 at [34]).

The $120,000 is Mike's solicitor-and-client bill. If he wins, the Court will fix the party-and-party costs he recovers, having regard to the O 21 r 2(2) factors (complexity, the solicitors' time and skill, conduct, proportionality and the stage at which the case ended). His pre-action letters and the mediation are contentious business and may form part of that claim. He will recover a proportion, not the whole, and in no event more than the $120,000 he owes.

Therefore Mike should expect to recover only part of his $120,000, as party-and-party costs fixed by the Court, including pre-action work, capped by what he owes his solicitors under s 112(2) LPA.

### 0.XIV.3 — Departing from the usual order; unreasonable refusal of ADR

**Qdrill-23** · `0.XIV.3` `0.II.3` · drill-23 · `drp-w-0-73` `drp-w-0-7`

November Pte Ltd won its $600,000 claim against Oscar Pte Ltd, but lost on two of its four pleaded issues (a fraud allegation that took three trial days, and a minor interest point argued in ten minutes). Before trial, November rejected Oscar's proposal to mediate without giving reasons, and the Court had ordered November to file a sealed document explaining its refusal. Advise Oscar on costs.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether November, the successful party, should be deprived of some of its costs. The Court may disallow or reduce November's costs, or order it to pay some of Oscar's, for the failed fraud allegation and for unreasonably refusing mediation (O 21 r 4(a)–(c) ROC), but not for the minor interest point; departures are exceptional, so Oscar should expect a percentage reduction rather than a reversal.

The Court may disallow or reduce a successful party's costs or order it to pay costs if it "(a) has failed to establish any claim or issue which that party has raised …, thereby unnecessarily increasing the amount of time taken, the costs or the complexity of the proceedings; (b) has done or omitted to do anything unreasonably; (c) has not discharged that party's duty to consider amicable resolution …; or (d) has failed to comply with any order of court … or any practice direction" (O 21 r 4). Solicitors must advise clients on potential adverse costs orders for unreasonable refusal to engage in ADR (PD 53(6)). A party refusing ADR may be ordered to give its reasons in a sealed document, opened after the merits are decided and referred to on costs (O 5 r 3(3)–(4)). Departures from costs following the event are exceptional (*Tullio Planeta v Maoro Andrew G* [1994] 2 SLR(R) 501 at [21]–[24]); a party losing on a point that did not increase time or costs is not penalised (*Management Corporation Strata Title Plan No 473 v De Beers Jewelry Pte Ltd* [2002] 1 SLR(R) 418 at [57]–[58]); and the factors are not exhaustive (*Lim Lie Hoa v Ong Jane Rebecca* [2005] 3 SLR(R) 116 at [60]–[64]).

The failed fraud allegation took three trial days, so it unnecessarily increased time and cost: the Court may disallow November's costs of that issue, or order November to pay Oscar's (r 4(a)). The interest point took ten minutes and added nothing material, so *De Beers* protects November there. The refusal to mediate without reasons engages r 4(c): Oscar should ask the Court to open November's sealed document, and if its reasons were not reasonable, ask for a further reduction.

Therefore Oscar should ask the Court to disallow November's costs of the fraud issue (and award Oscar its costs of that issue), and to reduce November's remaining costs for refusing mediation, but should not expect any adjustment for the interest point.

### 0.XIV.4 — Personal costs orders against solicitors; fictitious authorities and AI

**Qdrill-24** · `0.XIV.4` · drill-24 · `drp-w-0-74`

In Papa's action against Quebec, Quebec's closing submissions cite two "Court of Appeal" cases that do not exist. Quebec's solicitor on record admits an associate drafted the submissions with an AI tool and nobody checked the citations. Papa's counsel spent a day searching for the cases. Advise Papa on costs.

**Answer.**

The issue is whether Quebec's solicitors may be ordered to pay Papa's wasted costs personally. Yes: under O 21 r 6 ROC the Court may order a solicitor who incurred costs unreasonably to indemnify Papa for the costs wasted, after giving the solicitor a reasonable opportunity to be heard.

"If the solicitor is responsible, either personally or through an employee or agent, for incurring costs unreasonably in the proceedings, the Court may (a) disallow the costs as between the solicitor and his or her client …; (b) order the solicitor to repay to his or her client costs which the client has been ordered to pay …; and (c) order the solicitor to indemnify any other party in the proceedings for costs payable by them" (O 21 r 6(1)). The Court must first give the solicitor a reasonable opportunity to be heard (O 21 r 6(2)) and may direct notice to the client (O 21 r 6(3)). Where fictitious authorities were cited in closing submissions, the High Court, applying O 21 r 6 and the three-stage test of improper or unreasonable conduct, unnecessary costs caused, and whether it is just to order the solicitor to pay, held that the solicitor on record had a non-delegable duty to verify the authorities, and ordered him and the lawyer who drafted the submissions each to pay $5,000 personally on an indemnity basis (*Tan Hai Peng Micheal v Tan Cheong Joo* [2026] SGHC 49 at [34]–[66], [84]); personal costs may also follow unmeritorious applications that abuse the process (*Nagaenthran a/l K Dharmalingam v Attorney-General* [2022] SGCA 44).

Citing non-existent cases generated by an AI tool without checking them is unreasonable conduct for which the solicitor is responsible, personally and through the associate. It caused Papa a day of wasted counsel time. The solicitor on record cannot shift the blame to the associate, since the duty to verify is non-delegable. Papa should ask the Court for an order under r 6(1)(c) that Quebec's solicitor (and, following *Tan Hai Peng Micheal*, the drafting lawyer) indemnify Papa for the wasted costs; the Court must first hear them. Disciplinary consequences are a separate matter for the Ethics paper.

Therefore the Court may order Quebec's solicitors personally to pay Papa's wasted costs under O 21 r 6(1)(c), after hearing them.
