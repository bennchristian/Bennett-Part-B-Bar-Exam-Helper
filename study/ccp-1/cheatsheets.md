### ccp-1-0-1 — Why companies raise finance; factors in the choice
*Keywords: why raise finance, working capital, capital expenditure, capex, investments, acquisitions, credit quality, industry, tax, available sources, market conditions, flexibility, pricing, choice of finance*

**End point:** Companies need finance for (a) working capital (day-to-day operations) and (b) investments, acquisitions and capital expenditure (acquiring, upgrading and maintaining physical assets such as property, buildings and equipment). Six factors influence the choice of finance: credit quality, the company's industry, tax, available sources and market conditions, the need for flexibility, and pricing.

*Source note: BF1 s3–4; Cyril-BF §1.1.*

### ccp-1-0-2 — Types of finance; methods of raising debt
*Keywords: types of financing, equity, debt, private, public, venture capital, term loan, IPO, bonds, notes, debt securities, medium term notes, commercial paper, bank loans, vendor financing, securitisation*

**End point:** Finance is equity or debt, raised privately or publicly: private equity = subscriptions from private investors (e.g. venture capital); public equity = offers of shares to the public or existing shareholders (e.g. IPO); private debt = borrowing from banks or other corporates (e.g. term loan); public debt = issuing debt instruments (notes or bonds). Debt is raised by bank loans (bilateral or syndicated) or by debt securities: bonds (usually maturity of a year or more), medium-term notes under MTN programmes, and commercial paper (under a year). Other forms include vendor financing and securitisation.

> **Types of financing (BF1 s5)**
> - Equity, private: subscriptions from private investors (e.g. venture capital).
> - Equity, public: offer of shares to the public or existing shareholders (e.g. IPO).
> - Debt, private: borrowing from banks or other corporates (e.g. term loan).
> - Debt, public: issue of debt instruments (e.g. notes or bonds).

*Source note: BF1 s5; RL-Methods p1–3 (English-law note); Cyril-BF §1.1.*

### ccp-1-0-3 — Equity v debt: key features
*Keywords: equity v debt, shares v loan, shareholder, lender, dividends, profits, directors' discretion, interest, repayment, maturity date, increase in value, capital appreciation, insolvency, ranking, liquidation, creditors paid first, no market for private company shares, liability v asset, characterisation, Part B Course Pte Ltd, Big Whale LLP, annual payouts, dividend payments, payouts, no-lose investment, get all money back, sell shares for a profit*

**End point:** The course compares facilities on six features: characterisation, how the money is returned or realised, maturity date, returns, increase in value, and treatment in insolvency. A lender must be repaid on the agreed date(s) and receives interest whatever the company's profits, but does not share in its success. A shareholder's money is not repaid; he gets dividends only if the company has profits available and the directors decide to pay them, and can profit from a rise in value by selling (though there is no market for shares in a private company). On insolvency, creditors are paid first and shareholders rank last, so shareholders usually get nothing back because debts exceed assets. Shareholders are not liable to pay the company's debts.

> **Sample Questions**
> - SQ p6 Q1 (key A, II and V): correct the friend by saying dividends are paid only out of profits at the directors' discretion (II), and on insolvent winding up the assets go to creditors whose debts usually exceed them (V). Wrong: dividends are not necessarily paid twice a year (I); shares are property that can be sold (III); "no market for private company shares" was not the keyed correction (IV); shareholders do not pay the company's debts (VI).
> - SQ p9 Q6 (key A, II and IV): loan = regular payments or repayment at the end; shares = payments only from profits at the directors' discretion (II); only shares let the investor profit from the start-up's success (IV). Wrong: (I) the investor's loan and shares are both its assets (the loan is the start-up's liability); (III) neither guarantees full recovery in liquidation.

*Source note: BF1 s6 (key features); Cyril-BF §1.1 (equity v debt table) and §3.1; SQ p6 Q1, p9 Q6 and p114 (keys A, A). The reasons for the wrong options are applied here from the course comparison; the key gives the answer only.*

### ccp-1-0-4 — Bilateral v syndicated loans
*Keywords: bilateral loan, single lender, syndicated loan, multiple lenders, several liability, facility agent, club deal, risk distribution, advantages, disadvantages, confidentiality, larger amount, LMA documents, majority lenders*

**End point:** A bilateral loan has one lender; it suits smaller loans (term loans, overdrafts), is private, uses simpler documents and lower fees and allows flexible terms, but a large loan concentrates risk on one bank (so a higher margin) and the sum available may be smaller. A syndicated loan has several lenders lending on the same terms under one agreement, each severally (if one drops out the others need not make up its share), dealing with the borrower through a facility agent, which holds any security for all of them. It gives access to larger sums, usually at lower interest and in several currencies, but means complex documents, higher fees (arrangers, agents, underwriters, lawyers), adherence to market-standard terms, and less confidentiality.

*Source note: BF1 s8–9; RL-Types p1–2; Cyril-BF §1.2.1.*

### ccp-1-0-5 — Term loan, revolving facility, overdraft and other facilities
*Keywords: fixed term facility, term loan, revolving credit facility, RCF, re-borrow, overdraft, repayable on demand, working capital facility, clean-up, swingline, standby facility, bridging, availability period, tranches, instalments, bullet repayment*

**End point:** A term loan: the lender commits a set amount for a set period (typically up to 5 years); it is drawn during a short availability period, sometimes in tranches, and repaid in one sum or instalments; amounts repaid cannot be re-borrowed. A revolving credit facility: the lender commits up to a maximum, available for almost the whole life of the loan; the borrower can draw, repay and re-draw (more flexible, but with notice periods, minimum/maximum drawing limits and high commitment fees). An overdraft is usually uncommitted and repayable on demand, with interest on the amount actually used. Other types include swingline (very short-term, part of an RCF) and standby/bridging facilities.

*Source note: BF1 s10; RL-Types p2–5; Cyril-BF §1.2.2 (which describes a term loan's duration as "1 and 5 months"; read as up to 5 years per RL-Types p2).*

### ccp-1-0-6 — Committed v uncommitted; availability period; commitment fee
*Keywords: committed facility, uncommitted facility, lender obliged to lend if conditions met, discretion, availability period, drawdown window, commitment fee, undrawn amounts, Part B Course Pte Ltd, bank officer explanation*

**End point:** Under a committed facility the lender must advance on request once the conditions precedent are met; under an uncommitted facility it has discretion. A commitment fee is paid for the lender's keeping the facility available, calculated on the committed but undrawn amount during the availability period. For a term loan the availability period is the shorter period (or periods) in which drawdowns may be made, not the whole term. So a bank officer who says the fee is for the full 5-year term and is charged on amounts drawn is wrong on both counts (SQ p7 Q2, key A).

*Source note: RL-XB p2 (committed v uncommitted; fee on undrawn amount); RL-Types p2–3 (term loan: short availability period; commitment fee on committed amounts during it); Cyril-BF §1.3.1–1.3.2; SQ p7 Q2 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-0-7 — Secured v unsecured loans
*Keywords: secured loan, unsecured loan, collateral, priority on insolvency, lower interest, control in restructuring, restrictions on dealing with assets, power to grant security, guarantee*

**End point:** A secured loan protects the lender on the borrower's insolvency (priority over unsecured creditors) and gives it some control in a restructuring, and is usually cheaper and larger. For the borrower it restricts dealing with the secured assets, and it must check it has power to grant the security and is not breaching other agreements (e.g. a negative pledge). An unsecured loan leaves the borrower free to use its assets, but the lender may charge more or refuse to lend.

*Source note: BF1 s11; Cyril-BF §1.2.3; RL-Types p7–9.*

### ccp-1-0-8 — Syndicated loan v bond issue
*Keywords: syndicated loan v bond, advantages of bonds, broader investor base, fewer covenants, flexible interest, unsecured, less disclosure, tradeable, advantages of syndicated loan, drawdown flexibility, instalment repayment, multi-currency, confidential, renegotiation, smaller borrowers, common features, multiple lenders, interest, collateral, exchange traded*

**End point:** Bonds v a syndicated loan: access to a broader investor base, fewer covenants, more flexible interest options (fixed, floating, zero coupon), frequently unsecured, and disclosure usually limited to public information (e.g. financial statements, not management accounts); bonds are tradeable. A syndicated loan gives flexibility to draw when needed, instalment or revolving repayment (bonds usually repay in one bullet), multi-currency options, privacy (good for confidential purposes such as a takeover), easier renegotiation (bonds need bondholder meetings), and is open to smaller or weaker borrowers. Both involve borrowing from several lenders, pay a return as interest, and can be secured; only bonds are easily bought and sold on an exchange (SQ p12 Q10, key A: I, II and III).

*Source note: BF2 s5; RL-Methods p2–3; Cyril-BF §2.1.1 and "Extra stuff" (syndicated loan v bonds); SQ p12 Q10 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-0-9 — Syndication: arranger, bookrunner, IM, underwriting, agent, security trustee
*Keywords: syndication, arranger, mandated lead arranger, bookrunner, documentation bank, information memorandum, underwritten, best efforts, market flex, clear market, club deal, facility agent, mechanical and administrative, majority lenders, security trustee, transfer without re-registration*

**End point:** The arranger (mandated lead arranger) helps prepare and distribute the information memorandum (the borrower's document), negotiates the documents and manages syndication (as bookrunner). In an underwritten deal the underwriters guarantee the whole amount and take up any shortfall themselves (higher fees); on a best-efforts basis the borrower bears the shortfall risk. A market flex clause lets the arranger change pricing or terms to syndicate the loan. The facility agent's role is mechanical and administrative (notices, payments, drawdowns, acting on majority lenders' instructions after default) and it excludes fiduciary duties. A security trustee holds the security on trust for all lenders, so a lender's transfer passes the benefit of the security without re-registration.

*Source note: RL-Synd p1–9 (English-law note); RL-Sec p5 (security trustee in Singapore).*

### ccp-1-0-10 — Four categories of clauses
*Keywords: facility agreement, loan agreement, fundamental terms, mechanics, drawdown, conditions precedent, repayment, prepayment, costs of utilisation, general protection, covenants, events of default, representations, boilerplate*

**End point:** A facility agreement's clauses fall into four broad categories: (1) mechanics — drawdown and conditions precedent, repayment and prepayment; (2) costs of utilisation — interest, default interest and fees; (3) general protection — representations, covenants and events of default; (4) boilerplate.

*Source note: BF1 s13; Cyril-BF §1.3.*

### ccp-1-0-11 — Drawdown and conditions precedent
*Keywords: drawdown, drawdown notice, utilisation request, conditions precedent, CPs, first advance, every advance, representations remain true, no material adverse change, no event of default, legal opinions, board authority, signatories, security documents, lender obliged to lend*

**End point:** Before an advance the lender checks the conditions precedent, which confirm factual, legal and financial assumptions; once they are satisfied, the borrower has the right to the money and the lender must lend. Some CPs apply to the first advance only (e.g. constitutional documents, board authority, signatories, accounts, security documents, legal opinions), others to every advance. For each advance the borrower typically confirms that (a) the representations remain true, (b) no material adverse change has occurred since the original financial statements, and (c) no event of default exists or would result from the borrowing. Directors' confirmations that drawing is in the company's best interests, or new pro forma accounts, are not usual requirements (SQ p7 Q3, key A: I, II and V).

*Source note: BF1 s14; RL-XB p2–3; Cyril-BF §1.3.1; SQ p7–8 Q3 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-0-12 — Repayment and prepayment
*Keywords: repayment, specified date, schedule, instalments, on demand, prepayment, early repayment, prepayment fee, break costs, no right to prepay unless agreement allows, contractual penalty*

**End point:** Loans are repaid on a specified date or dates, by a schedule, or on demand (e.g. overdrafts). The borrower may prepay only if the agreement allows it; without a prepayment clause it has no right to repay early, and doing so may be a breach. A prepayment fee is usually required, compensating the lender for lost interest; it must be drafted so as not to be an unenforceable contractual penalty.

*Source note: BF1 s14; RL-XB p3; Cyril-BF §1.3.1.*

### ccp-1-0-13 — Interest, default interest, fees; benchmarks
*Keywords: interest, basic rate, benchmark, margin, associated costs rate, mandatory costs, interest period, default interest, penalty, commitment fee, prepayment fee, other fees, SORA, SIBOR, SOR, LIBOR discontinued*

**End point:** Interest = basic rate (the lender's cost of funds, a benchmark rate) + margin (for the credit risk) + an associated or mandatory costs rate (the lender's regulatory compliance costs); it is fixed at the start of each interest period and paid at its end. Default interest is a higher rate on overdue sums and should be reasonable so as not to be an unenforceable penalty. Other fees include commitment and prepayment fees. In Singapore, SORA (prescribed by MAS as a financial benchmark in 2020) has replaced SOR and SIBOR, and LIBOR has been discontinued.

*Source note: BF1 s15; RL-XB p4; Cyril-BF §1.3.2; RL-DCM p5–6 (SOR and SIBOR discontinuation timetable; SORA prescribed 5 Aug 2020).*

### ccp-1-0-14 — Representations and warranties
*Keywords: representations, warranties, legal representations, status, binding obligations, no conflict, power and authority, validity and admissibility in evidence, governing law, no filing or stamp taxes, pari passu ranking, no default, no misleading information, financial statements, no proceedings, insolvency, environmental, title to assets, repeating representations, misrepresentation*

**End point:** If a representation is untrue, the lender can refuse further advances and demand immediate repayment (misrepresentation is an event of default). Representations matter at signing and throughout the term, so many are repeated, e.g. on each drawdown or the first day of each interest period.

> **Customary representations (BF1 s16–17)**
> - Legal: status; binding obligations; no conflict with other obligations; power and authority; validity and admissibility in evidence; governing law and enforcement; deduction of tax; no filing or stamp taxes; pari passu ranking.
> - Information: no default; no misleading information; financial statements; no proceedings pending or threatened; authorised signatures.
> - State of business (possibly the whole group): insolvency; no breach of laws; environmental laws; taxation; holding and dormant companies; security and financial indebtedness.
> - Security / credit support: ranking; good title to assets, legal and beneficial ownership.

*Source note: BF1 s16–17; RL-XB p4; Cyril-BF §1.3.3.1.*

### ccp-1-0-15 — Covenants: general, financial, information
*Keywords: covenants, undertakings, positive covenants, negative covenants, general covenants, financial covenants, information covenants, negative pledge, quasi-security, pari passu, disposals, change of business, acquisitions, further borrowing, gearing, interest cover, net worth, loan to value, compliance certificate, monitor creditworthiness, audit inspection*

**End point:** Covenants protect the lender during the life of the facility by restricting activities that may put the borrower's creditworthiness at risk and letting the lender monitor it; breach is an event of default. They are positive or negative and fall into three groups: general covenants (commercial and protection of assets — e.g. negative pledge against giving security to others, including quasi-security; pari passu ranking; limits on disposals, acquisitions, further borrowing and change of business), financial covenants (ratios such as net worth, gearing, interest cover, current ratio, loan-to-value), and information covenants (annual and interim accounts, compliance certificates, notice of defaults). They do not stop the borrower taking any business risk at all, and the usual monitoring tool is information covenants, not bank auditors inspecting the accounts (SQ p8 Q4, key A).

*Source note: BF1 s18; RL-XB p4–8; Cyril-BF §1.3.3.2; SQ p8 Q4 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-0-16 — Events of default; cross-default; acceleration
*Keywords: events of default, non-payment grace period, breach of financial covenants, other obligations, misrepresentation, cross default, cross acceleration, insolvency proceedings, creditors' process, judicial management, change of ownership, unlawfulness, repudiation, moratorium on external indebtedness, cessation of business, acceleration, cancel commitments, enforce security, potential event of default*

**End point:** On an event of default the lender may cancel undrawn commitments, demand immediate repayment (accelerate) and enforce its security. Cross-default means a default under another financing is a default under this one (cross-acceleration: only when the other debt is actually accelerated). Remedy periods may apply.

> **Customary events of default (BF1 s19)**
> - Non-payment (with a grace period)
> - Breach of financial covenants
> - Breach of other obligations (including information undertakings)
> - Misrepresentation
> - Cross default / cross acceleration
> - Insolvency / insolvency proceedings / creditors' process / judicial management
> - Ownership of the obligors (change of ownership)
> - Unlawfulness
> - Repudiation
> - Moratorium on external indebtedness
> - Cessation of business

*Source note: BF1 s19; RL-XB p8; Cyril-BF §1.3.3.3.*

### ccp-1-0-17 — Boilerplate; assignment and transfer
*Keywords: boilerplate, assignment, transfer of loans, amendments and waivers, payment mechanics, set-off, notices, partial invalidity, counterparts, governing law, jurisdiction, arbitration, borrower consent to transfer*

**End point:** Boilerplate clauses: assignment/transfer of loans; amendments and waivers; payment mechanics; set-off; notices; partial invalidity; counterparts; governing law; jurisdiction; arbitration. Transfers by lenders are usually expressly allowed, often on condition that the borrower pays no more (e.g. withholding tax) to the new lender, or subject to borrower consent not to be withheld without good reason.

*Source note: BF1 s20; RL-XB p8–9; Cyril-BF §1.3.4.*

### ccp-1-0-18 — Why take security; what happens on the borrower's insolvency
*Keywords: reasons for taking security, protection on insolvency, enforce security, sell secured asset, proceeds of sale, shortfall, unsecured creditor for balance, ABCBanklegalofficer, priority over unsecured creditors, bank will always get back all the money, only lose out on interest, X.com post, secured bank insolvent borrower*

**End point:** Security protects the lender if the borrower becomes insolvent. The lender enforces the security, typically by selling the secured asset, and the proceeds go to the lender. If they are not enough, the lender claims the balance as an unsecured creditor, competing with the other unsecured creditors, and may not recover it in full. So a secured bank does not "always get back all the money it lent" (SQ p11 Q8, key D). An unsecured lender ranks equally with the other unsecured creditors.

*Source note: BF1 s22; Cyril-BF §1.4; SQ p11 Q8 and p114 (key D).*

### ccp-1-0-19 — Mortgage: legal v equitable
*Keywords: mortgage, legal mortgage, equitable mortgage, transfer of title, equity of redemption, debtor keeps possession, legal title, equitable interest*

**End point:** A mortgage transfers ownership (title) of the asset to the creditor as security, while the debtor usually keeps possession. Under a legal mortgage the mortgagor transfers legal title and keeps the equity of redemption; under an equitable mortgage the debtor keeps legal title and transfers the equitable interest.

*Source note: BF1 s23; Cyril-BF §1.4.1.*

### ccp-1-0-20 — Charge: fixed v floating; crystallisation
*Keywords: charge, fixed charge, floating charge, specific identifiable asset, no disposal without consent, changing pool of assets, inventory, stock, raw materials, finished goods, receivables, deal in ordinary course, crystallisation, crystallise on default, debenture, Charlie and Kate Pte Ltd*

**End point:** A charge gives the creditor a right to have the asset applied to satisfy the debt without transferring ownership. A fixed charge attaches to specific, identifiable assets, and the chargor cannot dispose of them without the chargee's consent (the test is objective: the creditor must have some control). A floating charge covers a changing class of assets (e.g. stock, raw materials, receivables) and lets the company deal with them in the ordinary course until an event such as default or insolvency, when it crystallises into a fixed charge and the company's ability to deal with the assets is restricted. So security over leather stock and finished bags in a factory should be a floating charge (SQ p12 Q9, key A); the company does not have to hand the assets to the bank, and a charge over the company's own shares is not the right security. A single "debenture" can combine fixed and floating charges over all present and future assets.

*Source note: BF1 s23; RL-Sec p1–3; Cyril-BF §1.4.1; SQ p12 Q9 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-0-21 — Weaknesses of a floating charge
*Keywords: floating charge disadvantages, lower priority, preferential debts, winding up expenses, employee wages, CPF, retrenchment benefits, taxes, subsequent fixed charge ranks ahead, negative pledge notice, s 229 IRDA, invalid floating charge, one year, two years connected person, new value*

**End point:** A floating charge (1) ranks behind preferential debts paid out of floating-charge assets, such as winding-up expenses, certain employee wages and benefits, CPF contributions, retrenchment benefits and taxes; (2) is postponed to a later fixed charge, unless the fixed chargee had notice that the floating charge prohibited later charges ranking ahead or equally; and (3) can be invalid if created shortly before winding up or judicial management (s 229 IRDA): within 1 year if the company was then unable to pay its debts (2 years for a connected person, without proof of insolvency), except to the extent of new value given at or after creation (money, goods or services, or reduction of debt) plus interest.

*Source note: IRDA s 229(1)–(3) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); RL-Sec p1–3, p8; Cyril-BF "Extra stuff" (disadvantages of floating charge); INS-CS1 p33–34 (s 229). Avoidance generally is in Subject 2 (Insolvency).*

### ccp-1-0-22 — Registration of charges at ACRA (s 131 CA)
*Keywords: registration of charges, ACRA, s 131 Companies Act, perfection, 30 days, charge executed abroad, void against liquidator, void against creditor, debt immediately payable, registrable charges, debenture, book debts, shares in subsidiary, floating charge, goodwill, IP, Cape Town Convention exception*

**End point:** A company must register particulars of a charge over nearly any of its property with ACRA within 30 days of its creation (s 131 CA). Registrable charges include charges securing debentures, on uncalled share capital, on shares in a subsidiary, on goods (bill of sale type), on land, on book debts, floating charges, on ships or aircraft, and on goodwill and IP. There is no longer any extra period for a charge created outside Singapore. If not registered in time, the charge is void (so far as it secures the money) against the liquidator and any creditor of the company (s 131(1)), and the money secured immediately becomes payable (s 131(2)).

*Source note: BF1 s24; RL-Sec p3–4; Cyril-BF §1.4.2; CA s 131(1)–(2) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026). Conflict: Cyril-BF §1.4.2 and RL-Sec p3 (law at 2022) allow 7 more days for a charge executed abroad; current s 131(1) has only the 30-day period, which is followed.*

### ccp-1-0-23 — Specialist asset registers
*Keywords: specialist registers, Singapore Land Authority, SLA, registered mortgage, caveat, Intellectual Property Office of Singapore, IPOS, Maritime and Port Authority, MPA, ship mortgage, aircraft, Cape Town Convention, International Registry, perfection, priority*

**End point:** Besides ACRA, security over certain assets is perfected by registration on specialist registers: land at the Singapore Land Authority (registered mortgage or caveat); registered IP at IPOS; ships with the Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore; aircraft, engines and airframes on the Cape Town Convention International Registry (charges registrable there need not be filed at ACRA). Unregistered security may not be enforceable against other creditors if the debtor becomes insolvent.

*Source note: BF1 s24; RL-Sec p4–5, p15–19; Cyril-BF §1.4.2.*

### ccp-1-0-24 — Security over particular assets
*Keywords: shares, share mortgage, share charge, blank transfer, share certificates, CDP scripless shares, receivables, book debts, assignment, notice to debtor, bank account, charge-back, flawed asset, set-off, equipment, movables, non-possessory charge, inventory, insurances*

**End point:** Shares in a private company: mortgage or charge, perfected by holding the certificates with a signed blank transfer (registration as holder is unusual); a charge over a subsidiary's shares is registered at ACRA; scripless (CDP) shares are charged through the CDP system. Receivables: usually a floating charge (a fixed charge needs control of proceeds) or assignment; notice to the debtor is not needed for validity but protects priority and limits set-off. Bank accounts: a "charge-back" if the lender is the account bank; otherwise assignment or charge with notice to the bank and a waiver of set-off. Equipment: a non-possessory fixed charge registered at ACRA. Inventory: a floating charge. Insurances: assigned with notice to insurers.

*Source note: RL-Sec p4, p16–20.*

### ccp-1-0-25 — Enforcing security; effect of insolvency proceedings
*Keywords: enforcement of security, private sale, power of sale, foreclosure court order, receiver, receiver and manager, possession, CLPA grace periods excluded, winding up, secured creditor stands outside, prove for shortfall, judicial management moratorium, leave of court, scheme moratorium 30 days, ipso facto s 440 IRDA, interest after winding up*

**End point:** Security may be enforced by private sale if the security document so provides; foreclosure generally needs a court order and is unusual; the creditor may take possession or appoint a receiver without a court order if the document (by deed) gives the power; statutory grace periods for the power of sale (CLPA) are usually excluded by contract. On winding up, a secured creditor can stand outside the liquidation, enforce its security and prove for any shortfall. Once a judicial management application is made, security cannot be enforced without leave of court (or the JM's consent), and a scheme application under the IRDA brings an automatic 30-day moratorium. Ipso facto clauses allowing termination only because restructuring proceedings have begun (or the company is insolvent) are restricted (s 440 IRDA; see [2-63](#ccp-1-2-63)).

*Source note: RL-Sec p6–7; Cyril-BF §1.4.2 ("Enforcement via", from the readings). Detail of the moratoria is in Subject 2 (Insolvency).*

### ccp-1-0-26 — Guarantees and security for others; financial assistance
*Keywords: guarantee, upstream guarantee, cross-stream, corporate benefit, shareholders' resolution, s 162 CA loans to directors, s 163 CA director-related companies 20%, financial assistance, s 76 CA, public company, acquisition of own shares, holding company shares, whitewash, s 76(9A), s 76(9B), s 76(10), void, voidable*

**End point:** A company giving a guarantee or security for another's debt must have power under its constitution and must benefit (corporate benefit is judged company by company, so upstream and cross-stream guarantees are the concern); lenders commonly ask for a shareholders' resolution of the guarantor. Loans, guarantees or security for a director (s 162 CA) or for a company in which directors hold 20% or more of the votes (s 163 CA) are restricted. A public company, or a company whose holding company is public, must not give financial assistance (loan, guarantee, security) for the acquisition of its own or its holding company's shares (s 76(1) CA), unless a whitewash procedure is followed (e.g. s 76(9A) directors' resolution for up to 10% of capital and reserves; s 76(9B) unanimous members' resolution with solvency statements; s 76(10) special resolution).

*Source note: RL-Sec p9–14.*

### ccp-1-0-27 — What a bond is; why issue bonds rather than borrow from banks
*Keywords: bond, debt instrument, evidencing a debt, issuer, bondholder, principal, maturity, coupon, interest payment dates, reasons for bond issue, broader investor base, fewer covenants, flexible interest, unsecured, limited disclosure, notes*

**End point:** A bond is an instrument constituting or evidencing a debt: the issuer promises to repay the principal borrowed from the bondholder at a specified future time, with a return in the form of interest on specified interest payment dates. Advantages over bank loans: (1) access to a broader investor base; (2) fewer covenants than a syndicated loan; (3) more flexible interest rate options; (4) frequently unsecured; (5) disclosure on the issuer usually limited to publicly available information, e.g. financial statements rather than detailed management accounts.

*Source note: BF2 s4–5; Cyril-BF §2.1.1.*

### ccp-1-0-28 — Eurobond v domestic bond; international debt capital markets
*Keywords: Eurobond, international bond, domestic bond, currency other than country where sold, domestic investors, domestic currency, international debt capital markets, larger pool of investors, larger supply of funds, reduced cost of funds, regulatory and compliance issues*

**End point:** A Eurobond is denominated in a currency other than that of the country or countries where it is sold ("Euro" means international, not the euro); a domestic bond is issued to domestic investors in their domestic currency. International debt capital markets offer a larger pool of investors, a larger supply of funds and a reduced cost of funds, at the price of more regulatory and compliance issues.

*Source note: BF2 s6–7; RL-BondOv p1; Cyril-BF §2.2.2–2.2.3.*

### ccp-1-0-29 — Standalone issue v MTN programme
*Keywords: standalone issuance, single issue, medium-term note programme, MTN, EMTN, debt issuance programme, framework of agreements, quick cheap frequent issues, base prospectus, pricing supplement, final terms, pricing statement, dealers, programme limit, drawdown*

**End point:** A standalone issuance is a single issue of bonds with specified terms, maturity and interest rate; it suits unusual or complex terms. A medium-term note programme is a framework of master agreements for the quick, cheap and frequent issue of a wide range of debt securities up to a programme limit: costlier to set up, but each drawdown needs only a pricing supplement (or final terms) against the base prospectus, so the issuer can react quickly to the market with consistent terms. In Singapore, an offer under a debenture issuance programme uses a base prospectus plus a pricing statement for each offer.

*Source note: BF2 s8; RL-EMTN p1–2; RL-DCM p18; Cyril-BF §2.2.3.*

### ccp-1-0-30 — Wholesale v retail bond markets; listing bonds on SGX
*Keywords: wholesale bond market, retail bond market, specified investors, institutional, accredited, larger denominations, over-the-counter, exchange traded, SGX-ST, board lot S$200,000, trustee required, listing debt securities, Rule 309, S$5m per series*

**End point:** Wholesale bonds are offered only to specified investors (institutional and accredited) in larger denominations and usually traded over-the-counter after issue; retail bonds are offered to all investors, including retail, and traded on the exchange. SGX-ST lists both. A trustee must be appointed for SGX-listed debt unless, for its whole tenor, it is offered only to specified investors and traded in a minimum board lot of S$200,000.

*Source note: BF2 s9; RL-DCM p9–13; Cyril-BF §2.2.4.*

### ccp-1-0-31 — Bearer v registered; global v definitive; clearing systems
*Keywords: bearer bond, registered bond, negotiable instrument, title by delivery, register, registrar, global note, definitive bonds, physical certificates, common depositary, clearing system, CDP, Euroclear, Clearstream, delivery against payment*

**End point:** Bearer bonds are negotiable instruments, title passing by delivery; registered bonds pass by entry on a register kept by a registrar. A global bond is a single document representing the whole issue, held for a clearing system (in Singapore, CDP; internationally Euroclear and Clearstream), so interests are transferred by book entry and delivery against payment is simultaneous; definitive bonds are individual physical certificates, issued only in limited cases.

*Source note: Cyril-BF §2.2.3.1 (global v definitive, clearing systems); RL-BondOv p2–5; RL-DCM p17, p24–25 (CDP).*

### ccp-1-0-32 — Key participants
*Keywords: issuer, bondholders, lead manager, managers, underwrite, subscription, paying agent, fiscal agent, trustee, registrar, transfer agent, calculation agent, listing agent, rating agency, auditors comfort letter, lawyers, printers, process agent*

**End point:** The issuer appoints managers (investment banks, led by a lead manager) to structure and market the bonds, who may underwrite them; a paying agent to pay interest and principal; and, if required, a trustee, who is duty-bound to act in the bondholders' interests as their representative. Bondholders pay the initial bond proceeds and receive interest and principal. Others: lawyers (documents, due diligence, legal opinions), auditors (comfort letters), registrar and transfer agent (registered bonds), calculation agent (floating rates), listing agent, rating agency, printers, process agent.

*Source note: BF2 s11–12; Cyril-BF §2.2.1.*

### ccp-1-0-33 — Trustee v fiscal agent structure
*Keywords: trustee structure, fiscal agent structure, representative of bondholders, agent of the issuer, enforcement action, event of default, individual bondholders, trust deed, fiscal agency agreement, deed of covenant, Singapore practice trust structure prevails*

**End point:** Under a trustee structure, a trustee represents the bondholders, is their main contact with the issuer, and can take enforcement action for all of them (sometimes on the instructions of a set percentage); it suits issues with many bondholders. Under a fiscal agent structure, the fiscal agent is the issuer's agent with limited, administrative discretion; there is no bondholder representative, so each bondholder monitors and enforces individually (supported by a deed of covenant). There is never both a fiscal agent and a trustee. In Singapore the trust structure prevails; the fiscal agent owes bondholders no duty of care.

*Source note: BF2 s11–12; Cyril-BF §2.2.2; RL-BondDocs p1, p3; RL-DCM p9, p16.*

### ccp-1-0-34 — Stages of a bond issue
*Keywords: stages of bond issue, pre-launch, can the issuer issue, borrowing restrictions, negative pledge, structuring, due diligence, launch, roadshow, invitation, term sheet, signing, subscription agreement, closing, delivery against payment, post-issue, interest payments, maturity*

**End point:** (1) Pre-launch: check the issuer can issue (powers, borrowing limits, negative pledges) and any guarantor can guarantee; decide bearer/registered, global/definitive, listing and rating; appoint parties; prepare documents; due diligence. (2) Launch: the lead manager announces the issue, invites managers, the terms are negotiated, and investors are sought (e.g. roadshows). (3) Issue: at signing (usually 2 days to a week before closing) the subscription agreement is signed and the prospectus published; at closing the remaining documents are signed and bonds are delivered against payment through the clearing systems. (4) Post-issue: interest payments and repayment at maturity.

*Source note: Cyril-BF §2.2.3; RL-BondSteps p1–8.*

### ccp-1-0-35 — Key documents
*Keywords: bond documents, prospectus, offering circular, subscription agreement, agency agreement, deed of covenant, trust deed, global bond, definitive bonds, terms and conditions, legal opinions, comfort letter, fiscal agency agreement, paying agency agreement*

**End point:** The course lists seven key documents: (1) prospectus or offering circular; (2) subscription agreement (issuer agrees to issue, managers agree to subscribe); (3) agency agreement (appointing the paying agents); (4) deed of covenant (direct enforcement rights for holders, used without a trustee); (5) trust deed (constitutes the bonds and appoints the trustee); (6) forms of global and definitive bonds; (7) terms and conditions of the bonds. Also legal opinions and auditors' comfort letters.

*Source note: BF2 s14; Cyril-BF §2.3; RL-BondDocs p1–7. Conflict: Cyril-BF §2.3 first describes the subscription agreement as between issuer and investor; the readings make it between issuer and managers, which is followed.*

### ccp-1-0-36 — Prescribed capital markets products (s 309B SFA)
*Keywords: s 309B SFA, prescribed capital markets product, non-prescribed, plain vanilla bonds, classification, notify exchange, relevant person, CMS licence holder, customer account review, customer knowledge assessment*

**End point:** Before offering a capital markets product (e.g. a bond), the issuer must determine whether it is a prescribed capital markets product (which includes plain vanilla bonds) or a non-prescribed one, and notify the classification to the exchange (if it will be listed) and to the relevant CMS licence holder distributing it (s 309B SFA). This decides whether the distributor must do further checks, such as a customer account review or customer knowledge assessment.

*Source note: Cyril-BF "Extra stuff" (prescribed CMP); RL-DCM p3; SFA s 309B(1) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-0-37 — Prospectus requirement; contents; PHS; lodgement and registration
*Keywords: prospectus, offence to offer without prospectus, SFA, all information investor would reasonably require, informed assessment, legal and disclosure document not marketing, product highlights sheet, signed by directors, lodged with MAS, OPERA, 7 days exposure, registration, 6 months, base prospectus, pricing statement*

**End point:** It is an offence to offer securities (including bonds) without a prospectus unless an exemption applies (SFA). The prospectus is the main offering document: principally a legal and disclosure document, not a marketing one, it must contain all information an investor would reasonably require to make an informed assessment (terms of the securities, the issuer's business, financial information, risk factors, management and ownership, use of proceeds). It must be accompanied by a product highlights sheet, signed by all directors (or their agents), and registered with MAS before circulation: it is lodged on MAS's OPERA database for at least 7 days before registration (MAS may register within the prescribed period, extendable to 28 days from lodgement, s 240(8)) and stays available there for 6 months after registration. No securities may be issued on a prospectus more than 6 months after registration (24 months for a base prospectus under a debenture issuance programme) (s 250(3)). A debenture programme uses a base prospectus plus a pricing statement per offer.

*Source note: BF2 s15; BF3 s14; Cyril-BF §2.4.1; RL-DCM p18; SFA ss 240(8), 250(3) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026). The 7-day minimum is from the course readings; the Act leaves the period to MAS's prescription.*

### ccp-1-0-38 — Prospectus liability; supplementary or replacement prospectus
*Keywords: prospectus liability, false or misleading statement, omission, ss 253 254 SFA, criminal liability, civil liability, issuer, directors, underwriter, issue manager, experts, due diligence defence, reasonable inquiries, supplementary prospectus, replacement prospectus, defective, information memorandum no liability, s 199 s 200*

**End point:** There is criminal and civil liability for false or misleading statements in, and omissions of material information from, a prospectus (ss 253–254 SFA). Those liable include the issuer and its directors, proposed directors named with consent, and the issue manager and underwriter named with consent; a defendant who made all reasonable inquiries and reasonably believed the statement was not false or misleading has a defence. If the issuer finds the prospectus defective after registration but before the offer closes, it must lodge a supplementary or replacement prospectus. An information memorandum attracts no ss 253–254 liability, but the general prohibitions on false statements and fraudulent inducement (ss 199–200 SFA) still apply.

*Source note: BF3 s14; Cyril-BF §3.3.1; RL-ECM p18–19; RL-DCM p21.*

### ccp-1-0-39 — Institutional and accredited investor exemptions; information memorandum
*Keywords: exemption, institutional investors, s 274 SFA, accredited investors, s 275 SFA, relevant persons, no advertisement, information memorandum, wholesale, accredited investor definition, net personal assets S$2 million, financial assets S$1 million, income S$300,000, corporation net assets S$10 million, opt-in, resale restriction 6 months*

**End point:** No prospectus is needed for offers to institutional investors (s 274 SFA) or to accredited investors and other "relevant persons" (s 275(1) SFA; s 275(1A) also covers offers of at least S$200,000 per transaction), provided the offer is not accompanied by an advertisement making or calling attention to it. Such wholesale offers use an information memorandum, which carries no prospectus liability. An accredited investor is, e.g., an individual with net personal assets over S$2m (primary residence counted only up to S$1m), net financial assets over S$1m or income of at least S$300,000 in the last 12 months, or a corporation with net assets over S$10m, who has opted in to AI status. Securities so acquired generally may be resold within 6 months only to institutional investors or s 275 persons.

*Source note: BF2 s16; Cyril-BF §2.4.2; RL-DCM p2–3 (primary residence cap); RL-ECM p32; SFA ss 4A(1)(a), 274, 275 (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-0-40 — Small offers (S$5m) and private placements (50 persons)
*Keywords: small offer exemption, S$5 million, 12 months, private placement exemption, 50 persons, 12 months, no advertisement, s 272A SFA, s 272B SFA, friends and family, start-up*

**End point:** No prospectus is needed for (a) small offers: personal offers where the total raised in any 12-month period does not exceed S$5 million (s 272A SFA), with no advertisement and no promotional expenses; or (b) private placements: offers made to no more than 50 persons in any 12-month period (s 272B SFA).

*Source note: BF3 s8; Cyril-BF §2.4.2, §3.2; RL-DCM p19 (thresholds, no section numbers); RL-ECM p17; SFA ss 272A(1), 272B(1) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-0-41 — Offer information statement (s 277)
*Keywords: offer information statement, OIS, s 277 SFA, issuer already listed, rights issue, lighter disclosure, no MAS registration, no exposure period, lodged with MAS, liability still applies*

**End point:** An issuer whose shares are already listed on SGX may make an offer (e.g. a retail bond issue or a renounceable rights issue) with an offer information statement instead of a full prospectus (s 277 SFA). The OIS has lighter disclosure because information about the issuer is already public; it is lodged with MAS but not registered and has no 7-day exposure period, though prospectus-type civil and criminal liability still applies.

*Source note: BF2 s16; Cyril-BF §2.4.1–2.4.2; RL-DCM p7, p19, p21; RL-ECM p12.*

### ccp-1-0-42 — Bond Seasoning and Exempt Bond Issuer Frameworks
*Keywords: Bond Seasoning Framework, Exempt Bond Issuer Framework, retail investors, seasoning period six months, plain vanilla, size test S$1 billion market cap, net assets S$500 million, listing 5 years, credit test, rating BBB, AA-, re-tap, simplified disclosure document, product highlights sheet, trustee, specified investors 20%*

**End point:** Both frameworks let established issuers reach retail investors without a full prospectus, for plain vanilla bonds listed on SGX with a trustee. Under the Bond Seasoning Framework, bonds first offered to specified investors (at least S$150m) can be traded by retail investors on SGX after a 6-month seasoning period. Under the Exempt Bond Issuer Framework, the issuer offers directly to retail investors with a simplified disclosure document and product highlights sheet. Both require size (e.g. market capitalisation of at least S$1bn, or net assets of at least S$500m), listing history (at least 5 years) and credit-quality tests, the credit test being stricter for exempt bond issuers (e.g. rating of at least AA- rather than BBB).

*Source note: BF2 s16; Cyril-BF §2.4.2; RL-DCM p7–9 (p8 mislabels the Exempt Bond Issuer paragraph as Bond Seasoning).*

### ccp-1-0-45 — Prospectus exemptions for raising private equity
*Keywords: prospectus exemptions private company, small offer, offer to no more than 50 persons, accredited investors, institutional investors, DeepFind, CheapGPT, Raman, friends and family, S$10 million*

**End point:** A private company raising equity usually relies on one of three exemptions: (1) a small offer (up to S$5m in 12 months); (2) an offer to no more than 50 persons (in 12 months); (3) an offer to accredited and/or institutional investors only (see [0-39](#ccp-1-0-39), [0-40](#ccp-1-0-40)). *Source note: BF3 s8; Cyril-BF §3.2; SQ p10 Q7 and p114 (key C).*

### ccp-1-0-46 — Shareholder limit; approval of existing shareholders
*Keywords: private company 50 shareholders limit, convert to public company, approval of existing shareholders, issue of new shares, s 161 Companies Act, general meeting, dilution, pre-emption*

**End point:** Two additional considerations: (1) a private company may have no more than 50 members, so a wider shareholding means converting to a public company; (2) directors may not issue new shares without the prior approval of the company in general meeting (s 161(1) CA; an issue in breach is void, s 161(6)), and the constitution or a shareholders' agreement may give pre-emption rights, since new shares dilute existing holders.

*Source note: BF3 s9; Cyril-BF §3.2; RL-VC p18; CA ss 18(1)(b), 161(1), (6) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-0-47 — Steps and documents: due diligence, SSA, SHA
*Keywords: share subscription agreement, SSA, warranties, due diligence, legal financial tax commercial, shareholders' agreement, SHA, protective provisions, corporate governance rights, board appointment, reserved matters, anti-dilution, pre-emption, transfer restrictions, right of first refusal, exit mechanisms, co-sale, tag-along, Big Fish VC LLP, MSH Company Pte Ltd, steps to implement the deal, venture capital fund invests, offering to one investor only, no offering to the public, no prospectus needed, convert to public company, due diligence on the investor, sole shareholder*

**End point:** Usual steps: the investor carries out due diligence on the company (legal, financial, tax, commercial); the company and investor negotiate and sign the share subscription agreement (terms and conditions of the subscription, including warranties about the company's state); and the shareholders (founder and investor) negotiate and sign a shareholders' agreement (protective provisions for the investor, governance rights such as board seats and reserved matters, anti-dilution mechanisms, transfer restrictions such as rights of first refusal, and exit mechanisms such as co-sale/tag-along rights). No prospectus is needed for an offer to one VC investor, the company need not become public, and the founder does not usually do due diligence on the VC (SQ p9 Q5, key B: II, III and VI).

### ccp-1-0-48 — Venture capital terms and fund structures
*Keywords: venture capital, VC v private equity, minority stake, preference shares, liquidation preference, participating, non-participating, anti-dilution, pre-emption rights, right of first refusal, ROFR, ROFO, tag-along, co-sale, drag-along, lock-up, board seat, observer, reserved matters, convertible loan, CARE, VIMA, term sheet, ESOP vesting, qualifying IPO, redemption, limited partnership, VCC, VCFM regime, accredited investors*

**End point:** VC takes minority stakes in early-stage, high-growth companies over successive rounds, usually by preference shares (priority on liquidation, preferential dividends, conversion, sometimes redemption) or convertible instruments. Investor protections: liquidation preference; anti-dilution; pre-emption on new issues; transfer restrictions (ROFR/ROFO, lock-ups of founders); co-sale/tag-along and drag-along; board seats or observers; reserved matters needing investor consent; information rights. Exits: trade sale (co-sale), qualifying IPO, redemption, liquidation preference on a "liquidation event". Standard Singapore templates are the SVCA/SAL VIMA documents (term sheet, SSA, SHA, and CARE, modelled on the US SAFE). VC funds are usually limited partnerships or variable capital companies; managers are licensed by MAS, with a lighter regime for VC fund managers offering only to accredited/institutional investors.

*Source note: RL-VC p2–22 (law at 1 Aug 2020); BF3 s10.*

### ccp-1-0-50 — Mainboard admission criteria
*Keywords: Mainboard listing criteria, profitability test A S$10 million pre-tax profit, S$30 million (old figure), three-year track record, profitability test B, market capitalisation S$150 million, market capitalisation test S$300 million operating revenue, same business same management, positive operating cash flow, shareholding spread, 500 shareholders, public float 12% to 25%, working capital 12 months, independent directors*

**End point:** A Mainboard issuer needs at least a 1-year track record and must meet one of three tests: (A) pre-tax profit of at least S$10m in the latest financial year and a 3-year operating track record; (B) profitable in the latest financial year, a 3-year operating track record, and market capitalisation at listing of at least S$150m; or (C) operating revenue in the latest financial year and market capitalisation of at least S$300m. Under A and B, the business and management must be substantially the same throughout the track record and the group must be financially healthy (positive operating cash flow). Other requirements include a shareholding spread (at least 500 shareholders and a public float of 12–25% depending on size), sufficient working capital for 12 months, and at least two independent directors.

*Source note: Cyril-BF "Misc. stuff from the readings"; RL-ECM p4–7 (law at 1 Feb 2022); SGX Rulebook Mainboard Rule 210(2) (rulebook.sgx.com, current at 11 Nov 2026). Conflict: Cyril-BF and RL-ECM give test A as S$30m consolidated pre-tax profit; the current Rule 210(2)(a) requires S$10m, which is followed. Tests B and C are unchanged.*

### ccp-1-0-51 — Key steps and timetable of an IPO
*Keywords: IPO steps, pre-IPO restructuring, due diligence, prospectus drafting, listing application, Listing Admissions Pack, SGX-ST review 2 to 4 months, MAS pre-lodgement review, lodgement, OPERA, registration lodgement plus 7 to 21 days, underwriting agreement, pricing and allocation, public offer about 28 days, listing about 33 days, 6 to 12 months*

**End point:** Ten steps: (1) pre-IPO restructuring if needed; (2) due diligence; (3) drafting the prospectus; (4) submitting the listing application (Listing Admissions Pack) to SGX-ST; (5) submitting the draft prospectus to MAS; (6) SGX-ST and MAS review and queries; (7) negotiating the legal documents (e.g. underwriting agreement); (8) registration of the prospectus with MAS; (9) pricing and allocation of shares; (10) admission to the Official List and listing and quotation. Timetable: the Admissions Pack goes in 2–4 months before lodgement; registration is 7–21 days after lodgement; the public offer opens about 28 days after lodgement for 3–5 days; listing is about 33 days after lodgement. The whole process takes about 6–12 months.

*Source note: BF3 s13; Cyril-BF §3.3.2 and "Timeline for SGX Mainboard Listing"; RL-ECM p9, p23.*

### ccp-1-0-52 — The IPO prospectus: contents, preparation, responsibility
*Keywords: IPO prospectus, all information reasonably required, informed assessment, rights and liabilities attaching to shares, assets and liabilities, profits and losses, financial position, prospects, prepared by issuer and advisers, experts reports, valuers, criminal and civil liability, omissions, misleading statements*

**End point:** The prospectus must contain all information that investors and their professional advisers would reasonably require to make an informed assessment of the shares, including the rights and liabilities attaching to them and the issuer's assets and liabilities, profits and losses, financial position and performance, and prospects. It is prepared by the issuer and its advisers (with expert reports, e.g. valuers or industry consultants, where relevant), after due diligence to verify key statements. The issuer and its directors face criminal and civil liability for omissions and misleading statements (see [0-38](#ccp-1-0-38)).

*Source note: BF3 s14; Cyril-BF §3.3.1.*

### ccp-1-0-53 — IPO marketing
*Keywords: IPO marketing, pre-IPO placements, cornerstone investors, IPO price, roadshows, institutional and accredited investors, after lodgement, advertising, retail offer, no advertising before lodgement, research reports exception*

**End point:** Four methods: (1) pre-IPO placements to early investors, building the issuer's credentials; (2) cornerstone investors, placed concurrently with but separately from the IPO at the IPO price, giving certainty; (3) roadshows for institutional and accredited investors after the prospectus is lodged; (4) advertising and other publicity, relevant to retail offers only — advertising before the prospectus is lodged with MAS is prohibited, subject to exceptions (e.g. certain research reports).

*Source note: BF3 s15; Cyril-BF §3.3.3; RL-ECM p20.*

### ccp-1-0-54 — Continuing obligations after listing; follow-on share issues
*Keywords: continuing obligations, disclosure of material information, false market, periodic financial reporting, 45 days, 60 days, interested person transactions 3% 5%, acquisitions and realisations 5% 20% 100%, corporate governance comply or explain, general mandate 50% 20% pro rata, rights issue, placement 10% discount*

**End point:** Once listed the issuer must: (1) disclose material information (to avoid a false market or that would materially affect price) and clarify rumours; (2) report financial results periodically (half-year within 45 days, full year within 60 days; quarterly where risk-based conditions apply); (3) comply with the IPT rules (announce at 3% of NTA, shareholders' approval at 5%); (4) announce acquisitions and disposals exceeding 5% of the relative figures, with shareholder approval above 20% (and more above 100%); (5) comply or explain against the Code of Corporate Governance. For follow-on equity, a Mainboard general mandate allows issuing up to 50% of issued shares, of which no more than 20% other than pro rata to existing shareholders. Details are in Subject 1.

*Source note: BF3 s16; Cyril-BF §3.3.4; RL-ECM p13, p27–28; SGX Rulebook Mainboard Rule 806(2) (current at 11 Nov 2026). Corporate Governance blocks [1-8](#ccp-1-1-8), [1-17](#ccp-1-1-17), [1-26](#ccp-1-1-26), [1-34](#ccp-1-1-34).*

## 1.I Introduction to corporate governance

- **What is corporate governance? Which principles?** Code 2018 definition; transparency, accountability, integrity, fairness, sustainability, diversity, capability, leadership → [1-1](#ccp-1-1-1)
- **Who is responsible for corporate governance? Who regulates? What is the CGAC?** ACRA, MAS, SGX RegCo; the Board is central (s 157A CA); dual role → [1-2](#ccp-1-1-2)
- **Why does corporate governance matter? Agency problem; benefits** → [1-3](#ccp-1-1-3)

### ccp-1-1-1 — What corporate governance is; principles of good governance
*Keywords: corporate governance definition, Code of Corporate Governance 2018, long-term shareholder value, stakeholders, transparency, accountability, integrity, fairness, sustainability, diversity, capability, leadership*

**End point:** The Code 2018 defines corporate governance as "having the appropriate people, processes and structures to direct and manage the business and affairs of the company to enhance long-term shareholder value, whilst taking into account the interests of other stakeholders". The course lists eight general principles of good governance, which apply whatever the company's size.

> **Principles of good corporate governance (CG1 s4)**
> - Transparency
> - Accountability
> - Integrity
> - Fairness
> - Sustainability
> - Diversity
> - Capability
> - Leadership

*Source note: CG1 s3 (Introduction to the Code 2018), s4; Cyril-CG §1.1–1.2.*

### ccp-1-1-2 — Who is responsible: regulators, CGAC, the board and its dual role
*Keywords: corporate governance ecosystem, ACRA, MAS, SGX RegCo, Corporate Governance Advisory Committee, CGAC, board of directors, s 157A Companies Act, managed by or under the direction of the directors, dual role, conformance, performance, Cadbury Report*

**End point:** The ecosystem has regulators (ACRA, MAS, SGX), companies (boards, management, company secretaries, auditors), shareholders (controlling, institutional, retail) and other stakeholders. ACRA, MAS and SGX RegCo regulate; the Corporate Governance Advisory Committee (CGAC), an industry-led body, monitors and advocates good practice and advises the regulators (e.g. on the Practice Guidance), leaving enforcement to SGX. The Board is central: the business of a company is managed by, or under the direction or supervision of, the directors (s 157A CA). The Board has a dual role, company performance (strategy and value creation) and regulatory conformance; an effective board does both.

*Source note: CG1 s5–8; Cyril-CG §1.3–1.5.*

### ccp-1-1-3 — Why corporate governance: principal–agent problem; benefits
*Keywords: why corporate governance, principal-agent problem, agency theory, board agent of shareholders, management agent of the board, benefits, investor confidence, cheaper capital, valuations, liquidity*

**End point:** In agency theory the Board is the agent of the shareholders and management the agent of the Board; problems arise when the agent's interests conflict with the principal's. Corporate governance aligns the interests of the board, shareholders and other stakeholders transparently and fairly. Benefits: sound, transparent systems; sustainable long-term profits; business continuity; less inefficiency; greater lender, investor and stakeholder confidence; higher valuations, liquidity and share prices; cheaper access to capital; more entrepreneurial activity and foreign investment; lower risk premiums.

*Source note: CG1 s9–11.*

## 1.II Disclosure-based regime and liability for failing to disclose

- **Merit-based or disclosure-based regime? When did Singapore change?** 1998–2000 → [1-4](#ccp-1-1-4)
- **Listed company fails to announce information the Listing Rules require — offence?** s 203 SFA: offence only if intentional or reckless; negligent = civil only; fine up to S$250,000 / 7 years → [1-5](#ccp-1-1-5)
- **Are the directors / officers personally liable for the company's disclosure breach?** s 331 SFA: consent, connivance or neglect → [1-6](#ccp-1-1-6)
- **How material must information be? Madhavan Peter; China Aviation Oil directors fined** → [1-7](#ccp-1-1-7)

### ccp-1-1-4 — Shift from merit-based to disclosure-based regulation
*Keywords: disclosure-based regime, merit-based regime, regulator as gatekeeper, market-driven, 1998-2000, Companies Act, SFA, Listing Manual, first Code of Corporate Governance 2001*

**End point:** Singapore used to regulate its capital markets on a merit basis, with the regulator as gatekeeper. Between 1998 and 2000 it moved to a market-driven, disclosure-based regime, with changes to the Companies Act, the SFA and the SGX Listing Manual, and the first Code of Corporate Governance (issued by MAS in 2001).

*Source note: CG1 s12; CG-LP p4 (Code first issued by MAS in 2001).*

### ccp-1-1-5 — s 203 SFA continuous disclosure: offence v civil; s 204 penalty
*Keywords: s 203 SFA, continuous disclosure, failure to notify SGX, intentionally, recklessly, negligently, criminal offence, civil penalty, s 204 SFA, fine $250,000, 7 years imprisonment, bedrock of disclosure regime, force of law*

**End point:** s 203 SFA is the bedrock of the disclosure-based regime: it gives SGX's disclosure rules the force of law. A listed entity must not intentionally, recklessly or negligently fail to notify SGX of information the Listing Rules require it to disclose (s 203(1)–(2)). The failure is an offence only if intentional or reckless (s 203(3)); a negligent failure attracts only civil penalty and civil liability. On conviction: fine up to S$250,000, imprisonment up to 7 years, or both (s 204(1)).

> **s 203 SFA**
> - (1) A listed entity must not intentionally, recklessly or negligently fail to notify the approved exchange of information required to be disclosed under the listing rules on specified events or matters as they occur.
> - (2) Applies to: (a) an entity whose securities or securities-based derivatives are listed on an approved exchange; (b) a trustee-manager of a listed business trust; (c) a responsible person of a listed collective investment scheme.
> - (3) A contravention is not an offence unless the failure is intentional or reckless.
> - s 204(1): offence — fine not exceeding $250,000 or imprisonment not exceeding 7 years or both. s 204(2): no prosecution after a civil penalty order or agreement under s 232.

*Source note: CG1 s13; SFA12 p9–10 (ss 203–204).*

### ccp-1-1-6 — s 331 SFA: officers liable (consent, connivance, neglect)
*Keywords: s 331 SFA, personal liability of directors and officers, offence by body corporate, consent, connivance, attributable to neglect, officer guilty*

**End point:** Where a body corporate commits an offence with the consent or connivance of an officer, or the offence is attributable to any neglect on the officer's part, the officer as well as the body corporate is guilty and liable to be punished (s 331 SFA). This makes directors and officers personally accountable for disclosure breaches.

*Source note: CG1 s14; Cyril-CG §2.1.*

### ccp-1-1-7 — Materiality: Madhavan Peter v PP; China Aviation Oil
*Keywords: Madhavan Peter v Public Prosecutor [2012] SGHC 153, materially affect price, significant change in price, China Aviation Oil, CAO, directors fined $150,000, oil options losses, Rule 703*

**End point:** In *Madhavan Peter v PP* [2012] SGHC 153, Chan Sek Keong CJ held that the Rule 703 test must refer to information "likely to effect a significant change in the price or value" of the issuer's securities. In the China Aviation Oil case, three directors were fined S$150,000 each for failing to disclose the company's losses from speculation in oil options, in breach of Rule 703.

*Source note: CG2 s4.*

## 1.III Immediate disclosure of material information (Rule 703, Appendix 7.1)

- **Must the listed company announce this information?** Test: needed to avoid a false market, or likely to materially affect price or value → [1-8](#ccp-1-1-8)
  - Fire destroys a major plant; big contract won or lost; merger / JV / acquisition; significant litigation; management change → announce immediately (SQ p15 Q7, key B) → [1-9](#ccp-1-1-9)
- **Can the company keep it confidential? Incomplete negotiation, trade secret, internal management information** → all three conditions in Rule 703(3), and must disclose once any ceases → [1-10](#ccp-1-1-10)
- **How and where to announce? Press release / newspaper instead of SGXNET? Already public on social media? Waiver?** → SGXNET first; no waiver; public availability is no excuse → [1-11](#ccp-1-1-11)
- **Chairman / CEO under police investigation, in a coma after an accident, video viral — announce?** Yes, immediately; personal matters can be material; don't wait for a charge or recovery (SQ p13 Q2 key D; SQ p17 Q10 key D) → [1-12](#ccp-1-1-12)
- **Rumour in the press; unusual trading; trading halt** → [1-13](#ccp-1-1-13)
- **Briefing analysts / selected shareholders first? Profit guidance when targets will be missed** → [1-14](#ccp-1-1-14)

### ccp-1-1-8 — Rule 703(1): false market / likely to materially affect price
*Keywords: Rule 703, Listing Rule 703(1), disclosure of material information, immediate announcement, false market, materially affect price or value, subsidiaries, associated companies, Corporate Disclosure Policy, Appendix 7.1*

**End point:** The key continuing obligation is to disclose material information as and when it arises. An issuer must immediately announce any information known to it concerning it or any of its subsidiaries or associated companies which (a) is necessary to avoid the establishment of a false market in its securities, or (b) would be likely to materially affect the price or value of its securities (Rule 703(1)). It must observe the Corporate Disclosure Policy in Appendix 7.1 and ensure its directors and executive officers are familiar with it (Rule 703(4)).

> **Rule 703**
> - (1) Announce any information known to the issuer concerning it or any of its subsidiaries or associated companies which: (a) is necessary to avoid the establishment of a false market in the issuer's securities; or (b) would be likely to materially affect the price or value of its securities.
> - (2) Does not apply to information which it would be a breach of law to disclose.
> - (3) Confidentiality carve-out: see [1-10](#ccp-1-1-10).
> - (4) Observe Appendix 7.1; directors and executive officers to be familiar with the disclosure requirements.
> - (5) The Exchange will not waive any requirement under this Rule.

*Source note: CG2 s3; LM r 703; Cyril-CG §2.2.*

### ccp-1-1-9 — Examples of material information and events needing immediate disclosure
*Keywords: material information examples, Appendix 7.1 paragraph 4, paragraph 8, joint venture, merger, acquisition, significant contract, sale of significant assets, significant litigation, management change, fire destroys plant, change in ownership by insiders*

**End point:** Appendix 7.1 para 4 gives examples of material information: information on the issuer's property, assets, business, financial condition and prospects; dealings with employees, suppliers and customers; material contracts or development projects; significant changes in shares owned by insiders; any development materially affecting shareholders' rights or interests. Para 8 gives events likely to need immediate disclosure under Rule 703: a JV, merger or acquisition; acquiring or losing a significant contract; buying or selling significant assets; significant litigation or dispute; a significant management change. A fire that destroys a major plant and is expected to affect production and profits significantly should generally be announced immediately, not held for the next financial statements (SQ p15 Q7, key B).

*Source note: CG2 s5; LM-App7.1 paras 4, 8; SQ p15 Q7 and p114 (key B).*

### ccp-1-1-10 — Exceptions: breach of law; the three conditions in Rule 703(3)
*Keywords: Rule 703(2), Rule 703(3), exception to disclosure, withhold disclosure, confidential, reasonable person would not expect disclosure, incomplete proposal or negotiation, supposition, insufficiently definite, internal management purposes, trade secret, condition ceases*

**End point:** Rule 703(1) does not apply to information it would be a breach of law to disclose (Rule 703(2)). It also does not apply while all three conditions in Rule 703(3) are met: (1) a reasonable person would not expect the information to be disclosed; (2) it is confidential; and (3) one of the listed categories applies. If any condition ceases to be satisfied, the information must be disclosed immediately.

> **Rule 703(3) — all three conditions**
> - Condition 1: a reasonable person would not expect the information to be disclosed;
> - Condition 2: the information is confidential; and
> - Condition 3: one or more of the following applies:
>   - (a) it concerns an incomplete proposal or negotiation;
>   - (b) it comprises matters of supposition or is insufficiently definite to warrant disclosure;
>   - (c) it is generated for the internal management purposes of the entity;
>   - (d) it is a trade secret.

*Source note: CG2 s6; LM r 703(2)–(3); Cyril-CG §2.4.*

### ccp-1-1-11 — How to announce: SGXNET first; no waiver; "already public" is no excuse
*Keywords: SGXNET, Rule 702, announcement via SGXNET first, simultaneously with other media, press statement, newspaper, no waiver Rule 703(5), generally available not a reason, Appendix 7.1 paragraph 5, social media*

**End point:** All announcements go through SGXNET unless specified otherwise (Rule 702). Material information must be released through SGXNET first, or simultaneously with any other media; a statement to the press instead of an SGXNET announcement does not comply. SGX will not waive the Rule 703 requirements (Rule 703(5)). That the information is already generally available (e.g. widely circulated on social media) is not a reason for failing to disclose.

*Source note: CG2 s7 (Rules 702, 703; Appendix 7.1 para 5); LM r 702, r 703(5); SQ p13 Q2 (option II wrong) and p17 Q10 (option II wrong), keys p114.*

### ccp-1-1-12 — Personal matters of a director or CEO
*Keywords: personal affairs, Chairman, CEO, police investigation, false police report, Penal Code, traffic accident, coma, hospitalised, unable to perform duties, video on social media, wait until charged, wait until recovers, Fondue Limited, Unlucky Limited, Listing Rules, announce investigation, official statement to the local press, chairman investigated*

**End point:** Information about a Chairman/CEO is not excluded just because it concerns his personal affairs: if it is material (it could create a false market or materially affect the share price, e.g. an investigation into serious offences, or his being in a coma and unable to act as Chairman and CEO), it must be announced immediately via SGXNET. The issuer cannot wait to see whether he is charged or until he regains consciousness, cannot use a press statement instead of SGXNET, and cannot rely on the matter already being public on social media. In both Sample Questions every statement suggesting otherwise was false.

*Source note: SQ p13 Q2 (key D, "none of the statements is true") and p17 Q10 (key D), keys p114; rules in CG2 s3, s7 (Rules 702, 703; App 7.1 para 5); LM r 704(7) (cessation of service of a director or CEO is itself a specific announcement, see [1-15](#ccp-1-1-15)).*

### ccp-1-1-13 — Rumours, unusual trading and trading halts
*Keywords: rumour, press report, unusual trading activity, clarify, confirm, explain, trading halt, suspension, price-sensitive information, dissemination before resumption*

**End point:** Rumours or reports, especially those causing unusual trading activity, should be clarified, confirmed or explained. If the information is materially price-sensitive, a trading halt or suspension may be needed so the information can be properly disseminated before trading resumes.

*Source note: CG2 s8; Cyril-BF §3.3.4 (clarify or confirm unsubstantiated rumours).*

### ccp-1-1-14 — No selective disclosure; profit guidance
*Keywords: selective disclosure, analysts, media, selected shareholders, privileged dealing position, Appendix 7.1 paragraph 7, profit guidance, profit warning, unlikely to meet performance targets*

**End point:** An issuer must not disclose information selectively, e.g. to certain media, analysts or shareholders: Appendix 7.1 para 7 forbids divulging information to anyone outside the issuer so as to put him in a privileged dealing position. Profit guidance is required if the issuer is unlikely to meet performance targets it has publicly disclosed.

*Source note: CG2 s11; Cyril-CG §2.6.*

**End point:** In addition to Rule 703, an issuer must immediately announce any appointment or cessation of service of key persons: a director, CEO, CFO, COO, general manager or other executive officer of equivalent authority, company secretary, registrar or auditors (Rule 704(7)(a)). ## 1.V Periodic reporting

- **Quarterly or half-yearly results? Deadline?** Half-yearly unless auditors' adverse/qualified/disclaimer opinion or going-concern uncertainty; 45 days (quarters / half year), 60 days (full year) → [1-17](#ccp-1-1-17)
- **When must the AGM be held and the annual report issued?** AGM within 4 months of FY end; annual report at least 14 days before the AGM → [1-18](#ccp-1-1-18)

### ccp-1-1-17 — Financial statements: half-yearly v quarterly; deadlines; voluntary updates
*Keywords: Rule 705, periodic reporting, financial statements, quarterly reporting, half-yearly reporting, semi-annual, risk-based approach, adverse opinion, qualified opinion, disclaimer of opinion, material uncertainty going concern, 45 days, 60 days, full year results, voluntary business updates, 2020 change, negative assurance*

**End point:** Since 2020 SGX takes a risk-based approach: quarterly reporting is required only if the auditors issued an adverse opinion, qualified opinion or disclaimer on the latest financial statements, or stated a material uncertainty relating to going concern (Rule 705(2)); SGX publishes the list. All other issuers report half-yearly, and are encouraged to give voluntary business updates in between. Deadlines: quarterly/half-year statements within 45 days of the period end; full-year statements within 60 days of the financial year end (Rule 705(1)–(3)). For interim statements the directors confirm that nothing has come to their attention that may render them false or misleading in any material aspect (Rule 705(5)).

*Source note: CG2 s10, s12–13; LM r 705(1)–(3), (5); Cyril-CG §2.5.2–2.5.3.*

### ccp-1-1-18 — Annual report and AGM (Rule 707); chairman's statement
*Keywords: Rule 707, annual general meeting within four months, annual report 14 days before AGM, Rule 708 chairman's statement, collective view of board, dissenting director, Rule 710 corporate governance report*

**End point:** An issuer must hold its AGM within four months from the end of its financial year (Rule 707(1)) and issue its annual report to shareholders and SGX at least 14 days before the AGM (Rule 707(2)). The chairman's statement must give a balanced, readable summary of performance and prospects representing the board's collective view; any dissenting director's view must be disclosed (Rule 708).

*Source note: CG2 s10; LM r 707–708.*

### ccp-1-1-19 — Board diversity policy (Rule 710A)
*Keywords: board diversity policy, Rule 710A, gender, skills and experience, targets, plans and timelines, progress, combination of skills talents experience, mandatory from 2022, Provision 2.4*

### ccp-1-1-20 — Sustainability reporting (Rules 711A, 711B; PN 7.6)
*Keywords: sustainability report, Rule 711A, Rule 711B, primary components, material ESG factors, climate-related disclosures, IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards, Practice Note 7.6, policies practices performance, targets, sustainability reporting framework, board statement, comply or explain, internal review, external assurance, 5 months, issued with annual report, one-time sustainability training*

**End point:** Every issuer must prepare an annual sustainability report describing its practices against the primary components in Rule 711B(1). The Board bears ultimate responsibility for sustainability reporting and decides the material ESG factors. All directors must undergo one-time training on sustainability (if the NC thinks a director needs none because of expertise, it must disclose the basis). From 1 Jan 2025 a component could be excluded on a comply-or-explain basis, except climate-related disclosures (which must follow PN 7.6, adopting the IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards); from 1 Jan 2026 all components are mandatory. Timing: until 31 Dec 2025, within 4 months of FY end; from 1 Jan 2026, issued together with the annual report; if externally assured, within 5 months of FY end. The process must be subject to internal review; external assurance is optional.

> **Rule 711B(1) primary components**
> - (a) material environmental, social and governance factors;
> - (aa) climate-related disclosures;
> - (b) policies, practices and performance;
> - (c) targets;
> - (d) sustainability reporting framework; and
> - (e) Board statement and associated governance structure for sustainability practices.

*Source note: CG2 s10, s16–20 (the 2025–2026 changes); CG-LP p3 (sustainability reporting "for information only"). Conflict: LM r 711A–711B (Dec 2023 text) still says within 4 months and allows exclusions; the block follows the 2026 slides.*

## 1.VII Interested person transactions: scope (Chapter 9)

- **What is Chapter 9 for? Form or substance?** → [1-21](#ccp-1-1-21)
- **Which group companies are covered? Listed subsidiary? Associated company?** "Entity at risk" → [1-22](#ccp-1-1-22)
- **Is X an interested person?** Director, CEO, controlling shareholder (15% or control), and their associates → [1-23](#ccp-1-1-23)
  - CEO's adopted child; director's brother; company 5% held by director + 30% by her husband (SQ p15 Q5) → associates → [1-23](#ccp-1-1-23)
  - Director's cousin; third party with an arrangement with an interested person → SGX may deem (Rule 904(4A)) (SQ p15 Q5, key D) → [1-24](#ccp-1-1-24)

### ccp-1-1-21 — Objective of Chapter 9; substance over form
*Keywords: Chapter 9 objective, Rule 901, Rule 902, interested person transactions, IPT, conflicts of interest, guard against risk, economic and commercial substance, legal form and technicality*

**End point:** Chapter 9 guards against the risk that interested persons could influence the issuer, its subsidiaries or associated companies to enter into transactions with them that may adversely affect the interests of the issuer or its shareholders (Rule 901); IPTs deal essentially with conflicts of interest. In applying the rules, regard must be had to that objective and to the economic and commercial substance of the transaction, instead of legal form and technicality (Rule 902). Chapter 10 (significant transactions) must also be observed (Rule 903).

*Source note: CG3 s3; LM r 901–903; Cyril-CG §3.1.*

### ccp-1-1-22 — Entity at risk
*Keywords: entity at risk, Rule 904(2), issuer, subsidiary not listed, associated company controlled by listed group, approved exchange*

**End point:** An "entity at risk" is (a) the issuer; (b) a subsidiary of the issuer not listed on SGX or an approved exchange; or (c) an associated company not so listed, provided the listed group (or the listed group and its interested persons) has control over it (Rule 904(2)). An IPT is a transaction between an entity at risk and an interested person (Rule 904(5)).

*Source note: CG3 s3; LM r 904(2), (5).*

### ccp-1-1-23 — Interested person: director, CEO, controlling shareholder and associates
*Keywords: interested person, Rule 904(4), director, chief executive officer, controlling shareholder, 15%, in fact exercises control, associate, immediate family, spouse, child, adopted child, step-child, sibling, brother, sister, parent, trustee, beneficiary, company 30% interest, Swift Limited, Castle Pte Ltd*

**End point:** For a company, an interested person is a director, CEO or controlling shareholder of the issuer, or an associate of any of them (Rule 904(4)(a)). A controlling shareholder holds directly or indirectly 15% or more of the voting shares, or in fact exercises control. An associate of an individual covers his immediate family (spouse, child, adopted child, step-child, sibling, parent), the trustees of a trust of which he or his immediate family is a beneficiary, and any company in which he and his immediate family together have an interest of 30% or more. So the CEO's adopted child and a director's brother are interested persons, and so is a company in which a director holds 5% and her husband 30% (35% together) (SQ p15 Q5: options A–C all false).

> **Definitions (CG3 s4)**
> - "Associate" of a director, CEO or controlling shareholder (individual): (i) his immediate family, i.e. spouse, child, adopted child, step-child, sibling and parent; (ii) trustees of any trust of which he or his immediate family is a beneficiary; (iii) any company in which he and his immediate family together have an interest of 30% or more.
> - "Controlling shareholder": a person who (a) holds directly or indirectly 15% or more of the voting shares in the issuer; or (b) in fact exercises control over the issuer.

*Source note: CG3 s4; LM r 904(4); SQ p15 Q5 and p114 (key D). The associate definition is from the Listing Manual's Chapter 1 definitions, as summarised in CG3 s4 (Chapter 1 is not in the course Reading List).*

### ccp-1-1-24 — SGX's power to deem an interested person (Rule 904(4A))
*Keywords: Rule 904(4A), deem interested person, agreement or arrangement with an interested person, cousin, not immediate family, conduit, interposed party, SGX may deem*

**End point:** SGX may deem any person or entity an interested person if it has entered into, or proposes to enter into, (a) a transaction with an entity at risk and (b) an agreement or arrangement with an interested person in connection with that transaction (Rule 904(4A)). So although a director's cousin is not an "associate", SGX may possibly deem him an interested person (SQ p15 Q5, key D).

*Source note: CG3 s5; LM r 904(4A); SQ p15 Q5 and p114 (key D).*

### ccp-1-1-25 — What counts as a "transaction" (Rule 904(6))
*Keywords: transaction definition, Rule 904(6), financial assistance, loan, guarantee, acquisition, disposal, leasing of assets, goods or services, issuance or subscription of securities, options, joint venture, joint investment, ordinary course of business, directly or indirectly, interposed entities*

**End point:** "Transaction" includes (a) providing or receiving financial assistance; (b) acquiring, disposing of or leasing assets; (c) providing or receiving goods or services; (d) issuing or subscribing for securities; (e) granting or being granted options; and (f) establishing joint ventures or joint investments — whether or not in the ordinary course of business, and whether entered into directly or indirectly (e.g. through interposed entities) (Rule 904(6)).

*Source note: CG3 s6; LM r 904(6).*

### ccp-1-1-26 — 3% announcement, 5% approval, aggregation, S$100,000 floor
*Keywords: Rule 905, Rule 906, 3% net tangible assets, immediate announcement, 5% shareholders approval, latest audited NTA, group, aggregation same interested person same financial year, below $100,000 disregarded, Exchange may aggregate small transactions, negative NTA market capitalisation*

**End point:** An IPT of 3% or more of the group's latest audited net tangible assets (NTA) must be announced immediately (Rule 905(1)); once IPTs with the same interested person in the same financial year aggregate to 3% or more, the latest and all future transactions with that person that year must be announced (Rule 905(2)). An IPT of 5% or more of NTA, alone or aggregated with other transactions with the same interested person in the same financial year, needs shareholders' approval (Rule 906(1)); a transaction already approved need not be aggregated again. Transactions below S$100,000 are disregarded (Rules 905(3), 906(2)), but SGX may aggregate them and treat them as one (Rules 905(5), 906(4)). If NTA is negative, consult SGX on another benchmark, e.g. market capitalisation. A transaction between 3% and 5% needs an announcement but not shareholders' approval (SQ p16 Q8, key B).

> **Thresholds (latest audited group NTA)**
> - < S$100,000: disregarded (SGX may aggregate such transactions).
> - ≥ 3% (single or aggregated, same interested person, same FY): immediate announcement.
> - ≥ 5% (single or aggregated): announcement and shareholders' approval.

*Source note: CG3 s7–8; LM r 905–906; Cyril-BF §3.3.4; SQ p16 Q8 and p114 (key B).*

### ccp-1-1-27 — Value of a transaction; "same interested person"; negative NTA
*Keywords: Rule 909, value of transaction, amount at risk, partly-owned subsidiary effective interest, joint venture equity shareholders' loans guarantees, borrowing interest payable, lending interest plus loan, disposal below market value or book value, Rule 908 same interested person, primary interested person, same group*

**End point:** The value of a transaction is the amount at risk to the issuer (Rule 909): (1) for a partly-owned subsidiary or associated company, the issuer's effective interest; (2) for a JV, the equity participation, shareholders' loans and guarantees given by the entity at risk; (3) borrowing from an interested person: the interest payable; lending to one: the interest payable plus the loan; (4) if an asset's market or book value exceeds the consideration from the interested person, the higher of market or book value. For aggregation, transactions with a primary interested person and with his associates, and with members of the same group, count as transactions with the "same interested person" (Rule 908), subject to the listed-group carve-out.

*Source note: LM r 908–909.*

### ccp-1-1-28 — Contents of the IPT announcement; audit committee statement (Rule 917)
*Keywords: Rule 917, announcement contents, details of interested person, nature of interest, book value, net profits, open market value, rationale and benefit, audit committee view normal commercial terms not prejudicial minority shareholders, independent financial adviser, IFA, current total for financial year*

**End point:** A Rule 905 announcement must give: details of the interested person and its interest; details of the transaction (book value, net profits attributable, latest open market value, terms and how they were arrived at); the rationale for and benefit to the entity at risk; a statement whether the audit committee considers the transaction on normal commercial terms and not prejudicial to the issuer and its minority shareholders, or that it is getting an independent financial adviser's opinion first (not needed for Rule 916(1)–(3) transactions); and the year-to-date totals with that interested person and of all IPTs (Rule 917).

*Source note: LM r 917.*

### ccp-1-1-29 — Shareholder approval: timing; interested person abstains
*Keywords: Rule 918, approval before transaction, conditional on approval, before completion, Rule 919, interested person and associates must not vote, proxies, abstain, circular*

**End point:** Shareholders' approval must be obtained before the transaction is entered into or, if the transaction is expressed to be conditional on approval, before completion (Rule 918). The interested person and any associate must not vote on the resolution, nor accept appointment as proxies unless given specific voting instructions (Rule 919).

*Source note: LM r 918–919; Cyril-BF §3.3.4 (voting restrictions on interested persons).*

### ccp-1-1-30 — Annual report disclosure of IPTs (Rule 907)
*Keywords: Rule 907, annual report, aggregate value of interested person transactions, name of interested person, nature of relationship, excluding transactions less than $100,000, shareholders' mandate Rule 920*

**End point:** The annual report must disclose the aggregate value of IPTs entered into during the financial year, giving for each interested person its name, the nature of the relationship, the aggregate value of all IPTs in the year (excluding those under S$100,000 and those under a shareholders' mandate), and the aggregate value of IPTs conducted under a shareholders' mandate (excluding those under S$100,000) (Rule 907).

*Source note: CG3 s9; LM r 907.*

### ccp-1-1-31 — Exceptions from all requirements (Rule 915)
*Keywords: Rule 915, exceptions, not required to comply with Rules 905 906 907, dividends, bonus issue, preferential offer pro-rata, rights, employees share option scheme, investee company less than 5%, open market securities, publicly quoted fixed or graduated scale, utilities, financial institution normal commercial terms ordinary course, director's fees, remuneration, golden parachute, D&O insurance, defence funding*

**End point:** Rule 915 transactions need no announcement, no shareholders' approval and no annual report disclosure (Rules 905, 906 and 907 do not apply).

> **Rule 915 — exempt from Rules 905, 906 and 907**
> - (1) Dividends, share subdivision or consolidation, bonus issue, preferential offer, or off-market acquisition of the issuer's shares, made to all shareholders pro rata (incl. exercise of rights, options or warrants under the preferential offer).
> - (2) Grant of options and issue of securities on their exercise under an SGX-approved employees' share option scheme.
> - (3) Transaction with an investee company where the interested person's interest in it (other than through the issuer) is less than 5%.
> - (4) Transaction in marketable securities on the open market where the counterparty's identity is unknown at the time.
> - (5) Goods or services at a publicly quoted fixed or graduated scale, applied consistently to all customers or a class of customers (e.g. telecoms, postal, utilities, fixed-price retail goods).
> - (6)–(7) Provision or receipt of financial assistance or services by/from an MAS-licensed or approved financial institution, on normal commercial terms and in the ordinary course of business.
> - (8) Director's fees and remuneration, and employment remuneration (excluding "golden parachute" payments).
> - (9) Insurance and indemnities for directors and CEOs, to the extent the Companies Act permits.
> - (10) Defence funding for directors and CEOs, as permitted by ss 163A–163B CA.

*Source note: CG3 s10–11; LM r 915; Cyril-CG §3.4.*

### ccp-1-1-32 — Exceptions from shareholder approval only (Rule 916)
*Keywords: Rule 916, exemption from shareholders approval, announcement still required, lease or tenancy not more than 3 years, property valuer valuation, joint venture with interested person risks and rewards proportionate, audit committee view, no existing equity interest, loan to joint venture pro rata same terms, public tender award, boards majority different, audit committees completely different*

**End point:** Rule 916 transactions need no shareholders' approval, but the announcement and annual report disclosure still apply.

> **Rule 916 — exempt from Rule 906 only**
> - (1) Entering into or renewing a lease or tenancy of real property of not more than 3 years, if the terms are supported by a property valuer's valuation.
> - (2) Investment in a JV with an interested person if: (a) risks and rewards are in proportion to each partner's equity; (b) the issuer announces that its audit committee is of that view and that the JV terms are not prejudicial to the issuer and its minority shareholders; and (c) the interested person had no existing equity interest in the JV before the entity at risk joined.
> - (3) A loan to such a JV if: (a) all JV partners lend in proportion to their equity and on the same terms; (b) the interested person had no prior equity interest; and (c) the AC confirms by announcement that the loan is not prejudicial and the risks and rewards are proportionate.
> - (4) Award of a contract by public tender to an interested person, if the awarder announces the prices of all bids and the basis for selecting the winner, and the listed bidder and listed awarder have boards with a majority of different directors (not accustomed to act on the interested person's instructions) and completely different audit committees.
> - (5) Receipt by an interested person of a contract awarded by public tender, on similar conditions.

*Source note: CG3 s12–13; LM r 916; Cyril-CG §3.5.*

### ccp-1-1-33 — General mandate for recurrent transactions (Rule 920)
*Keywords: Rule 920, general mandate, shareholders' mandate, recurrent transactions, revenue or trading nature, day-to-day operations, purchase and sale of supplies and materials, not assets undertakings businesses, annual renewal, circular, independent financial adviser opinion, audit committee, interested person abstain*

**End point:** An issuer may seek a general mandate from shareholders for recurrent transactions of a revenue or trading nature, or those necessary for its day-to-day operations (e.g. buying and selling supplies and materials), but not for buying or selling assets, undertakings or businesses (Rule 920(1)). The mandate is subject to annual renewal. The issuer must issue a circular with the prescribed information (including the interested persons, the nature and rationale of the transactions, the pricing methods, an IFA's opinion that they ensure normal commercial terms, and a statement that the interested person and associates will abstain), and disclose the aggregate value of mandated transactions in its annual report and periodic results. On renewal no IFA opinion is needed if the AC confirms the pricing methods are unchanged and still sufficient. Transactions under a mandate are not separately subject to Rules 905 and 906.

*Source note: CG3 s14; LM r 920; Cyril-CG §3.6.*

## 1.IX The Code of Corporate Governance and comply-or-explain

- **Is the Code law? Must a listed company follow it? Not appointing a lead independent director — what must it do?** Principles: comply; Provisions: comply or explain with a meaningful explanation; Practice Guidance: voluntary (SQ p16 Q9, key B) → [1-34](#ccp-1-1-34)

### ccp-1-1-34 — Status of the Code; Rule 710; Principles, Provisions, Practice Guidance
*Keywords: Code of Corporate Governance 2018, 4th edition, not law, comply or explain, Rule 710, principles mandatory, provisions comply or explain, explain variation, consistent with intent of principle, Practice Guidance voluntary non-binding, CGAC, lead independent director, Mercury Ltd*

**End point:** The Code (4th ed., issued with the Practice Guidance in August 2018 by MAS) is the foundation of Singapore's governance principles but is not law; Rule 710 applies it on a "comply or explain" basis. An issuer must describe its governance practices in its annual report with specific reference to the Principles and Provisions, and must comply with the Principles. Where its practice varies from a Provision, it must explicitly state which Provision, explain the reason, and explain how its practice is consistent with the intent of the relevant Principle. So a company without a lead independent director may keep its arrangement if it gives a meaningful explanation of the departure (SQ p16 Q9, key B). The Practice Guidance complements the Code but is voluntary; its content is overseen by the CGAC. Benefits of comply-or-explain: avoids burdensome mandatory rules and one-size-fits-all solutions, sets aspirational standards, allows innovation, and lets companies internalise good governance.

> **Hierarchy (CG3 s18)**
> - Listing Rules: mandatory rules every listed company must follow — mandatory.
> - Code 2018, 13 Principles: overarching, non-disputable statements of good governance — mandatory (comply and explain).
> - Code 2018 Provisions: actionable steps supporting the Principles — comply or explain.
> - Practice Guidance: non-binding guidance and best practice — voluntary.

*Source note: CG3 s15–19; LM r 710; CODE paras 7–8 (Introduction); SQ p16 Q9 and p114 (key B). The slide says "52 Provisions"; not counted here.*

> - Remuneration matters: 6 Procedures for developing remuneration policies; 7 Level and mix of remuneration; 8 Disclosure on remuneration.
> - Accountability and audit: 9 Risk management and internal controls; 10 Audit Committee.
> - Shareholder rights and engagement: 11 Shareholder rights and conduct of general meetings; 12 Engagement with shareholders.

*Source note: CG4 s3; CODE pp4–18.*

### ccp-1-1-36 — Who is "independent" (Provision 2.1); substantial shareholder
*Keywords: independent director definition, Provision 2.1, independent in conduct character and judgement, relationship with company, related corporations, substantial shareholders 5%, officers, interfere or reasonably perceived to interfere, independent business judgement, Rule 1207(10B), Practice Guidance further tests*

**End point:** An "independent" director is one who is independent in conduct, character and judgement, and has no relationship with the company, its related corporations (holding company, subsidiary, fellow subsidiary), its substantial shareholders (5% or more of voting shares) or its officers that could interfere, or be reasonably perceived to interfere, with the exercise of his independent business judgement in the company's best interests (Provision 2.1). The NC determines independence annually (Provision 4.4), and the Board identifies each independent director in the annual report (Rule 1207(10B)). The Rule 210(5)(d) tests (see [1-37](#ccp-1-1-37)) are mandatory; the further tests in the Practice Guidance are not.

*Source note: CG4 s4–5; CODE Provision 2.1 and footnotes 3–5, Provision 4.4.*

### ccp-1-1-37 — Rule 210(5)(d): employment, immediate family, 9-year rule
*Keywords: Rule 210(5)(d), not independent, employed by issuer or related corporation, current or past three financial years, immediate family member employed remuneration determined by remuneration committee, nine years aggregate, before or after listing, until next AGM, mandatory tests, Lexus Limited, Sigma Ltd, Ben, Sandy, Geraldine, Linda, John, Lionel, Anabelle, Ronald, subsidiary employee*

**End point:** A director is not independent if (i) he is or has been employed by the issuer or any related corporation in the current or any of the past three financial years; (ii) an immediate family member is or has been so employed in that period and his/her remuneration is or was determined by the RC; or (iv) he has been a director of the issuer for an aggregate of more than nine years (before or after listing) — he may stay independent until the next AGM ends (Rule 210(5)(d)). These tests are mandatory. Applied in the Sample Questions: a current employee of the issuer's subsidiary (related corporation) is disqualified (SQ p15 Q6, key D: Ronald); a person who was an ID from 2015 to 2018 (3 years in aggregate) is not disqualified (SQ p14 Q4, key D: Linda), whereas a candidate whose wife is the current CEO, a newly joined marketing director, and a current employee of a subsidiary are.

> **Rule 210(5)(d)**
> - A director will not be independent:
>   - (i) if he is employed or has been employed by the issuer or any of its related corporations in the current or any of the past three financial years;
>   - (ii) if he has an immediate family member who is employed or has been employed by the issuer or any of its related corporations in the current or any of the past three financial years, and whose remuneration is or was determined by the remuneration committee of the issuer;
>   - (iii) [Deleted 11 Jan 2023];
>   - (iv) if he has been a director of the issuer for an aggregate period of more than nine years (whether before or after listing); he may continue to be considered independent until the conclusion of the next AGM.

*Source note: CG4 s5; LM r 210(5)(d) (Nic's copy of Rule 210(5)); CODE Provision 2.1 footnote 6 (limb (iv) applies from the AGM for FY ending on or after 31 Dec 2023); SQ p14 Q4, p15 Q6 and p114 (keys D, D). The SQ keys give the answer only; the reasons for each candidate are applied here from Rule 210(5)(d).*

### ccp-1-1-38 — Board composition
*Keywords: board composition, Rule 210(5)(c), at least two independent directors, one-third of board, fill vacancy within two to three months, Provision 2.2 majority independent if Chairman not independent, Provision 2.3 non-executive majority, Provision 3.1 Chairman and CEO separate persons, Provision 3.3 lead independent director, Chairman not independent when also CEO or immediate family*

**End point:** The board must have at least two non-executive independent directors, and IDs must make up at least one-third of the board; a vacancy should be filled within two months and in any case within three (Rule 210(5)(c), mandatory). Under the Code: non-executive directors make up a majority of the board (Provision 2.3); IDs make up a majority where the Chairman is not independent (Provision 2.2); the Chairman and CEO are separate persons (Provision 3.1); and the board has a lead independent director where the Chairman is conflicted, especially when not independent (Provision 3.3).

> **Board composition table (CG4 s6)**
> - Non-executive directors: majority — Provision 2.3.
> - Independent directors, regular board: at least 2, and one-third of the board — Rule 210(5)(c).
> - Independent directors if the Chairman is not independent: majority of the board — Provision 2.2.
> - Chairman not independent: appoint a lead ID — Provision 3.3.
> - The Chairman is not independent if he is not an ID, is also the CEO, is an immediate family member of (or has close family ties with) the CEO, or is part of management (CODE fn 8).

*Source note: CG4 s6; LM r 210(5)(c); CODE Provisions 2.2, 2.3, 3.1, 3.3 and fn 8.*

### ccp-1-1-39 — Director training; re-nomination every 3 years
*Keywords: Rule 210(5)(a), mandatory training, no prior experience as director of listed issuer, Practice Note 2.3, nominating committee basis disclosed, Rule 720(5) re-nomination re-appointment every three years, sustainability training Rule 720(7)*

**End point:** A director with no prior experience as a director of an SGX-listed issuer must undergo the training prescribed in Practice Note 2.3 on a listed director's roles and responsibilities; if the NC thinks training is unnecessary because of other relevant experience, it must disclose the basis (Rule 210(5)(a)). All directors must submit themselves for re-nomination and re-appointment at least once every three years (Rule 720(5)). Directors must also do one-time sustainability training (see [1-20](#ccp-1-1-20)).

*Source note: CG4 s7; CG-LP p4 (Rule 210(5)(a), Rule 720(7), PN 2.3); LM r 210(5)(a); CG2 s17.*

### ccp-1-1-41 — Audit Committee: composition, duties, internal audit

**End point:** The AC has at least three directors, all non-executive, the majority (including the AC Chairman) independent; at least two members, including the Chairman, have recent and relevant accounting or related financial management expertise (Provision 10.2). It does not include former partners or directors of the existing auditing firm within two years of their leaving, or while they have a financial interest in it (Provision 10.3). Its duties include reviewing the adequacy, effectiveness, independence, scope and results of the external audit and internal audit function; significant financial reporting issues; the adequacy and effectiveness of internal controls and risk management; and the whistle-blowing policy (Provision 10.1). The issuer must establish and maintain an effective internal audit function (Rule 719(3)).

*Source note: CG4 s9–10; CODE Provisions 10.1–10.3.*

### ccp-1-1-42 — Remuneration Committee
*Keywords: remuneration committee, RC, Provision 6.1, Provision 6.2, at least three directors, all non-executive, majority independent, RC chairman independent, remuneration framework, key management personnel, specific packages, termination terms*

**End point:** The RC has at least three directors, all non-executive, the majority (including the RC Chairman) independent (Provision 6.2). Its duties include recommending to the Board a general framework of remuneration for the Board and key management personnel and the specific remuneration packages for each director and KMP, and considering all aspects of remuneration, including termination terms, to ensure they are fair (Provisions 6.1, 6.3).

*Source note: CG4 s11; CODE Provisions 6.1–6.3.*

### ccp-1-1-43 — Nominating Committee
*Keywords: nominating committee, NC, Provision 4.2, at least three directors, majority independent, NC chairman independent, CEO or executive director may be a member, lead independent director member, Provision 4.1, Provision 4.4, board appointments, determine independence annually, board evaluation, succession plans, training*

**End point:** The NC has at least three directors, the majority (including the NC Chairman) independent; the lead ID, if any, should be a member (Provision 4.2). Unlike the AC and RC, the NC need not be all non-executive, so the CEO or another executive director may sit on it. Its duties include recommending all board appointments and re-appointments, determining annually whether a director is independent, reviewing the process and criteria for evaluating the Board, its committees and directors, reviewing succession plans, and reviewing training and professional development programmes (Provisions 4.1, 4.4).

*Source note: CG4 s12–13; CODE Provisions 4.1–4.4.*

### ccp-1-1-44 — Board risk committee; board's comment on internal controls
*Keywords: board risk committee, Provision 9.1, nature and extent of significant risks, Rule 1207(10), adequacy and effectiveness of internal controls and risk management systems, audit committee concurrence, material weaknesses, Practice Note 12.2*

**End point:** The Board should consider establishing a Board Risk Committee to help determine the nature and extent of the significant risks the issuer is willing to take (Provision 9.1). The Board must comment in the annual report on the adequacy and effectiveness of the issuer's internal controls (financial, operational, compliance and IT) and risk management systems, state whether the AC concurs, and disclose any material weaknesses and the steps taken (Rule 1207(10), PN 12.2).

*Source note: CG4 s14; LM r 1207(10) (Nic's copy); CODE Provision 9.1.*

### ccp-1-1-47 — False trading, market rigging, market manipulation (ss 197–198)
*Keywords: s 197 SFA, false trading, market rigging, false or misleading appearance of active trading, wash sales, matched orders, s 198 SFA, market manipulation, two or more transactions, raising lowering maintaining stabilising price, intent to induce others*

**End point:** A person must not do anything or engage in a course of conduct whose purpose, or one of whose purposes, is to create a false or misleading appearance of active trading in capital markets products on an organised market, or of the market for or price of such products (s 197(1)); conduct that creates or is likely to create such an appearance is also caught (s 197(1A)). A person must not carry out two or more transactions in a corporation's securities that have or are likely to have the effect of raising, lowering, maintaining or stabilising their price, with intent to induce others to deal in them (s 198(1)).

*Source note: SFA12 p3–5 (ss 197–198).*

### ccp-1-1-48 — False or misleading statements; fraudulent inducement; deceptive devices (ss 199–201)
*Keywords: s 199 SFA, false or misleading statement, material particular, induce subscription purchase or sale, affect market price, does not care whether true, knows or ought reasonably to have known, s 200 fraudulently inducing persons to deal, misleading forecast, dishonest concealment, s 201 manipulative and deceptive devices, fraud*

**End point:** A person must not make a statement or disseminate information that is false or misleading in a material particular and likely to induce others to subscribe for, buy or sell securities, or to affect their market price, if he does not care whether it is true or knows or ought reasonably to have known it is false or misleading (s 199). A person must not induce or attempt to induce another to deal in capital markets products by a statement, promise or forecast he knows or ought reasonably to know is misleading, false or deceptive, by dishonest concealment of material facts, or by reckless statements (s 200(1)). A person must not, in connection with dealing in capital markets products, employ any device, scheme or artifice to defraud, or engage in any act or practice that operates as a fraud or deception (s 201).

*Source note: SFA12 p6–7 (ss 199–201). s 201 summarised from its heading and opening words.*

### ccp-1-1-49 — Insider trading: connected and other persons; "generally available"; material effect
*Keywords: insider trading, s 218 SFA connected person, officer, director, secretary, employee, receiver, judicial manager, liquidator, substantial shareholder, professional or business relationship, adviser, s 219 other persons, insider, tipping, communicate information, procure, s 215 generally available, readily observable, made known, reasonable period, s 216 material effect, persons who commonly invest, s 218(4) presumption of knowledge, s 220 no need to prove intention to use*

**End point:** Insider trading turns on information that is not generally available but which, if it were, a reasonable person would expect to have a material effect on the price or value of securities. A person in possession of it who knows (or, for a connected person, ought reasonably to know) those two facts must not deal in the securities, procure another to deal, or communicate the information to someone he knows or ought reasonably to know would deal or procure dealing (ss 218(2)–(3), 219(2)–(3)). For a connected person, knowledge is presumed once possession and non-availability are proved (s 218(4)). It is not necessary to prove an intention to use the information (s 220(1)).

> **Key definitions**
> - Connected person (s 218(5)–(6)): (i) an officer of the corporation or a related corporation — incl. a director, secretary, employee, receiver or receiver and manager, judicial manager, liquidator, or scheme administrator; (ii) a substantial shareholder of it or a related corporation; (iii) a person whose position may reasonably be expected to give access to such information through a professional or business relationship (of himself, his employer or a corporation of which he is an officer) with it, or through being an officer of a substantial shareholder.
> - Generally available (s 215): (a) readily observable matter; (b) made known in a manner likely to bring it to the attention of persons who commonly invest in such securities, and a reasonable period for dissemination has elapsed; or (c) deductions, conclusions or inferences from (a) or (b).
> - Material effect (s 216): the information would, or would be likely to, influence persons who commonly invest in the securities in deciding whether to subscribe for, buy or sell them.
> - Non-connected insider (s 219): the prosecution must prove he knew the information was not generally available and might have a material effect.

*Source note: SFA12 p16–23 (ss 215–220).*

### ccp-1-1-50 — Insider trading defences; parity of information (s 231)
*Keywords: insider trading defences, s 231 parity of information, other party knew, information made known publicly, s 222 redemption of units, s 223 underwriters, s 224 purchase pursuant to legal requirement, s 225 communication pursuant to legal requirement, s 226 Chinese wall corporations, s 228 own intentions or activities, s 229 corporations and officers, s 230 dealers acting on unsolicited instructions*

**End point:** It is a defence to dealing (or procuring) that the information came into the defendant's possession solely because it was made known in the manner in s 215(b)(i), or that the other party knew, or ought reasonably to have known, of it before the transaction (s 231(1)); there is a matching defence for communicating information (s 231(2)). Other exceptions cover redemption of CIS units (s 222), underwriters (s 223), purchases or communications required by law (ss 224–225), attribution of knowledge within corporations and partnerships, i.e. Chinese walls (ss 226–227), an individual's knowledge of his own intentions or activities (s 228), corporations and their officers (s 229), and dealers or representatives acting on a client's specific, unsolicited instruction without advice (s 230).

*Source note: SFA12 p24–29 (ss 222–231; section headings, and s 230 and s 231 text).*

### ccp-1-1-51 — Sanctions: criminal, civil penalty, civil liability
*Keywords: s 204 SFA, s 221 SFA, fine $250,000, imprisonment 7 years, s 232 civil penalty, MAS with consent of Public Prosecutor, balance of probabilities, three times profit gained or loss avoided, $2 million, minimum $100,000 corporation, $50,000 individual, s 233 six years, s 234 civil liability, contemporaneous dealing, compensation, reliance*

**End point:** Criminal: contravening Part 12 Division 1 (market misconduct) or ss 218–219 (insider trading) is an offence punishable by a fine up to S$250,000, imprisonment up to 7 years, or both (ss 204(1), 221(1)); no prosecution after a civil penalty order or agreement. Civil penalty: MAS, with the Public Prosecutor's consent, may sue; on a balance of probabilities the court may order a penalty up to the greater of 3 times the profit gained or loss avoided, or S$2 million, with a minimum of S$100,000 for a corporation and S$50,000 for anyone else (s 232(1)–(3)); action within 6 years, and not after a conviction or acquittal (s 233). Civil liability: a contravener who gained a profit or avoided a loss must compensate persons who dealt in the same products contemporaneously and suffered loss from the price difference (s 234(1)); for false statements under ss 199–201, investors who relied on them and suffered loss can claim whether or not the contravener profited (s 234(1A)).

*Source note: SFA12 p10 (s 204), p24 (s 221), p29–32 (ss 232–234).*

- **What is liquidation? Which Act — IRDA or Companies Act? Old proceedings?** → [2-1](#ccp-1-2-1)
  - Compulsory (court) v members' voluntary (solvent) v creditors' voluntary (insolvent) → [2-1](#ccp-1-2-1)

### ccp-1-2-1 — What liquidation is; IRDA framework; types of winding up
*Keywords: liquidation, winding up, IRDA, Insolvency Restructuring and Dissolution Act 2018, commenced 30 July 2020, Bankruptcy Act repealed, Companies Act insolvency provisions, transitional, compulsory winding up, members' voluntary winding up, MVL, creditors' voluntary winding up, CVL, solvent, insolvent*

**End point:** Liquidation is the process by which a company's assets are collected and sold to pay its debts; it seeks a fair distribution among creditors and members and ends the company's existence. The IRDA (in force 30 July 2020) consolidates corporate and personal insolvency law; the Bankruptcy Act and the Companies Act's insolvency provisions were repealed, though some Companies Act provisions remain relevant (e.g. s 210 schemes). The IRDA applies where the "relevant event" (e.g. the winding-up application or voluntary winding-up resolution) occurs after 30 July 2020. There are three species: compulsory winding up by the Court (usually on a creditor's application); members' voluntary winding up (solvent company); creditors' voluntary winding up (insolvent company placing itself in liquidation, with creditors choosing the liquidator).

*Source note: INS1 s2–5; INS-CS1 p4–5, p8; Cyril-INS §1.*

### ccp-1-2-2 — When winding up commences
*Keywords: commencement of winding up, s 126 IRDA, time of making the application, deemed to commence, s 161(6) IRDA, voluntary winding up, passing of resolution, provisional liquidator, statutory declaration lodged*

**End point:** A winding up by the Court is deemed to commence at the time the winding-up application is made (s 126(2)); if an order is later made, its effects relate back to the filing. A voluntary winding up commences when the resolution is passed, or, where a provisional liquidator was appointed before the resolution, when the directors lodged the statutory declaration with the Official Receiver (s 161(6)).

*Source note: INS-SYL p1 (ss 126, 161(6)); INS-CS1 p5, p16; Cyril-INS §1.1.*

### ccp-1-2-3 — Who may apply; contingent and prospective creditors
*Keywords: locus standi, s 124(1) IRDA, creditor, contingent creditor, prospective creditor, company, director, contributory, liquidator, judicial manager, Minister, MAS, Re People's Parkway Development, existing obligation*

**End point:** A company, whether or not in voluntary liquidation, may be wound up on the application of, among others, the company, a creditor (including a contingent or prospective creditor), a contributory, a liquidator, a judicial manager, the Minister or MAS (s 124(1)); the creditor route is s 124(1)(c). A contingent or prospective creditor is a person towards whom, under an existing obligation, the company may or will become subject to a present liability to pay money on a future event or date (*Re People's Parkway Development Pte Ltd* [1991] 2 SLR(R) 567).

*Source note: INS1 s7–8; INS-CS1 p11; INS-SYL p1; Cyril-INS §1.2.1. INS1 s8 cites People's Parkway at 576 and INS-SYL at 567; the citation here follows the Syllabus.*

### ccp-1-2-4 — Procedure for a winding-up application
*Keywords: winding up procedure, originating application, affidavit, advertisement, Gazette, newspaper, deposit Official Receiver, $10,000, service on company, notice of intention to appear, list of persons intending to appear, Corporate Insolvency and Restructuring Rules 2020*

**End point:** The application is made by originating application supported by affidavit; it must be advertised to notify the public; the applicant pays a deposit to the Official Receiver; it is served on the company; and those intending to appear give notice, with the applicant filing a list of persons intending to appear at the hearing. The details are in the Insolvency, Restructuring and Dissolution (Corporate Insolvency and Restructuring) Rules 2020.

*Source note: INS1 s9–10 (which says "Originating Summons", the pre-2022 term); Cyril-INS §1.2.2 (deposit of $10,000 with the Official Receiver; advertisement in an English daily and the Gazette).*

### ccp-1-2-5 — Inability to pay debts: the three routes
*Keywords: s 125(1)(e) IRDA, unable to pay its debts, most common ground, s 125(2)(a) statutory demand, s 125(2)(b) execution returned unsatisfied, s 125(2)(c) proved to satisfaction of court, contingent and prospective liabilities, presumption of insolvency*

**End point:** The most common ground is that the company is unable to pay its debts (s 125(1)(e)). A company is deemed unable to pay its debts if (a) it fails to comply with a statutory demand (s 125(2)(a)); (b) execution or other process on a judgment in favour of a creditor is returned unsatisfied in whole or part (s 125(2)(b)); or (c) it is proved to the Court's satisfaction that it is unable to pay its debts, taking into account contingent and prospective liabilities (s 125(2)(c)). A judgment is not a prerequisite to applying (SQ p21 Q7, option C wrong).

*Source note: INS1 s11–12; INS-CS1 p12–13; Cyril-INS §1.2.3.*

### ccp-1-2-6 — Statutory demand: over S$15,000, 3 weeks, pay / secure / compound
*Keywords: statutory demand, written demand, debt exceeding S$15,000, previously $10,000, left at registered office, 3 weeks, neglected to pay, secure or compound to reasonable satisfaction of creditor, BNP Paribas v Jurong Shipyard, irregular demand, Stoke, Maverick, Luka, $2,000*

**End point:** A company is deemed unable to pay its debts if a creditor owed more than S$15,000 (previously S$10,000 under the Companies Act) has served a demand by leaving it at the registered office, and the company has for 3 weeks afterwards neglected to pay the sum or to secure or compound for it to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction (s 125(2)(a)). The demand should state that the company may secure or compound the debt; otherwise it risks challenge as irregular (*BNP Paribas v Jurong Shipyard* [2009] 2 SLR(R) 949). The period is 3 weeks (SQ p19 Q3, key A). A S$2,000 debt cannot found the presumption, however long the company has failed to pay (SQ p21 Q7, key D).

> **s 125(2)(a) IRDA (as summarised in INS1, INS-CS1)**
> - A creditor to whom the company is indebted in a sum exceeding S$15,000 then due;
> - has served on the company, by leaving at its registered office, a written demand requiring payment;
> - and the company has for 3 weeks thereafter neglected to pay the sum, or to secure or compound for it to the reasonable satisfaction of the creditor.

*Source note: INS1 s13–14; INS-CS1 p12, p63; Cyril-INS §1.2.3; IRDA s 125(2)(a) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); SQ p19 Q3, p21 Q7 and p114 (keys A, D). The reason for Q7's key is inferred from the threshold; the question does not state it.*

### ccp-1-2-7 — Cash-flow test of insolvency
*Keywords: cash flow insolvency test, balance sheet test, current assets exceed current liabilities, debts as they fall due, 12-month timeframe, Sun Electric Power v RCMA Asia [2021] SGCA 60, factors*

**End point:** The test for inability to pay debts is cash-flow insolvency: whether the company's current assets exceed its current liabilities such that it can meet all debts as and when they fall due within a 12-month timeframe (*Sun Electric Power Pte Ltd v RCMA Asia Pte Ltd* [2021] SGCA 60). Relevant factors include the debts due or soon due, the demands for payment, the company's payment history, its liquid assets, its business and expected cash flows, and its financing arrangements.

*Source note: INS1 s17; INS-SYL p1; Cyril-INS §1.2.3 (factors).*

### ccp-1-2-8 — Disputed debt and cross-claim; abuse of process
*Keywords: disputed debt, substantial grounds, bona fide, genuine cross-claim, triable issues, stay or dismiss winding up application, injunction restraining application, abuse of process, Metalform Asia v Holland Leedon, Mohd Zain bin Abdullah v Chimbusco, RCMA Asia v Sun Electric exceptional, cross-claim exceeds debt, escrow, personal guarantee, Papaya Tree, Mega Farmer, Stoke, United, coerce a debtor to pay, judgment against applicant unpaid, undisputed debt, supplier wants to wind up customer*

**End point:** Where the debt is undisputed, the Court will ordinarily wind the company up (*Metalform Asia v Holland Leedon* [2007] 2 SLR(R) 268). But if the company genuinely disputes the debt on substantial grounds, or has a genuine cross-claim on substantial grounds, it may have the application stayed or dismissed, or restrain it by injunction, since presenting it would be an abuse of process; the standard is a triable issue (as on summary judgment). A cross-claim defeats the application where it equals or exceeds the debt: so a supplier owed an undisputed debt can apply to wind up only if its claim exceeds the debtor's unpaid judgment against it (SQ p18 Q1, key C), and a company sued-upon can resist if its genuine cross-claim on substantial grounds is equal to or more than the creditor's claim (SQ p19 Q2, key D) — the creditor need not first call on a personal guarantee, and the company need not already hold a judgment. In exceptional cases the Court may wind up despite a substantial dispute (*RCMA Asia v Sun Electric* [2020] SGHC 205).

*Source note: INS1 s15–16; INS-CS1 p14–15; INS-SYL p2 (BNP Paribas, Metalform, Mohd Zain); Cyril-INS §1.2.4; SQ p18 Q1, p18–19 Q2 and p114 (keys C, D). The "equal to or exceeding" gloss is from the SQ options; INS-CS1 p14 states only "genuine cross claim".*

### ccp-1-2-9 — Court's discretion not to wind up; public interest
*Keywords: court's discretion, s 128 IRDA, decline to wind up, temporarily insolvent, commercially viable, public interest, BNP Paribas at [19], employees, creditors, adjournment, s 201 IRDA wishes of creditors and contributories*

**End point:** The Court may decline to wind up a company even if it is unable to pay its debts (s 128), e.g. where it is only temporarily insolvent but commercially viable; it considers public interest elements, such as the effect on employees, other creditors, suppliers, customers and shareholders (*BNP Paribas v Jurong Shipyard* at [19]), and may adjourn. It may also have regard to the wishes of creditors and contributories (s 201).

*Source note: INS1 s18; INS-CS1 p15; INS-SYL p1, p3; Cyril-INS §1.2.5.*

### ccp-1-2-10 — When a company may wind up voluntarily; steps after the resolution
*Keywords: voluntary winding up, s 160 IRDA, special resolution, period fixed by constitution expires, event, general meeting resolution, lodge resolution with Registrar 7 days, Gazette, newspaper notice*

**End point:** A company may be wound up voluntarily (a) when the period fixed by its constitution for its duration expires, or an event on which it is to be dissolved occurs, and the company in general meeting resolves to wind up voluntarily; or (b) if it so resolves by special resolution (s 160(1)). Within 7 days of the resolution it must lodge a copy with the Registrar, and give notice in the Gazette and at least one local English daily newspaper.

*Source note: INS-SYL p2 (ss 160–165); Cyril-INS §1.3.1.*

### ccp-1-2-11 — Members' voluntary winding up: declaration of solvency
*Keywords: members' voluntary winding up, MVL, declaration of solvency, s 163 IRDA, majority of directors, inquiry into affairs, pay debts in full within 12 months, statement of affairs, lodged before resolution, liquidator appointed by general meeting s 164, creditors' meeting s 165, converts to creditors' winding up*

**End point:** For a members' voluntary winding up, a majority of the directors must make a declaration that, having inquired into the company's affairs, they believe it will be able to pay its debts in full within a period not exceeding 12 months after the winding up commences, with a statement of affairs (assets, liabilities, estimated expenses) attached; it must be lodged with the Registrar before the resolution (s 163). The company in general meeting appoints the liquidator (s 164). If the liquidator forms the view that the company will not be able to pay its debts in full within the declared period, he must summon a creditors' meeting (s 165), and the winding up proceeds as a creditors' one.

*Source note: INS-SYL p2; Cyril-INS §1.3.2. Cyril also says the declaration is made within 5 weeks of the resolution; INS-CS1 and the slides do not give a period, so only the "before the resolution" lodgement rule is relied on.*

### ccp-1-2-12 — Creditors' voluntary winding up
*Keywords: creditors' voluntary winding up, CVL, statutory declaration, cannot by reason of liabilities continue business, s 161 IRDA, provisional liquidator, licensed insolvency practitioner, meetings within one month, special resolution, creditors' meeting s 166, nomination of liquidator s 167, creditors' nominee prevails, committee of inspection*

**End point:** In a creditors' voluntary winding up the directors, at a directors' meeting, resolve and make a statutory declaration that the company cannot by reason of its liabilities continue its business, lodge it with the Official Receiver and the Registrar, and appoint a licensed insolvency practitioner as provisional liquidator (s 161). Meetings of members and creditors are then held within one month: the members pass a special resolution to wind up and nominate a liquidator; the creditors' meeting (on the same day or the next) may nominate a liquidator and a committee of inspection (s 166). If the two nominate different persons, the creditors' nominee is liquidator; if the creditors nominate no one, the company's nominee is (s 167).

*Source note: INS-CS1 p9; INS-SYL p2 (ss 160–163, 166–170); Cyril-INS §1.3.1, §1.3.3.*

## 2.IV Effect of winding up

- **Winding-up application filed; creditor is suing the company — can the action be stopped?** s 129 stay → [2-13](#ccp-1-2-13)
- **Winding-up order made — can a creditor start or continue an action?** Only with leave of court (SQ p20 Q4, key D) → [2-14](#ccp-1-2-14)
- **Company sold assets / paid a creditor / transferred shares after the application; uncompleted execution** → void unless the court orders otherwise → [2-15](#ccp-1-2-15)
- **Voluntary winding up: company's status, business, share transfers, legal proceedings** → [2-16](#ccp-1-2-16)

### ccp-1-2-13 — After the application: stay of proceedings (s 129)
*Keywords: s 129 IRDA, after winding up application before order, stay or restrain proceedings, company creditor or contributory may apply, discretionary stay*

**End point:** After a winding-up application is made and before an order, the company or any creditor or contributory may apply to the Court to stay or restrain further proceedings in any action against the company; the stay is discretionary and on terms (s 129).

*Source note: INS1 s19; INS-CS1 p16; Cyril-INS §1.4.1.*

### ccp-1-2-14 — After the order: no proceedings without leave (s 133)
*Keywords: s 133(1) IRDA, winding up order, provisional liquidator appointed, no action or proceeding proceeded with or commenced, leave of court, terms, mandatory stay, Korea Asset Management v Daewoo, Jumabhoy Rafiq, permission of the court*

**End point:** Once a winding-up order is made or a provisional liquidator appointed, no action or proceeding may be proceeded with or commenced against the company except with the leave of the Court and on the terms it imposes (s 133(1)). This is a mandatory stay; the exception is the Court's permission, not shareholders' permission, a size of claim, or directors' breach of duty (SQ p20 Q4, key D). Admiralty actions in rem against a ship are a separate matter (see Subject 3).

*Source note: INS1 s20; INS-CS1 p17; INS-SYL p3 (ss 129, 130, 133; Korea Asset Management; Jumabhoy Rafiq); Cyril-INS §1.4.1; Cyril-Exam (in rem proceedings unaffected); SQ p20 Q4 and p114 (key D).*

### ccp-1-2-15 — Void dispositions and executions; directors functus officio
*Keywords: s 130 IRDA, disposition of property after commencement void, transfer of shares, alteration in status of members, unless court otherwise orders, validation order, attachment sequestration distress execution void, s 206 uncompleted execution, directors functus officio, powers vest in liquidator*

**End point:** In a winding up by the Court, any disposition of the company's property (including things in action), and any transfer of shares or alteration in members' status, made after the commencement of the winding up (i.e. after the application) is void unless the Court orders otherwise (s 130(1)); attachments, sequestration, distress or execution put in force after commencement are void (s 130(2)), and creditors cannot keep the benefit of an uncompleted execution (s 206). On the winding-up order the directors become functus officio; their powers pass to the liquidator or Official Receiver.

*Source note: INS1 s20–21; INS-CS1 p17–18; INS4 s21; Cyril-INS §1.4.1.*

### ccp-1-2-16 — Effect of voluntary winding up (ss 162, 170)
*Keywords: voluntary winding up effects, s 162 IRDA, corporate state continues until dissolved, cease business except for beneficial winding up, share transfers void without liquidator's sanction, s 170 IRDA, execution void, no proceedings without leave of court*

**End point:** In a voluntary winding up the company's corporate state and powers continue until it is dissolved, but it must cease business except so far as the liquidator considers necessary for its beneficial winding up (s 162(1)–(2)); share transfers not made to or sanctioned by the liquidator, and alterations in members' status, are void (s 162(3)). Executions put in force after commencement are void unless the Court orders otherwise, and no action may be proceeded with or commenced against the company except with leave of the Court (s 170).

*Source note: INS-SYL p3 (ss 162, 170); Cyril-INS §1.4.2.*

## 2.V The liquidator and the end of the liquidation

- **Who runs the company in liquidation? Duties of the liquidator; can the court sanction his acts retrospectively?** → [2-17](#ccp-1-2-17)
- **Liquidator sues on the company's behalf and loses — who bears the adverse costs?** Estate costs rule → [2-18](#ccp-1-2-18)
- **Company dissolved — can the dissolution be undone?** Within 2 years → [2-19](#ccp-1-2-19)

### ccp-1-2-17 — Liquidator / Official Receiver: status, duties, powers
*Keywords: liquidator, Official Receiver, appointed by court, officer of the court, fiduciary duties, independent, act fairly, remuneration, licensed insolvency practitioner, consent, s 144 IRDA powers, sanction, Re Kirkham no retrospective authorisation, statement of affairs s 141, examination s 244, close business, realise assets, adjudicate proofs, distribute, dissolve*

**End point:** On a winding-up order the Official Receiver (with consent) or a private liquidator (a licensed insolvency practitioner who consents) takes over. The liquidator is appointed by and is an officer of the Court, owes fiduciary duties to the company and its creditors, must be independent and act fairly, and is entitled to remuneration (ss 139, 144). His tasks: close the business, collect and realise the assets, adjudicate proofs of debt, distribute by priority (s 203) and dissolve the company. He relies on the statement of affairs (s 141) and may apply to examine persons with knowledge of the company's affairs (s 244). His powers are in s 144 (s 177 in voluntary winding up); where sanction is required, the Court will not authorise acts retrospectively (*Re Kirkham* [2023] 5 SLR 635).

*Source note: INS1 s21–25; INS-CS1 p7, p62; INS-SYL p3 (ss 139, 140, 144, 177); Cyril-INS §1.5.*

### ccp-1-2-18 — Estate costs rule
*Keywords: estate costs rule, liquidator sues on behalf of company, adverse costs order, priority over liquidation expenses and unsecured creditors, liquidator personally liable, Ho Wing On Christopher v ECRC Land, Lim Siew Soo v Sembawang Engineers*

**End point:** A liquidator may bring proceedings in the company's name, but under the estate costs rule an adverse costs order against the company ranks in priority over the other general expenses of the liquidation and the claims of unsecured creditors; a liquidator who pays out in breach of the rule is personally liable (*Ho Wing On Christopher v ECRC Land Pte Ltd* [2006] 4 SLR(R) 817).

*Source note: INS1 s35; INS-SYL p3 (Lim Siew Soo v Sembawang Engineers [2021] SGCA 16 also listed, not summarised in the materials).*

### ccp-1-2-19 — Dissolution and release; dissolution declared void
*Keywords: dissolution, release of liquidator, ss 147 149 IRDA, s 208 IRDA, court may declare dissolution void, within 2 years*

**End point:** When the affairs are wound up, the liquidator may apply for the company's dissolution and his own release (ss 147–149). The Court may declare a dissolution void at any time within 2 years after the date of dissolution (s 208).

*Source note: INS1 s36; INS-SYL p4–5 (ss 147–149, 208).*

### ccp-1-2-20 — Pari passu principle; no contracting out
*Keywords: pari passu, s 172 IRDA, like creditors treated alike, not all creditors equally, rank, contracting out contrary to public policy, Joo Yee Construction v Diethelm Industries, British Eagle v Air France, direct payment to subcontractors on liquidation*

**End point:** The company's property must be applied pari passu in satisfaction of its liabilities (s 172): like creditors are treated alike, according to their rank, which does not mean all creditors equally. Parties cannot contract out of pari passu distribution; such terms are struck down, e.g. a contract by which the company's debtor is to pay the company's unsecured creditors directly if it is liquidated (*Joo Yee Construction v Diethelm Industries* [1990] 1 SLR(R) 171).

*Source note: INS1 s26–27; INS-SYL p4–5; Cyril-INS §1.5.1.*

### ccp-1-2-21 — Order of priority; preferential debts (s 203)
*Keywords: ranking of creditors, secured creditors, preferential debts, s 203 IRDA, costs and expenses of winding up, Official Receiver, liquidator's remuneration, applicant's costs, wages or salary, retrenchment benefit, ex gratia, work injury compensation, CPF contributions, vacation leave, tax, GST, floating charge holders, unsecured creditors, subordinated, shareholders residue, capped amounts*

**End point:** Secured creditors realise their security outside the liquidation. Out of the rest, preferential debts are paid in priority to other unsecured debts in the order of s 203(1), ranking equally within each class; preferential debts are paid ahead of floating charge holders (from floating-charge assets) where the free assets are insufficient. Then ordinary unsecured creditors pari passu, then subordinated creditors, and any surplus to members under the constitution.

> **s 203(1) IRDA — preferential debts, in order**
> - (a) costs and expenses of the winding up incurred by the Official Receiver as liquidator (incl. any insolvency practitioner he appoints);
> - (b) other costs and expenses of the winding up, incl. the liquidator's remuneration and audit costs;
> - (c) costs of the applicant for the winding-up order;
> - (d) wages or salary of employees (capped by Ministerial order);
> - (e) retrenchment benefits or ex gratia payments (capped);
> - (f) work injury compensation under the Work Injury Compensation Act;
> - (g) CPF and similar contributions for a 12-month period around the commencement;
> - (h) vacation leave remuneration (capped);
> - (i) tax assessed and GST due before commencement, and tax assessed before the time for proving debts expires.

*Source note: INS1 s26, s28; INS-CS1 p7; IRDA s 203(1)–(2) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); Cyril-INS §1.5.1 (ranking list); RL-Sec p8.*

### ccp-1-2-22 — Provable debts (s 218); assessing proofs
*Keywords: provable debts, s 218 IRDA, proof of debt, debts and liabilities at commencement, future liabilities from obligations incurred before commencement, interest, not provable, unliquidated damages in tort, bailment, restitution, cannot fairly estimate, contingent liabilities, Panorama Development, Fustar Chemicals, liquidator not bound by audited accounts, basis to query*

**End point:** Creditors lodge proofs of debt (Part 9 Division 2). Provable debts are the debts and liabilities, present or future, certain or contingent, to which the company is subject at the commencement of the winding up (or JM), or may become subject afterwards by reason of an obligation incurred before it, plus interest for the period before commencement in an insolvent winding up or JM (s 218(1)–(2)). Not provable: demands in unliquidated damages arising otherwise than by contract, promise or breach of trust (e.g. tort, bailment, restitution) (s 218(3)), and debts whose value cannot fairly be estimated. The liquidator must assess each proof; he is not bound by the audited accounts or audit confirmations, but must show some basis to query a debt that appears genuine on its face (*Fustar Chemicals v Liquidator of Fustar Chemicals* [2009] 4 SLR(R) 458).

*Source note: INS1 s28–30; INS-SYL p4 (s 218); Cyril-INS §1.5.2.*

### ccp-1-2-23 — Insolvency set-off (s 219)
*Keywords: insolvency set-off, s 219 IRDA, mutual credits mutual debts mutual dealings, mandatory, net balance provable, account taken at commencement date of application, personally liable, beneficially own, clear and ascertainable, contingent claims, excluded debts after notice, displaces contractual and equitable set-off, Good Property Land v Societe Generale, Park Hotel CQ v Law Ching Hung, Feima misfeasance not a dealing*

**End point:** Where there have been mutual credits, mutual debts or other mutual dealings between the company and a creditor, an account is taken and only the net balance is provable (s 219(2)); e.g. creditor owes $10, company owes $40, creditor proves for $30. Insolvency set-off is mandatory and displaces all other set-off, legal, equitable or contractual (*Park Hotel CQ v Law Ching Hung* [2024] 5 SLR 138). The account is taken as at the commencement of winding up, the date of the application (*Good Property Land v Société Générale* [1996] 1 SLR(R) 884), though later events may be considered for valuation. Mutuality requires each party to be personally liable for the debt owed to the other, and to own beneficially the debt owed to it, clearly and without inquiry. Excluded: debts not provable, and debts incurred after the creditor had notice of the pending application. A liquidator's misfeasance claim is not a "dealing" (*Feima International v Kyen Resources* [2022] SGHC 304).

*Source note: INS1 s31–34; INS-CS1 p17, p20–21, p62; INS-SYL p4; Cyril-INS §1.5.3. Conflict: INS1 s32 first says the account is taken at "the date of the winding up order", then corrects it to the date of the application; INS-CS1 p20 and s 126 support the application date, which is followed.*

### ccp-1-2-24 — Interest on debts (s 222); foreign currency claims
*Keywords: interest on debts, s 222 IRDA, prescribed rate, written instrument payable at certain time, written demand with notice of interest, period up to commencement, foreign currency conversion date, Re Lehman Brothers Finance Asia, date of winding up order, secured creditor interest realise within 12 months s 223*

**End point:** In an insolvent winding up or JM, where interest was not reserved or agreed, interest up to the commencement is allowed at no more than the prescribed rate: on a debt payable at a certain time under a written instrument, from that time; otherwise, if a written demand was made before commencement giving notice that interest would run, from the demand (s 222). A secured creditor is entitled to post-commencement interest only if it realises its security within 12 months of commencement or such further period as the liquidator allows (s 223). For foreign-currency claims in a compulsory liquidation, the conversion date is the date of the winding-up order, not of the application (*Re Lehman Brothers Finance Asia*).

*Source note: INS1 s34; INS-SYL p4 (s 222); Cyril-INS §1.5.4; RL-Sec p6 (s 223); Cyril-BF §1.4.2.*

### ccp-1-2-25 — Court's jurisdiction over the debtor (s 310)
*Keywords: bankruptcy jurisdiction, s 310 IRDA, domiciled in Singapore, property in Singapore, ordinarily resident, place of residence, carried on business, within 1 year before bankruptcy application*

**End point:** The Singapore Court has jurisdiction over a debtor who (a) is domiciled in Singapore; (b) has property in Singapore; or (c) within the year before the application was ordinarily resident or had a place of residence in Singapore, or carried on business in Singapore (s 310(1)). These are alternatives, so a debtor without property here can still be made bankrupt if, e.g., he lived here within the year.

*Source note: INS2 s4; INS-SYL p5 (s 310(1)); INS-CS1 p64 (practice MCQ, no answer given; the alternatives in the slides answer it); Cyril-INS §2.1.1.*

### ccp-1-2-26 — Who may apply; grounds (s 311)
*Keywords: bankruptcy application, creditor's application s 307, debtor's application s 308, s 311 IRDA, not less than S$15,000, aggregate, liquidated sum payable immediately, unable to pay, debt incurred outside Singapore, judgment or award enforceable in Singapore, Ambank v Yong Kim Yoon, registered foreign judgment*

**End point:** A bankruptcy application may be made by a creditor (s 307) or by the debtor himself (s 308). A creditor's application requires that (a) the debt, or aggregate of debts, is not less than S$15,000; (b) it is a liquidated sum payable to the applicant immediately; (c) the debtor is unable to pay his debts; and (d) where the debt was incurred outside Singapore, it is payable by virtue of a judgment or award enforceable by execution in Singapore (s 311). So the minimum is S$15,000 (SQ p20 Q5, key C). A foreign judgment must be registered in Singapore to be enforceable (*Ambank (M) Bhd v Yong Kim Yoon Raymond* [2009] 2 SLR(R) 659).

*Source note: INS2 s5–6, s8; INS-SYL p5; Cyril-INS §2.1.2–2.1.3; IRDA s 311(1)(a) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026; the Minister may change the figure by order, s 311(2)); SQ p20 Q5 and p114 (key C).*

### ccp-1-2-27 — Statutory demand (s 312); expedited application (s 314)
*Keywords: bankruptcy statutory demand, s 312 IRDA, presumption of inability to pay, 21 days, set aside statutory demand 14 days, served out of jurisdiction 21 days, IRDA Personal Insolvency Rules 64 65 66 67 73, defects in statutory demand, s 314 expedited, property significantly diminished*

**End point:** The debtor is presumed unable to pay if he has not, within 21 days of service of a statutory demand for a debt payable immediately, complied with it or applied to set it aside (s 312); an application to set aside is made within 14 days of service (21 days if served out of jurisdiction). Form, service and setting aside are governed by the IRDA Personal Insolvency Rules (rr 64–67, 73). A creditor may apply before the 21 days expire if there is a serious possibility that the debtor's property, or its value, will be significantly diminished in that period and the application says so (s 314).

*Source note: INS2 s7, s9; INS-SYL p5 (s 312; cases on defective demands); Cyril-INS §2.1.3.*

### ccp-1-2-28 — Effect of a bankruptcy order; Official Assignee and private trustee
*Keywords: bankruptcy order, commencement on date of order s 326, Gazette, s 327 effects, property vests in Official Assignee, OA receiver of bankrupt's property, private trustee s 36, mandatory private trustee unless OA consents in public interest, proof of debt 4 months, s 329 divisible property, s 352 order of distribution*

**End point:** A bankruptcy commences on the date the order is made and continues until discharge (s 326); the order is advertised in the Gazette. The bankrupt's property vests in the Official Assignee, who is receiver of the property and administers the bankrupt's affairs (s 327(1)); the property divisible among creditors is defined in s 329, and the order of distribution is in s 352. A private trustee in bankruptcy must be appointed in all cases (s 36(2)) unless the OA considers it in the public interest and consents to act. Creditors must file proofs of debt within 4 months.

*Source note: INS2 s9–10, s18, s20; INS-SYL p6 (ss 22–23, 36–39, 327).*

### ccp-1-2-29 — Duties of the bankrupt
*Keywords: duties of bankrupt, s 399 IRDA, assist Official Assignee, disclose property, deliver documents, attend creditors' meetings, statement of affairs s 332, accounts s 333*

**End point:** The bankrupt has a general duty to assist the Official Assignee in identifying and recovering the property of his estate (s 399), e.g. disclosing all property and proceedings, delivering documents and attending creditors' meetings; he must file a statement of affairs (s 332(1)) and submit accounts when the OA directs (s 333).

*Source note: INS2 s11; INS-SYL p7; Cyril-INS §2.2.*

### ccp-1-2-30 — Restrictions on the bankrupt
*Keywords: restrictions on bankrupt, s 400 trustee or personal representative, executor, s 401 legal proceedings, personal injury, matrimonial proceedings, leave Official Assignee, leave Singapore, s 412 obtain credit $1,000, business under another name, guarantee $1,000, s 148(1) Companies Act director, management of corporation, offence*

**End point:** An undischarged bankrupt (a) may not act as trustee or personal representative of any trust or estate without leave of Court (s 400); (b) may not commence, continue or defend proceedings, other than for injury to his person or matrimonial proceedings, without the OA's sanction (s 401(1)(a)); (c) may not leave Singapore without the OA's previous permission (s 401(1)(b)); (d) commits an offence if he obtains credit of S$1,000 or more, or gives a guarantee or security of S$1,000 or more, without disclosing his status, or trades under another name without disclosure (s 412(1)); (e) commits an offence if he acts as a director or takes part in the management of a corporation without the Court's or the OA's written permission (s 148(1) CA).

*Source note: INS2 s12 (section list); INS-SYL p7; Cyril-INS §2.3.*

### ccp-1-2-31 — Distribution of dividends; trustee's remuneration
*Keywords: dividend distribution, s 385 IRDA, Official Assignee declares dividends, end administration, trustee's remuneration, Rule 47 factors, complexity, exceptional responsibility, effectiveness, value and nature of assets, ss 41 42*

**End point:** The OA declares and distributes dividends whenever he has sufficient funds (s 385), and may end the administration once he has realised as much of the estate as he can without needlessly protracting the bankruptcy. A trustee's remuneration is fixed having regard to four factors (IRDA PI Rules r 47): the complexity of the case; any exceptional responsibility assumed; the effectiveness of the trustee's performance; and the value and nature of the estate's assets.

*Source note: INS2 s19, s21; INS-SYL p7 (ss 41–42, 385–387).*

### ccp-1-2-32 — Annulment; composition or scheme
*Keywords: annulment, s 392 IRDA court annulment, order ought not to have been made, debts and expenses paid or secured, Malaysia, s 393 Official Assignee certificate annulment, all proved debts paid, composition, scheme of arrangement, s 357 special resolution of creditors*

**End point:** The Court may annul a bankruptcy order if it ought not to have been made, or if all the debts and expenses of the bankruptcy have since been paid or secured to the Court's satisfaction, or in certain cases involving Malaysian proceedings (s 392(1)). The OA may annul by certificate if all proved debts and the expenses have been paid (s 393). Creditors may also accept a composition or scheme of arrangement of the bankrupt's affairs (s 357).

*Source note: INS2 s22–23; INS-SYL p7; Cyril-INS §2.4.*

### ccp-1-2-33 — Discharge by the Court (s 394)
*Keywords: discharge by court, s 394 IRDA, application by OA bankrupt or interested person, refuse discharge, absolute discharge, conditional discharge, future income, after-acquired property, s 394(4) (5) circumstances, books of account, rash speculation, undue preference, assets less than 20% of unsecured liabilities*

**End point:** The OA, the bankrupt or any interested person may apply for discharge at any time after the order (s 394(1)). The Court may (a) refuse discharge; (b) discharge absolutely; or (c) discharge on conditions, e.g. as to income later due or property acquired after discharge (s 394(3)). Where specified circumstances are proved (e.g. failure to keep books, trading while insolvent, rash speculation or extravagance, undue preferences, assets under 20% of unsecured liabilities), the Court must refuse discharge or impose conditions (s 394(4)–(5)).

*Source note: INS2 s24; INS-SYL p7; Cyril-INS §2.4 (Method 3).*

### ccp-1-2-34 — Discharge by certificate of the Official Assignee (s 395)
*Keywords: discharge by certificate, s 395 IRDA, differentiated discharge framework, first-time bankrupt, repeat bankrupt, target contribution, 3 years, 5 years, 7 years, 9 years, administration date, creditors' objection, more than half in number or one quarter in value, s 396*

**End point:** The OA (or private trustee) may issue a certificate of discharge under a differentiated framework (s 395). A first-time bankrupt: after paying the target contribution (or being unable to because of extenuating circumstances) and 3 years from the administration date with no objection by the relevant creditors, or 5 years; or in any case after 7 years. A repeat bankrupt: 5 years without objection or 7 years, after paying the target contribution; otherwise 9 years. Creditors holding not less than half in number, or more than one-fourth in value, of the proved debts can object (ss 395–396); time spent abroad without the OA's permission is disregarded (s 395(5)).

*Source note: INS2 s25–26; Cyril-INS §2.4 (Method 4 periods); IRDA s 395(2), (5) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-2-35 — Effect of discharge
*Keywords: effect of discharge, released from provable debts, s 397 IRDA, debts not released, Government debts, taxes, fines, fraud, fraudulent breach of trust, MediShield Life, personal injury damages, Women's Charter maintenance orders, confiscation, secured creditors unaffected, co-debtors not released*

**End point:** Discharge releases the bankrupt from all debts provable in the bankruptcy, with exceptions (s 397). It does not release debts owed to the Government or chargeable at its suit (unless the Minister agrees), debts from fraud or fraudulent breach of trust, or fines; and, unless the Court orders, it does not release personal-injury damages, Women's Charter (family) orders or confiscation orders. It does not affect secured creditors' rights to enforce security, and does not release co-debtors or sureties.

*Source note: INS-SYL p7 (s 397); Cyril-INS §2.7. Cyril cites s 271 for these rules; the Syllabus names s 397 as the effect-of-discharge section, which is followed (s 271 relates to voluntary arrangements).*

### ccp-1-2-36 — Individual voluntary arrangement (Part 14)
*Keywords: individual voluntary arrangement, IVA, Part 14 IRDA, binding arrangement with creditors, avoids bankruptcy, not available to undischarged bankrupt s 275, interim order ss 276-279, nominee, qualified and willing, creditors' meeting ss 280-285, nominee's report, approved implement, rejected discharged*

**End point:** An IVA lets a debtor make a binding voluntary arrangement with his creditors for repaying debts, avoiding bankruptcy; it is not available to an undischarged bankrupt (s 275). The Court may make an interim order (protecting the debtor while the proposal is considered) if the debtor intends to propose an arrangement, his nominee is qualified and willing to act, and the order is appropriate to facilitate the proposal (ss 276–279). The nominee reports and a creditors' meeting considers the proposal (ss 280–285); if approved it is implemented, if not the interim order is discharged and creditors can proceed.

*Source note: INS2 s13–15; INS-SYL p6; Cyril-INS §2.5.*

### ccp-1-2-37 — Debt repayment scheme (Part 15)
*Keywords: debt repayment scheme, DRS, Part 15 IRDA, liabilities not exceeding S$150,000, no bankruptcy or voluntary arrangement within 5 years, no DRS within 5 years, not sole proprietor partner LLP, moratorium s 293, secured creditors unaffected, certificate of completion s 301, Official Assignee*

**End point:** Under the DRS the debtor repays creditors under a plan administered by the OA instead of being made bankrupt. Conditions: total liabilities not exceeding S$150,000 (s 289(2)(a) and the DRS Regulations); the debtor was not bankrupt or under a voluntary arrangement, and not subject to a DRS, within the 5 years before the bankruptcy application; and he is not a sole proprietor or a partner of a firm or LLP. A moratorium applies while the scheme runs (s 293), but secured creditors may still realise their security; on completing the plan the debtor receives a certificate of completion (s 301).

*Source note: INS2 s16–17; INS-SYL p6; Cyril-INS §2.6.*

### ccp-1-2-38 — Aims and features of judicial management; which companies
*Keywords: judicial management, JM, Part 7 IRDA, rehabilitation, preservation, going concern, Pan-El crisis 1987, UK administration, alternative to winding up, moratorium, directors replaced, powers vest in judicial manager, corporation liable to be wound up, foreign company, not banks insurers*

**End point:** JM (Part 7 IRDA), introduced in 1987 after the Pan-El crisis and modelled on UK administration, gives a company in financial trouble the chance to rehabilitate and/or preserve its business as a going concern, on the basis that creditors are better served than in a winding up (the measure of last resort). Its features: a moratorium against proceedings and enforcement, giving time to restructure; and the replacement of the directors by independent professionals, with all the directors' powers vested in the judicial managers. The Court may make a JM order over any corporation liable to be wound up under the IRDA (so including a foreign company with a substantial connection), but not, e.g., banks or insurers.

*Source note: INS3 s2–5; INS-CS1 p36–38; INS6 s17; Cyril-INS §3.1 (exclusion of banks, finance and insurance companies).*

### ccp-1-2-39 — Threshold and test: the three purposes (s 89(1))
*Keywords: insolvency threshold, unable or likely to become unable to pay debts, would be likely to achieve, s 89(1)(a) survival going concern, s 89(1)(b) approval of compromise or arrangement s 210 CA or s 71, s 89(1)(c) more advantageous realisation of assets than winding up, not a purpose to assess grounds for winding up*

**End point:** The company must be, or be likely to become, unable to pay its debts, and the applicant must show that the order "would be likely" to achieve one or more of the three statutory purposes in s 89(1): (a) the survival of the company, or the whole or part of its undertaking, as a going concern; (b) the approval of a compromise or arrangement under s 210 CA or s 71 IRDA; (c) a more advantageous realisation of the company's assets than on a winding up. Assessing whether there are grounds to wind the company up is not a purpose (SQ p22 Q10, key D).

> **s 89(1) IRDA — purposes of judicial management**
> - (a) the survival of the company, or the whole or part of its undertaking, as a going concern;
> - (b) the approval of a compromise or an arrangement between the company and its creditors (s 210 CA or s 71 IRDA);
> - (c) a more advantageous realisation of the company's assets or property than on a winding up.

*Source note: INS3 s6–8; INS-CS1 p39–40; INS-SYL p8 (s 89); Cyril-INS §3.1; SQ p22 Q10 and p114 (key D).*

### ccp-1-2-40 — Who may apply (s 90); procedure; interim judicial manager
*Keywords: s 90 IRDA, who can apply, company by members' resolution, directors by board resolution, any creditor, originating application, affidavit, s 91 court's power, s 92 interim judicial manager, licensed insolvency practitioner, s 50, nominee opposed by creditors, Marina, Mr Shenton, creditor apply for judicial management, locus standi, standing, sell business as a going concern, smoother transition to liquidation, interests of shareholders*

**End point:** A court JM application may be made by (a) the company (by members' resolution), (b) its directors (by board resolution), or (c) any creditor (s 90), by originating application in open court supported by affidavit. A creditor of a struggling company can therefore apply, if JM is likely to achieve a s 89 purpose — e.g. there is a reasonable prospect of selling the business as a going concern (SQ p22 Q9, key C); a smoother route to liquidation, or benefit to shareholders, is not the test. The Court may appoint an interim judicial manager pending the hearing (s 92). The judicial manager must be a licensed insolvency practitioner (s 50); creditors may oppose the company's nominee.

*Source note: INS3 s14–15, s17; INS-CS1 p39; INS-SYL p8 (ss 90–92); Cyril-INS §3.2; SQ p22 Q9 and p114 (key C).*

### ccp-1-2-41 — Opposition by floating charge holder (s 91(6)); public interest (s 91(10))
*Keywords: s 91(6) IRDA, opposition to judicial management, person entitled to appoint receiver and manager, debenture secured by floating charge, disproportionately greater prejudice, unsecured creditors, s 91(10) public interest, Re Cosmotron Electronics, Re Bintan Lagoon Resort, public interest requires*

**End point:** The Court must dismiss a JM application if it is opposed by a person entitled to appoint a receiver and manager of the whole (or substantially the whole) of the company's property under a debenture secured by a floating charge, and the prejudice to that person from the order would be disproportionately greater than the prejudice to the unsecured creditors if it were not made (s 91(6)). But the Court may still make the order if the public interest so requires (s 91(10)). The question is not merely whether JM is in the public interest but whether the public interest requires it (*Re Bintan Lagoon Resort Ltd* [2005] 4 SLR(R) 336); in *Re Cosmotron Electronics* no public interest was found.

*Source note: INS3 s9–13; INS-CS1 p41; INS-SYL p8; RL-Sec p7; Cyril-INS §3.2.*

### ccp-1-2-42 — Judicial management by creditors' resolution (s 94)
*Keywords: s 94 IRDA, judicial management without court order, creditors' resolution, creditors' meeting, interim judicial manager appointed by company, 7 days' written notice, 21 days, members' or directors' resolution, consent, statutory declaration, s 95 moratorium on lodgment*

**End point:** A company can also be placed into JM without a court order, by a resolution of its creditors at a creditors' meeting (s 94). The company first appoints an interim judicial manager (authorised by a members' or directors' resolution) after giving 7 days' written notice to the prescribed persons, within 21 days of the notice and with their written consent; the appointee (a licensed insolvency practitioner, not the company's auditor) declares he has no conflict. The interim JM holds office for 30 days (extendable) and the creditors' meeting must be held within 30 days of lodgment of his declaration; it decides whether to place the company in JM. The s 95 moratorium applies on lodgment of the notice of appointment.

*Source note: INS3 s16; INS-CS1 p42; INS-SYL p8 (s 94); Cyril-INS §3.3; IRDA s 94(1)–(7) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-2-43 — Moratoria (ss 95, 96); contractual set-off
*Keywords: s 95 IRDA moratorium on application, no winding up resolution, no enforcement of charge or security, no proceedings execution legal process without leave, s 96(4) expanded moratorium after order, re-entry or forfeiture under lease, appointment of receiver or manager, receiver vacates, winding up application dismissed, s 96(5) contractual set-off unaffected, Electro Magnetic v DBS, Altus Technology v OCBC, carve-outs*

**End point:** Once a JM application is made: (a) no resolution may be passed to wind up the company; (b) no steps may be taken to enforce any charge or security over its property (or repossess goods under hire-purchase or similar agreements); and (c) no other proceedings, execution or legal process may be commenced or continued, except with leave of Court (s 95). After the JM order, an expanded moratorium applies until discharge (s 96(4)): it also bars re-entry or forfeiture under leases and the appointment of receivers or managers; any receiver and manager must vacate and any winding-up application is dismissed. Contractual rights of set-off are not affected (s 96(5); *Electro Magnetic (S) Ltd v DBS*; *Altus Technology v OCBC*).

*Source note: INS3 s18–20; INS-CS1 p44–45; INS-SYL p8; RL-Sec p6–7.*

### ccp-1-2-44 — The judicial manager: powers, proposals, duration, discharge
*Keywords: judicial manager powers, s 99 IRDA, First Schedule, take possession of records, secure premises, cannot deal with trust property, no payment of pre-JM debts except s 99(6), s 107 statement of proposals 90 days, 180 days, extension by court, s 111(3) creditors' approval majority in number and value, s 112 discharge purpose achieved or cannot be achieved, s 115 unfairly prejudicial, one quarter in value, s 101 rescue financing*

**End point:** The judicial manager has the powers in s 99 and the First Schedule; his immediate steps are to take possession of records, secure premises, review operations and meet key employees. He cannot deal with property held on trust, or pay debts incurred before JM except as allowed by s 99(6). He must send creditors a statement of proposals for achieving the purposes within 90 days of the order (s 107). The order lasts 180 days unless discharged, extendable by the Court, or by creditors' approval (majority in number and value) without a court order (s 111(3)). He must apply for discharge if the purposes have been achieved or cannot be achieved (s 112). A creditor or member may seek relief if he manages the company in a way unfairly prejudicial to creditors or members generally, some of them, or a creditor holding at least a quarter in value of the debts (s 115). He may also apply for rescue financing priority (s 101, see [2-62](#ccp-1-2-62)).

*Source note: INS3 s21–26; INS-CS1 p46–49; INS-SYL p8–9 (ss 99–118).*

### ccp-1-2-45 — Unfair preference: elements (s 225)
*Keywords: unfair preference, undue preference, s 225 IRDA, s 362 bankruptcy, creditor surety or guarantor, better position in liquidation, influenced by desire to prefer, insolvent at time or as a result, repaying director's loan, personal guarantee*

**End point:** A transaction is an unfair preference if: (a) the person preferred is a creditor, or a surety or guarantor for the company's debts; (b) the company does (or suffers) something that puts that person in a better position in the event of its liquidation than he would otherwise have been in; (c) the company was influenced in deciding to do so by a desire to prefer him; and (d) the company was insolvent at the time or became insolvent as a result (ss 225–226; for individuals ss 362–363). Repaying a debt guaranteed by a director preferentially benefits the director as surety.

*Source note: INS4 s5–6; INS-CS1 p24; INS-SYL p9 (ss 224–227; Progen); Cyril-INS §4.1; RL-Sec p9.*

### ccp-1-2-46 — Relevant time; connected persons and associates
*Keywords: relevant time, s 226 IRDA, 1 year unrelated, 2 years connected person, commencement of winding up or judicial management, making of bankruptcy application s 363, connected person, associate, s 217 IRDA, director, associate of director, associate of company, relative, spouse, brother, sister, uncle, aunt, nephew, niece*

**End point:** A preference is caught if given within 1 year before the commencement of the winding up or JM (or the making of the bankruptcy application) for an unconnected person, or 2 years for a person connected with the company (s 226; s 363), and the company must have been insolvent at the time or become so in consequence. Connected persons (s 217) include a director of the company, an associate of a director, and an associate of the company (e.g. a related company); for individuals, associates include a spouse and relatives such as siblings, uncles, aunts, nephews and nieces (ss 217, 364).

*Source note: INS4 s7–8; INS-CS1 p25–26; Cyril-INS §4.1; IRDA s 226(1)–(3) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026).*

### ccp-1-2-47 — Desire to prefer: test, presumption, defence
*Keywords: desire to prefer, influenced by, not dominant intention, subjective state of mind, direct evidence or inference, one of the factors, presumption for connected persons s 225(5), Liquidators of Progen Engineering v Progen Holdings, burden on payee, DBS Bank v Tam Chee Chong, proper commercial consideration, genuine belief, Rabobank v Jurong Technologies, creditor pressure*

**End point:** The test is not whether there was a dominant intention to prefer, but whether the company's decision was influenced by a desire to prefer the creditor — a subjective state of mind, shown by direct evidence or inferred from the circumstances; it need only be one of the factors, not the sole or decisive one (*DBS Bank v Tam Chee Chong* [2011] 4 SLR 948). A transaction actuated by a proper commercial consideration is not a preference, and a genuine belief in such a consideration may suffice even if objectively unsustainable. Where the preferred person is connected with the company, the desire to prefer is presumed (s 225(5)), and the payee bears the burden of rebutting it (*Progen Engineering v Progen Holdings* [2010] 4 SLR 1089). Creditor pressure does not necessarily negate the desire (*Rabobank v Jurong Technologies* [2011] 4 SLR 977).

*Source note: INS4 s9–13; INS-CS1 p27–28; INS-SYL p9.*

### ccp-1-2-48 — Transaction at an undervalue (s 224)
*Keywords: transaction at an undervalue, s 224 IRDA, s 361 bankruptcy, gift, no consideration, consideration significantly less in money or money's worth, consideration of marriage, 3 years, s 226(1)(a), connected person presumption of insolvency s 226(3), transaction includes unilateral payment Mercator v Velstra, mistaken payment not a transaction Velstra v Dexia, valuation at time Buildspeed, defence good faith business purposes reasonable grounds benefit s 224(4), Rothstar v Leow Quek Shiong*

**End point:** A company enters into a transaction at an undervalue if it makes a gift or receives no consideration, or receives consideration significantly less in money or money's worth than the value it provides (s 224(3); for individuals also a transaction in consideration of marriage, s 361). The company must have been insolvent at the time or become so as a result; if the counterparty is connected, insolvency is presumed (s 226(3)). The look-back is 3 years before the commencement of the winding up or JM (s 226(1)(a); 3 years before the bankruptcy application, s 363). A "transaction" includes a unilateral payment or gift (*Mercator & Noordstar v Velstra*), but the company must have intended to transact with the recipient, so a mistaken payment is not one (*Velstra v Dexia Bank*); values are assessed as at the time of the transaction (*Buildspeed v Theme Corp*). Defence: no order if the company acted in good faith for the purposes of its business with reasonable grounds for believing the transaction would benefit it (s 224(4)).

*Source note: INS4 s14–20; INS-CS1 p29–31; INS-SYL p9–10 (s 225 heading as listed; Rothstar Group v Leow Quek Shiong [2022] SGCA 25 listed, not summarised); RL-Sec p8–9.*

### ccp-1-2-49 — Void dispositions after commencement (s 130; s 328)
*Keywords: void disposition, s 130 IRDA, after commencement of winding up by court, unless court otherwise orders, validation, s 328 IRDA disposition by bankrupt after bankruptcy application, Cheo Sharon Andriesz v Official Assignee, consent judgment in divorce, bankruptcy search, QCD Sdn Bhd v Wah Nam Plastic, disposition excludes beneficial owner obtaining its property*

**End point:** Any disposition of the company's property, transfer of shares or alteration in members' status after the commencement of a winding up by the Court (the application date) is void unless the Court orders otherwise (s 130). For individuals, dispositions by the bankrupt after the bankruptcy application are similarly restricted (s 328) — even a transfer under a consent order in divorce proceedings (*Cheo Sharon Andriesz v Official Assignee* [2013] 2 SLR 297), so litigants should do bankruptcy searches. "Disposition" does not include a beneficial owner obtaining its own property, or its proceeds, from the company when entitled to it (*QCD Sdn Bhd v Wah Nam Plastic* [1997] 1 SLR(R) 270).

*Source note: INS4 s21–23; INS-CS1 p32.*

### ccp-1-2-50 — Floating charges for no value (s 229)
*Keywords: s 229 IRDA, avoidance of floating charge, invalid except new value, fresh consideration, money paid, goods or services supplied, debt discharged or reduced, interest, 2 years connected person, 1 year unconnected person, unable to pay debts at the time, s 229(3), period between commencement of JM and entry into JM*

**End point:** A floating charge created at a "relevant time" before the commencement of winding up or JM is invalid except to the extent of the value of money paid, goods or services supplied, or debt discharged or reduced, at or after its creation, plus interest (s 229(1)). The relevant time is 2 years before commencement for a connected person and 1 year for anyone else, plus the interval between the JM application and the JM order (s 229(2)). For an unconnected person, the charge is caught only if the company was then unable to pay its debts or became so as a result (s 229(3)).

*Source note: INS4 s24–25; INS-CS1 p33–34; IRDA s 229(1)–(3) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); RL-Sec p2.*

### ccp-1-2-51 — Orders the Court may make (s 227)
*Keywords: s 227 IRDA, orders on undervalue or preference, restore position, vest property, release or discharge security, repayment, revive guarantee, third parties good faith*

**End point:** On an application by the liquidator or judicial manager, the Court may make such order as it thinks fit to restore the position to what it would have been had the company not entered into the transaction at an undervalue or given the preference (ss 224(2), 225(2)); s 227 sets out examples, such as requiring property to be re-vested in the company, releasing or discharging security, ordering payment to the officeholder, and reviving guarantees, while protecting third parties who acquired interests in good faith and for value.

*Source note: INS-SYL p9 ("Extent of orders that can be granted by Court: Section 227"); INS4 s2 (ss 224–227); RL-Sec p8 ("wide court orders"). The s 227 examples are summarised from IRDA-2018 s 227.*

### ccp-1-2-52 — Fraudulent trading (s 238)
*Keywords: fraudulent trading, s 238 IRDA, intent to defraud creditors, fraudulent purpose, knowingly party, personal liability, winding up and judicial management, Tang Yoke Kheng v Lek Benedict, actual dishonesty, real moral blame*

**End point:** If in a winding up or JM it appears that the company's business was carried on with intent to defraud creditors (of the company or any other person) or for any fraudulent purpose, the Court may declare any person who was knowingly a party to carrying it on in that manner personally liable for the company's debts (s 238). "Defraud" and "fraudulent purpose" connote actual dishonesty involving real moral blame by current notions of fair trading (*Tang Yoke Kheng v Lek Benedict* [2004] 4 SLR(R) 788).

*Source note: INS4 s4, s26–27; INS-SYL p10; Cyril-INS §4.3.*

### ccp-1-2-53 — Wrongful trading (s 239)
*Keywords: wrongful trading, s 239 IRDA, company insolvent incurs debts without reasonable prospect of meeting them in full, becomes insolvent, personal liability, party to trading, no criminal conviction needed, honesty defence s 239(2), who can apply s 239(5)*

**End point:** A company trades wrongfully if, when insolvent, it incurs debts or liabilities without reasonable prospect of meeting them in full, or incurs debts that result in its becoming insolvent. In a winding up or JM, any person knowingly party to it may be declared personally liable for the company's debts (s 239), on an application by those listed in s 239(5) (e.g. the liquidator, judicial manager or a creditor); no criminal conviction is required. An officer may be relieved if he acted honestly (s 239(2)).

*Source note: INS4 s4, s28–29; INS-SYL p10; Cyril-INS §4.4.*

### ccp-1-2-54 — What a scheme is; which companies; key steps
*Keywords: scheme of arrangement, compromise or arrangement, s 210 Companies Act, ss 63-72 IRDA, binding contract, debtor in possession, company liable to be wound up, substantial connection, foreign company, key steps, leave to convene, meeting, sanction, lodgement*

**End point:** A scheme is a compromise or arrangement between a company and its creditors (or a class of them) that becomes binding on all of them once approved by the statutory majorities and sanctioned by the Court; its terms are essentially contractual, and management stays in control (debtor in possession). It is available to any corporation liable to be wound up under the IRDA, including a foreign company with a substantial connection with Singapore (s 63(3) IRDA; s 210(11) CA). Key steps: (1) application to Court for leave to convene creditors' meeting(s) (s 210(1) CA); (2) meetings and vote by the requisite majority (s 210(3AB)); (3) Court sanction (s 210(4)); (4) lodgement of the order with the Registrar (s 210(5)). A moratorium may be sought at any time before sanction (s 64 IRDA).

*Source note: INS5 s2–3, s6–7; INS-CS1 p51–54; INS-SYL p10 (s 210 CA; RBS v TT International); Cyril-INS §5.*

### ccp-1-2-55 — Moratoria: s 64 automatic interim, court-ordered, s 65 related companies
*Keywords: moratorium, s 64 IRDA, automatic interim moratorium 30 days, on filing of application, s 64(14), court-ordered moratorium, restrain winding up, receivers, proceedings, execution, enforcement of security, repossession, re-entry, s 64(4) evidence of creditor support, s 64(5) acts outside Singapore, extraterritorial, s 65 subsidiary holding company ultimate holding company, prescribed set-off netting not affected, Re IM Skaugen, s 211B Companies Act*

**End point:** A company that proposes or intends to propose a scheme may apply to Court for moratorium orders restraining winding-up resolutions, the appointment of receivers, proceedings, execution, enforcement of security, repossession of goods and re-entry (s 64(1)). On filing, an automatic interim moratorium of 30 days arises (s 64(14)) until the Court decides the application, and the Court may order a longer moratorium. The application must include, e.g., evidence of creditor support and, if no scheme has yet been proposed, a brief description of it with sufficient particulars (s 64(4); *Re IM Skaugen* [2019] 3 SLR 979). The moratorium can restrain acts outside Singapore by creditors within the Court's jurisdiction (s 64(5)), and can be extended to the company's subsidiary, holding or ultimate holding company (s 65). Prescribed set-off and netting arrangements are not affected. The automatic moratorium does not arise if the company made an earlier s 64(1) application attracting it within the previous 12 months (s 64(9)).

*Source note: INS5 s4–5; INS-CS1 p52, p55, p60; IRDA s 64(1)–(2), (9), (14) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); RL-Sec p7.*

### ccp-1-2-56 — Application for leave to convene meetings (s 210(1) CA)
*Keywords: leave to convene creditors' meeting, s 210(1) Companies Act, applicants company creditor member liquidator, ex parte, affidavit, The Royal Bank of Scotland v TT International, not forum for merits or fairness, realistic prospect of approval, court should not act in vain, Re Ng Huat Foundations, disclose all material information, classification brought to court's attention, Pathfinder Strategic Credit v Empire Capital Resources*

**End point:** The company, a creditor, a member, or (in winding up) the liquidator may apply ex parte, supported by affidavit, for leave to convene the scheme meeting(s) (s 210(1)–(2) CA). At this stage the Court mainly decides whether a meeting should be called and how it should be conducted; it is not the forum for the merits and fairness of the scheme (*RBS v TT International* [2012] 2 SLR 213). It will not grant leave if there is no realistic prospect of the scheme receiving the requisite approval. The company must unreservedly disclose all material information and bring any classification issues to the Court's attention (*TT International*; *Pathfinder Strategic Credit v Empire Capital Resources* [2019] 2 SLR 77).

*Source note: INS5 s8–10, s12; INS-CS1 p56; INS-SYL p10–11. INS5 s8 refers to "O.38 r.2(1) ... originating summons" (pre-2022 rules); the form follows the current Rules of Court.*

### ccp-1-2-57 — Notice and explanatory statement (s 211 CA)
*Keywords: notice of meeting, explanatory statement, s 211(1)(a) Companies Act, effect of scheme, material interests of directors, full and fair disclosure, advertised notice s 211(1)(b), proofs of debt, proxies, s 68 IRDA adjudication*

**End point:** After leave, a notice of the meeting with an explanatory statement must be sent to creditors (and advertised notices must state where it can be obtained) (s 211(1) CA). The statement must explain the effect of the scheme, state any material interests of the directors (and how the scheme affects them differently from others), and give full and fair disclosure of the information reasonably necessary for creditors to decide how to vote. Proofs of debt are filed and adjudicated for voting (s 68 IRDA).

*Source note: INS5 s11; INS-CS1 p54, p57; INS-SYL p11.*

### ccp-1-2-58 — Classification of creditors
*Keywords: classification of creditors, dissimilarity principle, rights so dissimilar cannot sensibly consult together with a view to common interest, TT International, separate classes, secured creditors, preferential creditors paid in full, unsecured creditors, subordinated creditors, related creditors, contingent creditors, purpose of classification*

**End point:** Creditors must be divided into separate classes if their rights are so dissimilar that they cannot sensibly consult together with a view to their common interest (the dissimilarity principle, *TT International*). The purpose is that creditors with similar rights are grouped so they can consult together with a view to their common interest — not ease of counting or explaining (SQ p21 Q8, key D). Possible classes: secured creditors; creditors with priority or paid in full v those paid in part; unsecured creditors; subordinated creditors; related and contingent creditors may raise class (or vote-discounting) issues.

*Source note: INS5 s12–14; INS-SYL p11; SQ p21 Q8 and p114 (key D).*

### ccp-1-2-59 — Voting: statutory majority (s 210(3AB) CA)
*Keywords: statutory majority, s 210(3AB) Companies Act, majority in number, three-fourths in value, 75%, present and voting, in person or by proxy, each class, adjournment same majority*

**End point:** The scheme must be approved by a majority in number representing three-fourths in value of the creditors, or of each class of creditors, present and voting (in person or by proxy) at the meeting (s 210(3AB) CA). (The Court may order a different number requirement.)

*Source note: INS5 s6, s15; INS-CS1 p53, p57; INS-SYL p11.*

### ccp-1-2-60 — Cram-down (s 70) and pre-pack schemes (s 71)
*Keywords: cram down, s 70 IRDA, dissenting class, at least one approving class, majority in number and three-quarters in value overall, no unfair discrimination between classes, fair and equitable to each dissenting class, pre-packaged scheme, pre-pack, s 71 IRDA, approval without meeting, would have been approved had meeting been summoned, re-vote s 69*

**End point:** The Court may sanction a scheme despite a dissenting class ("cram-down", s 70) if: at least one class approved it; the creditors meant to be bound, taken as a whole, approved it by a majority in number and three-quarters in value; it does not discriminate unfairly between two or more classes; and it is fair and equitable to each dissenting class. Under s 71 the Court may approve a scheme without any creditors' meeting (a "pre-pack") if it is satisfied the conditions for approval would have been met had a meeting been summoned.

*Source note: INS5 s16–17; INS-CS1 p53, p58; INS-SYL p11 (ss 68–71, listed as extra readings).*

### ccp-1-2-61 — Court sanction; effect on creditors and third parties
*Keywords: court approval of scheme, s 210(4), statutory provisions complied with, fairly representative, no coercion of minority, intelligent and honest man of business would reasonably approve, Wah Yuen Electrical, Re Econ Corp, Re Horizon Knowledge Solutions, transparency, binding on dissenting creditors, Oriental Insurance v Reliance National, third-party releases, Daewoo v CEL Tractors, Pathfinder, lodgement with Registrar s 210(5), effective, termination events*

**End point:** Before sanctioning, the Court must be satisfied that (1) the statutory provisions have been complied with; (2) those who attended were fairly representative of the class and the majority did not coerce the minority; and (3) the scheme is one an intelligent and honest man of business, a member of the class acting in his own interest, would reasonably approve. It retains a discretion despite the vote, and will refuse sanction for lack of transparency (e.g. *Wah Yuen Electrical v Singapore Cables* [2003] 3 SLR(R) 629; *Re Econ Corp*; *Re Horizon Knowledge Solutions*). Once sanctioned, the scheme binds all creditors of the classes, including dissenters (*Oriental Insurance v Reliance National Asia Re* [2008] 3 SLR(R) 121), and may include terms affecting third parties' rights (*Daewoo v CEL Tractors*; *Pathfinder*). The order takes effect when a copy is lodged with the Registrar (or from an earlier date the Court specifies) (s 210(5)). The scheme document sets its own termination events.

*Source note: INS5 s18–22; INS-CS1 p58–59; INS-SYL p12.*

## 2.XII Rescue financing and ipso facto clauses

- **New money lender wants priority over existing creditors in a scheme / JM — super priority?** ss 67, 101 → [2-62](#ccp-1-2-62)
- **Supplier / lessor terminates because the company applied for a scheme moratorium or JM** → ipso facto restriction s 440 → [2-63](#ccp-1-2-63)

### ccp-1-2-62 — Rescue financing and super priority (ss 67, 101)
*Keywords: rescue financing, super priority, s 67 IRDA scheme, s 101 IRDA judicial management, costs and expenses of winding up s 203(1)(b), priority over all preferential debts, security on unencumbered property, subordinate security, priming lien, same or higher priority than existing security, adequate protection, necessary for survival as going concern, more advantageous realisation, would not have obtained financing otherwise, notice to creditors, lodge order 14 days, protection from avoidance*

**End point:** Where a company has applied for a scheme (s 210(1) CA) or a moratorium (s 64), the Court may on the company's application order that debt from rescue financing (s 67) — and in JM, on the judicial manager's application (s 101) — be (a) treated as part of the costs and expenses of the winding up if the company is wound up; (b) given priority over all preferential debts and other unsecured debts (super priority), if the financing could not otherwise be obtained; (c) secured on unencumbered property, or by a subordinate security interest, if the financing could not otherwise be obtained; or (d) secured with the same or higher priority than an existing security interest, if it could not otherwise be obtained and the existing secured creditor is adequately protected. "Rescue financing" is financing necessary for the survival of the company (or its undertaking) as a going concern, or to achieve a more advantageous realisation of its assets than on a winding up.

> **s 67(1) IRDA (s 101(1) is in the same terms for JM)**
> - (a) treat the rescue financing debt as part of the costs and expenses of the winding up (s 203(1)(b));
> - (b) priority over all preferential debts in s 203(1)(a)–(i) and all other unsecured debts — if the company would not otherwise have obtained the financing;
> - (c) secured by (i) a security interest on unencumbered property or (ii) a subordinate security interest on encumbered property — if it would not otherwise have obtained the financing;
> - (d) secured by a security interest of the same or higher priority than an existing one — if it would not otherwise have obtained the financing and there is adequate protection for the existing holder (cash payments, replacement security, or the "indubitable equivalent", s 67(6)).
> - Super priority debts rank equally among themselves and, if needed, are paid out of floating-charge assets ahead of the floating charge holder (s 67(3)–(4)); orders are protected from the avoidance provisions (s 67(7)); the order is lodged with the Registrar within 14 days (s 67(8)).

> - Differences in s 101 (JM): any creditor may oppose the application (s 101(3)); "rescue financing" has a third limb — financing necessary for the Court's approval of a scheme (s 101(10)); the ranking subsections are s 101(4)–(5) and lodgement is s 101(9).

*Source note: INS-LP p1 (objective 4: rescue financing); IRDA ss 67, 101 (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026; unchanged from IRDA-2018). The slides and contact session do not cover rescue financing; the block is taken from the statute.*

### ccp-1-2-63 — Ipso facto clauses (s 440)
*Keywords: ipso facto clause, s 440 IRDA, terminate or amend agreement, accelerated payment, forfeiture, by reason only that proceedings commenced or company insolvent, simplified debt restructuring programme, scheme application, s 64 s 65 moratorium, judicial management application, interim judicial manager, not winding up, cash for new supplies, no further advance, contrary provision no effect, significant financial hardship, excluded contracts, eligible financial contracts, ship charters, aircraft, prescribed companies, Section 440 Order 2020, Regulations 2020*

**End point:** After proceedings are commenced and before they conclude, no person may terminate or amend, or claim accelerated payment or forfeiture under, an agreement with the company (including a security agreement), or terminate or modify any right or obligation under it, by reason only that the proceedings have commenced, the company is insolvent, or (from 29 Jan 2026) it has entered the simplified debt restructuring programme (s 440(1)). "Proceedings" are restructuring proceedings: a scheme application under s 210(1) CA or s 71, a moratorium application under s 64 or 65, a JM application, or lodgment of notice of an interim JM's appointment — not winding up. The counterparty may still require cash payment for post-commencement supplies, and need not advance further money or credit (s 440(2)); contrary contract terms have no effect (s 440(3)); the Court may disapply the section for significant financial hardship (s 440(4)). Excluded: prescribed eligible financial contracts, government licences, contracts affecting national interest, commercial ship charters, aircraft-equipment agreements, prescribed treaty contracts (s 440(5)), and prescribed companies (Section 440 Order 2020).

*Source note: INS-CS1 p62 (s 440; Prescribed Companies Order 2020; Prescribed Contracts Regulations 2020); INS-LP p1; IRDA-2018 s 440(1)–(6); IRDA s 440(1) as amended by Act 3 of 2025 wef 29 Jan 2026 (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026); RL-Sec p7 (a payment default can still be acted on).*

## 2.XIII Choosing a procedure

- **Which procedure fits? Liquidation v scheme v JM — who controls, moratorium, outcome** → [2-64](#ccp-1-2-64)

### ccp-1-2-64 — Winding up v scheme of arrangement v judicial management
*Keywords: comparison, winding up, liquidation, scheme of arrangement, judicial management, debtor in possession, management replaced, moratorium, outcome, going concern, terminal, creditor approval, court involvement, advantages and disadvantages*

**End point:** Winding up is terminal: a liquidator replaces the directors, realises the assets, distributes pari passu by priority and dissolves the company; there is a statutory stay once an order is made. A scheme is a rescue tool in which management stays in control (debtor in possession); the moratorium is by application (automatic 30 days under s 64) and the scheme binds creditors once approved by a majority in number and 75% in value of each class (or crammed down) and sanctioned by the Court; it needs creditor support and court time. JM is also a rescue tool, but the directors are replaced by an independent judicial manager with all their powers; a statutory moratorium arises automatically on application (s 95) and expands on the order (s 96); it lasts 180 days (extendable) and is aimed at the s 89 purposes; a floating charge holder can block it unless the public interest requires otherwise.

> **Comparison (from the course materials)**
> - Who controls: winding up — liquidator; scheme — existing management; JM — judicial manager.
> - Moratorium: winding up — s 129 discretionary stay on application, s 133 automatic on order; scheme — s 64 automatic 30 days on application, then by court order; JM — s 95 automatic on application, s 96 expanded on order.
> - Creditor approval: winding up — none needed for an order; scheme — majority in number + 75% value per class (s 210(3AB)), cram-down (s 70); JM — creditors approve the JM's proposals (s 107) and may extend it (s 111(3)).
> - Rescue financing priority: scheme — s 67; JM — s 101.
> - Outcome: winding up — dissolution; scheme — binding compromise, company continues; JM — survival, scheme, or better realisation than winding up.

*Source note: INS-LP p1 (objective 2: comparison); INS1 s20–21; INS3 s3–5, s18–21; INS5 s4–6; INS-CS1 p17, p36–38, p51–53. The comparison is assembled from these slides; the slides give no single table.*

## 2.XIV Cross-border insolvency

- **Foreign liquidator wants help in Singapore — Model Law recognition; COMI; main or non-main; what relief?** → [2-65](#ccp-1-2-65)
- **Can a foreign company be wound up in Singapore? Substantial connection** (SQ p20 Q6, key A) → [2-66](#ccp-1-2-66)
- **Singapore liquidator of a foreign company: pay local creditors first? Ring-fencing** → [2-67](#ccp-1-2-67)
- **Foreign company restructuring through a Singapore scheme or JM** → [2-68](#ccp-1-2-68)

### ccp-1-2-65 — UNCITRAL Model Law: recognition, COMI, main v non-main, relief
*Keywords: UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency, Third Schedule IRDA, in force 23 May 2017, no reciprocity, access, recognition, relief, cooperation, centre of main interests, COMI, registered office presumption Article 16(3), Re Zetta Jet, Re Rooftop Group, foreign main proceeding automatic relief, stay of proceedings and execution, no transfer of assets, Article 20(3) security set-off unaffected, foreign non-main proceeding discretionary, Article 22 adequate protection, Re PT Garuda, Re Tantleff*

**End point:** The Model Law (IRDA Third Schedule, in force 23 May 2017, without any reciprocity requirement) gives foreign insolvency officeholders access to the Singapore courts, recognition of foreign proceedings, relief to assist them, and cooperation between courts and officeholders. The extent of relief depends on whether the foreign proceeding is in the debtor's centre of main interests (COMI); the registered office is presumed to be the COMI unless rebutted (Art 16(3); *Re Zetta Jet* [2019] SGHC 53). Recognition of a foreign main proceeding brings automatic relief (stay of proceedings and execution, suspension of the right to transfer assets), but this does not affect the enforcement of security, repossession under hire-purchase agreements, or set-off (Art 20(3)). Relief for a foreign non-main proceeding is at the Court's discretion. In either case further relief (e.g. examination of witnesses, evidence, information about assets) may be sought, and the Court must be satisfied that creditors' (including secured creditors') and other interested persons' interests are adequately protected (Art 22).

*Source note: INS6 s2–11; INS-CS1 p62; INS-SYL p12–13.*

### ccp-1-2-66 — Winding up foreign companies: substantial connection (s 246)
*Keywords: winding up foreign company, unregistered company, s 246(1)(d) IRDA, substantial connection with Singapore, s 246(3) factors, COMI in Singapore, carrying on business, place of business, registered foreign company, substantial assets, Singapore governing law, submission to jurisdiction, moratorium available*

**End point:** A foreign company may be wound up in Singapore only if it has a substantial connection with Singapore (s 246(1)(d)) — not merely because it has property here, and it is not barred altogether (SQ p20 Q6, key A). Factors (s 246(3)): Singapore is its COMI; it carries on business or has a place of business in Singapore; it is a foreign company registered under the Companies Act; it has substantial assets in Singapore; it chose Singapore law to govern a loan or other transaction or the resolution of related disputes; it submitted to the Singapore courts' jurisdiction for such disputes.

> **s 246(3) IRDA — substantial connection factors**
> - (a) Singapore is the centre of main interests of the company;
> - (b) the company is carrying on business in Singapore or has a place of business in Singapore;
> - (c) the company is a foreign company registered under the Companies Act;
> - (d) the company has substantial assets in Singapore;
> - (e) the company has chosen Singapore law to govern a loan or other transaction, or the resolution of disputes arising from it;
> - (f) the company has submitted to the jurisdiction of the Singapore courts for the resolution of such disputes.

*Source note: INS6 s12–13; INS-SYL p12; IRDA s 246(1)(d), (3) (SSO, current at 11 Nov 2026; the factors are non-exhaustive); SQ p20 Q6 and p114 (key A).*

### ccp-1-2-67 — Liquidating a foreign company's Singapore assets; ring-fencing
*Keywords: foreign company wound up in place of incorporation, Singapore liquidator, transmit assets to principal liquidation, relevant companies, pay Singapore debts first, ring-fencing, s 250(3)(c) IRDA*

**End point:** Where a foreign company being wound up in its place of incorporation is also wound up in Singapore, the question is whether and how its Singapore assets go to the principal liquidation. There is generally no ring-fencing of Singapore assets for local creditors, except for certain types of foreign companies ("relevant companies"), whose Singapore liquidator must first pay debts incurred in Singapore before transmitting assets abroad (s 250(3)(c)).

*Source note: INS6 s14.*

### ccp-1-2-68 — Foreign companies using Singapore schemes and judicial management
*Keywords: Model Law does not restructure debts, foreign company scheme of arrangement Singapore, liable to be wound up, s 63(3) IRDA, s 210(11) CA, judicial management foreign company, s 88 definition company, Deutsche Bank v Asia Pulp & Paper, control of foreign subsidiaries*

**End point:** The Model Law does not itself restructure a company's debts; a foreign company must start a Singapore scheme or JM to do so. It can do either only if it is liable to be wound up in Singapore, i.e. has a substantial connection (s 63(3) IRDA; s 210(11) CA; s 88 IRDA "company"). The Court may weigh other factors too: in *Deutsche Bank v Asia Pulp & Paper* [2003] 2 SLR(R) 320 a JM application was dismissed partly because it was uncertain the judicial managers could control key foreign subsidiaries.

*Source note: INS6 s15–18; INS-SYL p8.*

## 3.I Admiralty jurisdiction (s 3 HCAJA)

### ccp-1-3-1 — Why use admiralty jurisdiction: action in rem, arrest for security
*Keywords: admiralty jurisdiction, High Court (Admiralty Jurisdiction) Act, HCAJA, action in rem, action in personam, ship arrest, security before judgment, detain ship, s 4(1), General Division of the High Court*

**End point:** Admiralty jurisdiction lets a claimant obtain security for a maritime claim before judgment, by bringing an action in rem and arresting the ship (or obtaining security in place of arrest). An arrested ship is detained and cannot sail or be dealt with until security is provided, the claim is settled or the ship is sold. The jurisdiction may always be invoked in personam (s 4(1)); the in rem route depends on the claim falling within s 3(1) and satisfying s 4(2), (3) or (4).

## 3.II Liens and priorities

- **Statutory lien: when does it arise? Does it survive a sale of the ship? Time bar?** → arises on issuing the in rem claim; survives sale after that → [3-3](#ccp-1-3-3)
- **Maritime lien: crew wages, salvage, collision damage — can the crew arrest after the ship was sold?** Yes, the lien travels with the ship (SQ p44 Q2, key D) → [3-4](#ccp-1-3-4)
- **Unpaid ship repairer still holds the ship** → possessory lien → [3-5](#ccp-1-3-5)
- **Ship sold; proceeds too small — who is paid first? Does the arresting party rank first?** Sheriff's expenses, arrest costs, maritime liens, mortgage, statutory liens → [3-6](#ccp-1-3-6)

### ccp-1-3-3 — Statutory liens
*Keywords: statutory lien, created by issuing originating claim in rem, s 3 and s 4 HCAJA satisfied, effect, time bar, change of ownership after action brought, The Monica S, valid sale before action extinguishes*

**End point:** A statutory lien arises when a claim within s 3(1) can be brought in rem under s 4 and the claimant issues the originating claim in rem. Once the action is brought, the claim is protected against a later change of ownership and the time bar stops running. But a valid change of ownership before the action is brought extinguishes the right to proceed in rem against that ship under s 4(4) (*The Monica S* [1967] 2 Lloyd's Rep 113).

*Source note: ADM1 s4; Cel-ADM (lecture 1 notes: statutory lien; The Monica S); Cyril-ADM §2, §3A.*

### ccp-1-3-4 — Maritime liens
*Keywords: maritime lien, salvage, collision damage, damage done by a ship, master's and crew wages, arises automatically by operation of law, no need to issue writ, travels with the ship, survives change of ownership, still subject to time bar, s 4(3) HCAJA, previous master and crew, BLUES Ltd, GUNNERS Ltd*

**End point:** A maritime lien arises automatically, by operation of law, when the event occurs — the common ones are salvage, collision damage (damage done by a ship) and master's and crew's wages. It attaches to the ship and survives a change of ownership, so the claimant can arrest the ship under s 4(3) even after it has been sold to a new owner who is not personally liable. So the previous master and crew of a sold ship can arrest it for unpaid wages regardless of whether the old owner still owns it (SQ p44 Q2, key D). The claimant must still issue an in rem claim within the time bar.

*Source note: ADM1 s4; ADM2 s10; Cyril-ADM §2 (Type 2); Cel-ADM (lecture 1); SQ p43–44 Q2 and p116 (key D). Cyril-ADM §3.1 also lists "port and canal dues" as giving a maritime lien; the course slides do not, and the block does not rely on it.*

### ccp-1-3-5 — Possessory (repairer's) lien
*Keywords: possessory lien, repairer's lien, ship repairer, unpaid repair dues, retain possession until paid, shipyard, HOODOO Yard*

**End point:** A ship repairer who has repaired or improved a ship and not been paid has a possessory lien: it may keep possession of the ship until paid. It depends on keeping possession. A repairer can also bring an in rem claim under s 3(1)(m) read with s 4(4).

*Source note: ADM1 s4; ADM2 s10; Cyril-ADM §2 (Type 3); SQ p45 (HOODOO Yard facts).*

### ccp-1-3-6 — Priorities of competing claims
*Keywords: priorities, sale proceeds, insufficient, Sheriff's expenses, Sheriff's commission, arresting party's costs of arrest and sale, maritime liens, possessory liens, mortgages, statutory liens, arresting first gives no priority, Omar Bank, Bruno Bunkers, determination of priorities*

**End point:** When a ship is sold and the proceeds do not cover all the claims, the Court determines priorities. The Sheriff's commission and expenses come first; the arresting party's costs of the arrest, preservation and sale (incurred for the benefit of all claimants) rank next. Then the claims rank by their nature: maritime liens, then possessory liens, then mortgages, then statutory liens (other in rem claims), which share pari passu among themselves. Being the first to arrest gives no priority for the claim itself, so a mortgagee is paid ahead of a bunker supplier who arrested the ship.

> **Order of priority (course materials)**
> - Sheriff's commission and expenses (ADM-Guide para 74);
> - arresting party's costs of arrest and of obtaining the order for appraisement and sale (ADM-Guide para 76; standard sale order para 15);
> - maritime liens;
> - possessory liens;
> - mortgages;
> - statutory liens (other in rem claims).

*Source note: ADM-LP p1 (objective: priorities of competing in rem claims); ADM2 s10 ("Common Maritime Claims & Priorities", listing maritime lien, mortgage, possessory lien, statutory liens) and s23 (sample question: mortgagee v arresting bunker supplier; no answer given); ADM-Guide paras 74–76 and the standard sale order para 15; Cel-ADM (lecture 1: liens matter for priority). The full ranking (incl. possessory lien after maritime liens and before mortgages) is the generally stated order; the course slides list the categories without ranking them expressly.*

### ccp-1-3-7 — s 4(2): claims in s 3(1)(a), (b), (c), (r)
*Keywords: s 4(2) HCAJA, action in rem against the ship or property in question, possession or ownership, co-owners, mortgage or charge, forfeiture, droits, mortgagee, Omar Bank, intervene*

**End point:** For claims under s 3(1)(a) (possession or ownership), (b) (co-owners), (c) (mortgage or charge) and (r) (forfeiture, condemnation, droits of admiralty), an action in rem lies against the ship or property in question (s 4(2)), without the "relevant person" tests of s 4(4). So a ship mortgagee proceeds under s 3(1)(c) read with s 4(2).

> **s 4(2) HCAJA**
> - The admiralty jurisdiction may in the cases mentioned in section 3(1)(a), (b), (c) and (r) be invoked by an action in rem against the ship or property in question.

*Source note: HCAJA s 4(2); ADM2 s6, s18–19; ADM1 s5; Cyril-ADM §3.1.*

### ccp-1-3-8 — s 4(3): maritime lien or other charge
*Keywords: s 4(3) HCAJA, maritime lien, other charge, action in rem against that ship, crew wages, salvage, collision, regardless of ownership*

**End point:** Where there is a maritime lien or other charge on a ship for the amount claimed, an action in rem lies against that ship (s 4(3)), whoever owns it when the action is brought. This is the route for crew wages, salvage and collision damage claims (SQ p43 Q1, key C: s 3(1)(n) read with s 4(3)).

> **s 4(3) HCAJA**
> - In any case in which there is a maritime lien or other charge on any ship, aircraft or other property for the amount claimed, the admiralty jurisdiction may be invoked by an action in rem against that ship, aircraft or property.

*Source note: HCAJA s 4(3); ADM2 s6; ADM1 s5; Cyril-ADM §3.1; SQ p43 Q1 and p116 (key C).*

### ccp-1-3-9 — s 4(4): claims in s 3(1)(d)–(q); arresting the ship itself
*Keywords: s 4(4) HCAJA, claims s 3(1)(d) to (q), arises in connection with a ship, relevant person, person liable in personam, owner, charterer, in possession or control when cause of action arose, beneficial owner as respects all the shares, demise charterer, bareboat charterer, at the time when the action is brought, time charterer not enough, GO JING, ZAO ZIJING*

**End point:** For claims in s 3(1)(d)–(q), an action in rem may be brought (whether or not the claim carries a maritime lien) if (a) the claim arises in connection with a ship, and (b) the person who would be liable in personam (the "relevant person") was, when the cause of action arose, the owner or charterer of, or in possession or control of, the ship. The action may be brought against (c) that ship, if when the action is brought the relevant person is its beneficial owner as respects all the shares, or its demise (bareboat) charterer; or (d) any other ship of which the relevant person is then the beneficial owner as respects all the shares (s 4(4)). A time charterer's claim against the demise charterer satisfies (a)–(c) if the demise charterer is still demise charterer when the action is brought (SQ p46 Q7, key D); a repair yard's claim, where the owner requested the repairs and still owns the ship, satisfies (a)–(c) through the owner (SQ p47 Q10, key C).

> **s 4(4) HCAJA**
> - In the case of any claim mentioned in s 3(1)(d) to (q), where —
>   - (a) the claim arises in connection with a ship; and
>   - (b) the relevant person (who would be liable in personam) was, when the cause of action arose, the owner or charterer of, or in possession or in control of, the ship,
> - an action in rem may (whether or not the claim gives rise to a maritime lien on that ship) be brought against —
>   - (c) that ship, if at the time when the action is brought the relevant person is either the beneficial owner of that ship as respects all the shares in it or the charterer of that ship under a charter by demise; or
>   - (d) any other ship of which, at the time when the action is brought, the relevant person is the beneficial owner as respects all the shares in it.

*Source note: HCAJA s 4(4); ADM1 s5; ADM2 s7; Cyril-ADM §3.1; SQ p46 Q7, p47 Q10 and p116 (keys D, C).*

### ccp-1-3-10 — Sister-ship arrest (s 4(4)(d)); one-ship companies
*Keywords: sister ship, associated ship, s 4(4)(d) HCAJA, any other ship, beneficially owned by relevant person, all the shares, at the time action brought, demise chartered ship not a sister ship, managers' fleet, one-ship company, separate entity, The Skaw Prince, sham transfer, The Aventicum, The Saudi Prince, MV DEEVEESIE, MT VARDEE, MT MADDISSON*

**End point:** Under s 4(4)(d) the claimant may instead arrest any other ship of which the relevant person is the beneficial owner (as respects all the shares) when the action is brought. So a cargo owner whose claim lies against the carrier that issued the bills of lading can arrest another ship that carrier owns (SQ p45 Q5, key D). It cannot arrest a ship the relevant person only charters by demise, or a ship in the same managed fleet that belongs to a different one-ship company, or a ship of the manager (who is not the person liable). One-ship companies within a group are separate owners if the structure was in place before the cause of action accrued (*The Skaw Prince* [1994] 3 SLR(R) 379); transfers into new companies after the claim arose may be challenged as shams.

*Source note: HCAJA s 4(4)(d); ADM1 s5–6, s13–14, s20, s22 (mock questions on managers' fleet and bareboat-chartered ships; no answers given); Cel-ADM (lecture 1: Skaw Prince, Aventicum, Saudi Prince); SQ p45 Q5 and p116 (key D).*

### ccp-1-3-11 — The Bunga Melati 5 five-step test
*Keywords: The Bunga Melati 5 [2012] 4 SLR 546, five-step test, para 112, jurisdictional facts balance of probabilities, arguable case, claim arises in connection with a ship, identify person liable in personam, owner charterer possession control when cause of action arose, beneficial owner or demise charterer when action brought*

**End point:** To invoke s 4(4), the claimant must (1) prove on a balance of probabilities the jurisdictional facts of the s 3(1)(d)–(q) limb relied on, and show an arguable case that its claim is of the type that limb requires; (2) prove on a balance of probabilities that the claim arises in connection with a ship; (3) identify, without having to prove it, the person who would be liable in personam; (4) prove on a balance of probabilities that that person was, when the cause of action arose, the owner or charterer of, or in possession or control of, the ship; and (5) prove on a balance of probabilities that that person was, when the action was brought, the beneficial owner (all shares) or demise charterer of the offending ship, or the beneficial owner of the sister ship (*The Bunga Melati 5* [2012] 4 SLR 546 at [112]).

*Source note: ADM1 s7–8; ADM2 s8–9 (which cites [2012] 4 SLR 346; the Learning Plan reading list gives 546, which is followed); ADM-LP p4; Cyril-ADM §3.1.*

### ccp-1-3-12 — Change of ownership or end of a demise charter before the action
*Keywords: change of ownership before action brought, The Monica S, extinguishes right in rem, sold ship renamed, maritime lien survives, demise charter terminated, alleged repudiatory breach, time charterer YOUNG GOH, MT QUANZHEN, relevant person at time action brought*

**End point:** For a s 4(4) claim, the relevant person must own (or demise charter) the ship when the action is brought; a valid sale before the in rem claim is issued defeats an action against that ship (*The Monica S*), though the claimant may arrest a sister ship of the person liable, and a claim with a maritime lien survives the sale (see [3-4](#ccp-1-3-4)). In the Sample Question where the demise charterer had purported to terminate the demise charter for the owner's alleged repudiatory breach just before the time charterer's action, the key treats the time charterer as still able to invoke the jurisdiction against the demise charterer's ship (SQ p46 Q8, key B; Q7, key D) — i.e. an alleged, unestablished termination does not by itself end the demise charter for s 4(4)(c).

*Source note: HCAJA s 4(4)(c); ADM1 s6 (mock question on sale and renaming); Cel-ADM (lecture 1: The Monica S); Cyril-ADM §3A; SQ p45–46 Q7–Q8 and p116 (keys D, B). The key gives no reasons; the explanation for Q8 is inferred from the facts ("alleged repudiatory breach") and is not stated in the course materials.*

- **What must be disclosed to the court at the ex parte hearing? Arbitration clause; prior arrest; doubts about who is liable** → [3-14](#ccp-1-3-14)
- **Preparations: ownership searches, ship tracking, list of arrested vessels; caveat against arrest** → [3-15](#ccp-1-3-15)

### ccp-1-3-13 — Strategic considerations before an arrest
*Keywords: one ship one arrest, The Banco, abuse of process, The Blue Fruit, security up to claim or value of ship whichever lower, The Charlotte, valuation, S&P brokers, mortgage, cargo on board, discharge costs, port dues, Sheriff's expenses, solicitor's undertaking, arrest for foreign arbitration, ss 6 7 12 IAA, ICL Raja Mahendra, Vasiliy Golovnin, alternatives, Mareva injunction, associated ship, service without arrest*

**End point:** A claimant may arrest only one ship for each cause of action (*The Banco*), so it must choose a ship whose value covers its claim; security is limited to the claim (plus interest and costs) or the value of the arrested ship, whichever is lower (*The Charlotte*). Before arresting it should weigh the ship's value (valuations, existing mortgages), the costs of a long arrest — port dues and Sheriff's expenses, discharge of cargo on board — which its solicitors' undertaking to the Sheriff may have to meet if the sale proceeds are insufficient, and its exposure to damages for wrongful arrest. An arrest may be used to obtain security for a foreign arbitration (ss 6, 7, 12 IAA; *ICL Raja Mahendra*; *Vasiliy Golovnin*). Alternatives include a Mareva injunction or serving the in rem claim without arresting.

*Source note: Cel-ADM (lecture 2: strategic considerations); ADM-Guide paras 4–10; Cyril-ADM §3A, §4.1.*

### ccp-1-3-14 — Duty of full and frank disclosure
*Keywords: full and frank disclosure, ex parte application, material facts, knock-out blow, The Eagle Prestige, defences on the merits not generally required, abuse of process, The Vasiliy Golovnin prior arrest or action for same claim, The Xin Chang Shu agent or principal, The Ocean Winner restructuring orders, arbitration agreement, set aside warrant, damages*

**End point:** An arrest is applied for ex parte, so the claimant must make full and frank disclosure of all material facts, including facts that are disadvantageous or fatal to its application. Given the low merits threshold, it need not generally disclose defences the owner may raise; but it must disclose matters of such weight as to deliver a "knock-out blow" to the claim, or facts showing the arrest would be an abuse of process (*The Eagle Prestige* [2010] SGHC 93). Examples: a prior arrest or action for the same claim (*The Vasiliy Golovnin*), doubts whether the defendant contracted as agent or principal (*The Xin Chang Shu* [2016] 1 SLR 1096), and restructuring orders in play (*The Ocean Winner*). If the duty is breached the Court may set aside the warrant, and if the failure was deliberate or malicious the claimant may be liable for substantial damages for wrongful arrest.

*Source note: ADM2 s11; ADM1 s12; ADM-LP p4 (Eagle Prestige, Xin Chang Shu); Cel-ADM (lecture 2, quoting The Eagle Prestige at [84]–[85]); Cyril-ADM §3.2.*

### ccp-1-3-15 — Pre-arrest preparation; caveats against arrest
*Keywords: ship watch, tracking, ownership searches, Seaweb, Portnet, registry, list of vessels under arrest, deposit for Sheriff's expenses, caveat against arrest, Form 52, undertaking to provide security, caveat search*

**End point:** Preparation includes ship tracking (when will she arrive and leave), ownership searches (registries, databases), checking the Singapore Courts' list of vessels already under arrest, and arranging funds and the undertaking for the Sheriff's expenses. A shipowner may pre-empt an arrest by filing a caveat against arrest, undertaking to provide security; the Duty Registrar is then unlikely to issue a warrant unless there are good reasons, and the solicitor's undertaking extends to all claims against the vessel unless limited.

*Source note: Cel-ADM (lecture 2: pre-arrest preps); ADM-Guide paras 9–10, 70.*

## 3.V The arrest

- **What does the arresting party file? Who must give an undertaking? Caveat search 15 minutes before** → [3-16](#ccp-1-3-16)
- **How are the arrest papers served? Arrest outside port limits; notify MPA** → [3-17](#ccp-1-3-17)

### ccp-1-3-16 — Arrest papers, solicitor's undertaking, Duty Registrar
*Keywords: originating claim in rem, request for warrant of arrest, affidavit leading to arrest, solicitor's undertaking to Sheriff, Order 33 Rules of Court 2021, Duty Registrar, Zoom, caveat search within 15 minutes, consul notice for wages or possession claims against foreign ship, warrant of arrest, after hours*

**End point:** The claimant issues an admiralty originating claim in rem and files, together, a request for a warrant of arrest, an affidavit leading to arrest (with the particulars the Rules require and full and frank disclosure), and its solicitors' undertaking to the Sheriff for the expenses of the arrest (O 33 ROC 2021, formerly O 70). It attends before the Duty Registrar, after a search of the caveat record made no more than 15 minutes before the hearing. For possession or wages claims against a foreign ship whose state has a consulate in Singapore, notice must first be given to the consul. Arrests can be made after hours in urgent cases.

*Source note: ADM-Guide paras 11–16; ADM2 s11; Cel-ADM (lecture 2: arrest procedure, O 70 now O 33); ADM-LP p1 (O 33).*

### ccp-1-3-17 — Service of the arrest papers; port limits; after the arrest
*Keywords: service of originating claim in rem and warrant of arrest, O 33 r 10, affixed to wheelhouse window, Sheriff, cargo landed, arrest outside port limits, within territorial waters, The Trade Resolve, MPA notification, report of arrest, Sheriff's expenses, security guard*

**End point:** The originating claim in rem and the warrant of arrest are served on the ship, generally by fixing them to the windows in front of the wheelhouse (O 33 r 10); the Sheriff then notifies the MPA and the arrest is reported. An arrest effected outside Singapore's port limits (though within territorial waters) without authority was set aside, with damages, in *The Trade Resolve* [1999] 4 SLR(R) 424. The arresting party must keep the Sheriff in funds for the ship's preservation during the arrest.

*Source note: ADM-Guide paras 24–26, 75; Cel-ADM (lecture 2: The Trade Resolve); Cyril-ADM §5.*

## 3.VI Security and release

### ccp-1-3-18 — Security demand: quantum and justification
*Keywords: security demand, reasonably arguable best case, principal interest costs, interest over 3 years, The Moschanthy, cap value of ship, quantification with explanation, breakdown, The H156, The Evpo Agsa, supporting documents The Arktis Fighter, act with expedition, not oppressive, good faith*

**End point:** After arrest the owner must provide security to obtain release. The claimant may demand security for its reasonably arguable best case — principal, plus interest (usually up to 3 years) and costs (*The Moschanthy*) — but not more than the value of the arrested ship. It must quantify and explain the demand with a breakdown (*The H156*; *The Evpo Agsa*); how much supporting documentation is needed varies with the case (*The Arktis Fighter* [2001] 3 SLR(R) 394). The arresting party must act with expedition, not abuse the arrest by acting oppressively or unreasonably, and act in good faith. A claimant cannot insist on security for its principal only being inadequate, nor demand more than this (ADM2 s20).

*Source note: ADM2 s12, s20 (sample question; no answer given); ADM1 s9; Cyril-ADM §4.1; Cel-ADM (lecture 3).*

### ccp-1-3-19 — Acceptable forms of security
*Keywords: acceptable security, bank guarantee first class Singapore bank, letter of undertaking reputable P&I Club, H&M underwriters, The Arcadia Spirit, The Arktis Fighter, financial information of club, payment into court, bail bond O 33 r 16, The Piya Bhum, foreign club no assets, solicitors' letter of undertaking, wording, final judgment of English court, Fillberth Side P&I Club*

**End point:** Acceptable forms: (1) a bank guarantee from a first-class Singapore bank (the best, always accepted); (2) a letter of undertaking from a reputable P&I Club or hull and machinery underwriter — whether the club is reputable may need its latest financial information and ratings, and if it is in financial difficulty the claimant may ask for a bank guarantee instead; (3) payment into court; (4) a bail bond (the only form in the Rules, now rare; one from a foreign club with no Singapore assets was rejected as unenforceable in *The Piya Bhum*); (5) a solicitors' letter of undertaking, depending on the firm's standing (payment into court is preferred). A P&I Club LOU is not inadequate as such (ADM2 s20). The wording must answer to a judgment of the competent court (e.g. Singapore), not merely a foreign court.

*Source note: ADM1 s10–11; ADM2 s12, s20; Cyril-ADM §4.2; Cel-ADM (lecture 3); ADM1 s15, s21 (mock question on a club in financial difficulty; no answer given). ADM1 s10 cites O 33 r 16 for bail bonds; Cyril-ADM cites the old O 70 r 15.*

### ccp-1-3-20 — Disputes over security; security under protest
*Keywords: disputes over security, quantum most common, wording, sufficiency of guarantor, court moderates quantum The Moschanthy, reasonableness of wording The Benja Bhum, court's jurisdiction to set amount, security under protest, wrongful continued detention, The Evmar, release*

**End point:** Most disputes concern quantum; wording and the guarantor's sufficiency are less common. The Court can moderate an excessive demand (*The Moschanthy*), assess whether the wording is reasonable (*The Benja Bhum*), and set the amount of security. If the claimant unreasonably refuses acceptable security, the owner can provide security under protest and apply for release and damages for wrongful continued detention (*The Evmar*).

*Source note: ADM1 s11; Cyril-ADM §4.3.*

### ccp-1-3-21 — Release from arrest; caveats against release; judicial sale
*Keywords: release from arrest, security posted, settlement, instrument of release, O 33 r 13, undertaking for Sheriff's fees, caveat against release Form 55, 24 hours' written notice, intervener, take over arrest, appraisement and sale, judicial sale, lengthy arrest, GUNNERS Ltd, BLUES Ltd, reserve rights, release as expeditiously as possible, advice should not form part, set aside warrant wrongly invoked, new owner arrested for old owner crew wages*

**End point:** Once security is provided (or the claim is settled), a party applies for an instrument of release (O 33 r 13), undertaking to pay the Sheriff's fees and expenses; it must search for caveats against release and, if one exists, give the caveator at least 24 hours' written notice to withdraw it or arrest in its own action. If no security is posted and there is no settlement, the arrest continues, and the Court may order the ship's appraisement and judicial sale. A new owner whose ship is arrested for the old owner's crew wages (a valid maritime lien claim) should not apply to set aside the arrest for want of jurisdiction; to release the ship quickly it should demand that the old owner provide security (claim, 3 years' interest, costs) while reserving its rights, and if the old owner refuses, put up the security itself under reservation (SQ p44 Q4, key A: option A is the advice NOT to give).

*Source note: ADM2 s13; ADM-Guide paras 40–43, 71–72; SQ p44 Q4 and p116 (key A).*

## 3.VII Challenging the arrest; wrongful arrest

- **Can the owner set aside the arrest? No jurisdiction (wrong relevant person, demise charterer at the wrong time); non-disclosure; claim unsustainable; arrest to enforce a settlement; outside port limits** (ADM2 s21) → [3-22](#ccp-1-3-22)
- **Can the owner get damages for wrongful arrest? Only for bad faith or gross negligence (malice)** (ADM2 s22) → [3-23](#ccp-1-3-23)

### ccp-1-3-22 — Grounds to set aside the arrest or strike out the action
*Keywords: set aside warrant of arrest, lack of admiralty jurisdiction, ss 3 and 4 HCAJA, Bunga Melati 5, not party liable in personam, The Eurohope security for foreign court action, non-disclosure, Eagle Prestige, striking out, legally or factually unsustainable, The STX Mumbai, anticipatory breach, The Dilmun Fulmar enforce settlement, The Trade Resolve outside port limits, after release, Ruben Tankers*

**End point:** The owner may apply to set aside the warrant or strike out the action on grounds of: (1) lack of admiralty jurisdiction — e.g. the claim does not fit s 3(1), or the s 4 requirements are not met, such as the owner not being the party liable in personam (*The Bunga Melati 5*; *The Eurohope*); (2) failure to give full and frank disclosure of material facts (*The Eagle Prestige*); (3) the claim being legally or factually unsustainable, so the action is struck out (*The Bunga Melati 5*; *The STX Mumbai* [2014] SGHC 122); (4) an arrest that in effect enforces a settlement agreement (*The Dilmun Fulmar*); (5) an arrest outside port limits (*The Trade Resolve*). So an owner can set aside if it shows it is not the party liable in personam for the claim (ADM2 s21 option c); release of the ship does not by itself prevent a challenge.

*Source note: ADM2 s14, s21 (sample question; no answer given — option c follows from the s 4(4) requirements); ADM1 s12; ADM-LP p4 (STX Mumbai); Cyril-ADM §5.*

### ccp-1-3-23 — Damages for wrongful arrest and wrongful continuation of arrest
*Keywords: wrongful arrest, damages, mala fides, crassa negligentia, bad faith, gross negligence implying malice, The Kiku Pacific, The Vasiliy Golovnin, not every set-aside leads to damages, knew no factual basis, rejection of acceptable security, wrongful continued detention, The Evmar, The Dilmun Fulmar*

**End point:** Damages for wrongful arrest are not awarded just because an arrest is set aside (even for non-disclosure), nor in every case subject only to proof of loss. The claimant must have acted with bad faith (mala fides) or gross negligence implying malice (crassa negligentia) in bringing the action or arresting (*The Kiku Pacific*; *The Vasiliy Golovnin*) — e.g. arresting when it knew it had no factual basis to say the defendant was liable (ADM2 s22 option d). Damages for wrongful continuation of the arrest may follow from unreasonably rejecting acceptable security (*The Evmar*), and an arrest to enforce a settlement is wrongful (*The Dilmun Fulmar*).

*Source note: ADM1 s12, s19; ADM2 s22 (sample question; no answer given — option d is the one matching the bad-faith test); ADM-LP p1 (damages for wrongful arrest and wrongful continuation); Cyril-ADM §5.*
